Prologue

1,250 words · about 5 min read

A preface to all three volumes.

Mountain Brook, Alabama — 2026

This book is three books, and it is one book, and the reason it is both is a thing the family did for four hundred and thirty years without ever quite having a word for it.

I began, as the first volume will tell you at length, with a single wrong sentence in a genealogy printed in New York in 1925 — a sentence that gave a woman named Barbara the wrong father and sent everyone who came after looking in the wrong county. I set out to correct one sentence. I ended with this. That is the way of the work: you pull a thread to see where it is anchored, and the whole cloth comes up in your hand, and by the time you have followed it back you are holding not a corrected sentence but a line of people running from a Tudor priory in Hertfordshire to a cleared ridge in north Mississippi, and every one of them, in their own country and their own century, doing the same plain thing.

The plain thing is this. Someone in each generation kept the record. Not the great record — not the one the counties and the colleges of arms and the census marshals kept, though those are in here too, and I have read them until my eyes gave out. The small record. The one on the fly-leaf of the family Bible, written in the hand of whoever was keeping the book that year: a birth, a death, a marriage, set down in the place kept for it and not embellished. The book changed hands and it changed countries and eventually it changed languages, because the first person to keep it wrote his entries in a formal French that no one in the line could read within three generations. But it did not stop. Someone always held it. And when the Bible itself was finally gone — lost, or divided among heirs, or simply worn past use — the keeping went on in the things keeping becomes when no one is trained to it anymore: a box of photographs, a story told once at a kitchen table, a chart somebody started and did not finish. I am the one holding it now. What holding it has come to mean, for me, is these three books.

The three books are three countries and three centuries, and I have let each one find its own shape, because the people in each one lived a different life and the record keeps them a different way. Volume One is England, from about 1490 to 1714 — the priory house at Little Wymondley, the surveyor who rose out of a carpenter's trade into the king's service and back into the gentry, the heralds and the visitations and the long slow coming-apart of a Hertfordshire seat, and at the end of it a woman named Barbara standing at the rail of a brigantine as Charleston comes up out of the February haze, going to marry a man she has agreed to across an ocean. Volume Two is Virginia and Carolina, from 1742 to 1794 — the line come across the water and pushing south into the tobacco country, a colonel's daughter married and buried young, a man who rides out on his place's business in the autumn of 1775 and does not come back, and his fifteen-year-old granddaughter at a writing-desk on a winter night, finding at the foot of a letter that the family's long going-on had wanted a name all along. Volume Three is Mississippi, from 1832 to 1920 — a girl at a loom on a ridge three miles north of the post road, who will watch slavery arrive on her father's place and then be swept off it, who will marry late and out of a dead father's house, who will take a farm and hold it through a war and a widowhood and sixty more years of a world that would not stop moving under her, and who will die at last under a homemade coffin her sons made with their own hands.

Three women anchor the three books, a century apart, and each of them, without knowing the others' words, said the same thing in three words or fewer. Barbara, coming down the gangway at Charleston to a man who has waited on a bench for thirty-five days, and who years later writes under her name in the Bible, in a hand still swollen with feeling: She came. Her great-granddaughter Mary Baker, at the desk on that November night in 1779, closing a letter to a brother at the war with two words she then wrote a second time in the book that stayed in the house: we continue. And Mary Baker's line, carried down through the daughters — Johnson to Hubbard and on through the marriages, the name going sideways into other names the way it always did — reaching at last a woman named Mary Jane, on a ridge in Mississippi, of whom the truest thing anyone ever said is the thing this last book is named for: she held the line. One keeping. Three refrains. The same act.

I have tried, across all three volumes, to hold to one rule, which is the rule the family itself seems to have held: set the known thing down, in the place kept for it, and do not invent to fill the silence. Every date in these pages that could be checked against a calendar has been checked. Where two records disagree — and they disagree about the day a woman died, and the year a man died, and a good deal in between — I have put both readings on the page and let them stand rather than choose the tidier one. Where the record simply stops, as it stops on the question of where one man died and when, I have said so and left the silence open, because closing it would have meant writing fiction and calling it history. And where the record is not empty but was made empty — where a probate book that should name three enslaved people on a Mississippi farm names no one, because the estate that would have listed them was never opened — I have said that too, as plainly as I know how, because a silence that was made is a different thing from a silence that merely fell, and the people inside it are owed the difference.

So this is my entry on the fly-leaf. It is longer than any of theirs, and it is in English, and it has footnotes they would have found strange. But it is the same act they performed, one keeper at a time, for four hundred and thirty years: setting the names down so that whoever breaks the book open a long way off and a long time from now will know whose hands carried the words this far.

Each volume opens in its own place and its own year, and you may read them straight through or one at a time as the mood takes you. But they are one line, and one keeping, and I have put them under one cover at last so that the whole of it can be held the way it was always meant to be held — together, in one pair of hands.

Here they are. Here we are.

I am coming.
