Volume I

Prologue

2,038 words · about 9 min read

There is a sentence in a book published in New York in 1925 that I have been arguing with for almost two and a half years.

The book is called History of the de Graffenried Family from 1191 A.D. to 1925, written by Thomas P. de Graffenried, a New York lawyer who spent decades collecting the genealogy of his family. On page 149, near the bottom of the page, in the dignified language of a family history that was sent to subscribers in a printed binding with marbled endpapers, he wrote that on the twenty-second of February 1714, at Charleston, South Carolina, Christopher VI de Graffenried married a young Englishwoman — Barbara Tempest (née Needham), daughter of the distinguished Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire, England. She was born in 1688, her mother’s maiden name being Wingate.

That sentence is the only documentary trace of Barbara’s parentage that the de Graffenried family preserved into the twentieth century. It is the sentence from which everyone who has tried to find her has worked. And it is wrong about the central fact.

There was no Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire. There never was. Thomas P. de Graffenried, writing in his Manhattan study in the early 1920s, was working from imperfect family papers and from a 1735 letter by Baron Christoph V de Graffenried to his grandson Tscharner — a letter in which the Baron describes Barbara only as Madame Needham an English gentlewoman and then concludes, with disarming honesty, You may put here the Genealogie of your spouse yourself. The grandson never did. Two centuries later, his great-great-grandson in New York filled in the blank with the wrong name.

It took me a long time to figure that out. I started this work in the late winter of 2024, looking up an old family story about a great-aunt’s research into our line, and discovered within the first month that no one in the family had ever quite known who Barbara was. We knew she had crossed the Atlantic. We knew she had married a Swiss baron’s son in Charleston. We knew our line descended from her. We did not know who her father had been, or where she had grown up, or what her life had looked like before the wedding. The 1925 book was the closest thing we had to an answer, and the 1925 book was wrong about the central fact.

So I started looking.

There is no particularly dramatic story about how I found her. I subscribed to genealogy databases. I read the old wills at the National Archives at Kew in transcribed form. I learned to read Tudor secretary hand well enough to puzzle through a 1572 visitation pedigree, and then well enough to read it cleanly, and then well enough to recognize the handwriting of the College of Arms clerks across half a century of records. I built a workbook in Excel that grew to thirty sheets. I built a master report that grew to fifty thousand words. I corresponded with researchers in Hertfordshire, in London, in Derbyshire, in North Carolina. By the end of the first year I knew her grandfather. By the end of the second year I knew her great-great-grandfather. By the spring of 2026 I knew the name signed at the bottom of a 1572 visitation pedigree — not Barbara’s, but her great-great-great-grandfather’s, a man named John Nedham, who had ridden in from Wymondley to register his arms with the Clarenceux King of Arms in the seventeenth year of Elizabeth’s reign.

Her father turned out to be Maurice — the fourth son of a Hertfordshire priory house, a man who held a £500 portion under his father's will and never inherited the priory itself, who was living down in London in the lawyers' parish at St Bride's when he married, and who died in November 1712, fourteen months before his daughter wrote a three-word letter that crossed the Atlantic and changed our family's geography forever.

So the 1925 source was wrong about the father. Sir Arthur never existed. Maurice did.

And I spent two and a half years being wrong about the mother.

The 1925 sentence gave her a maiden name — Wingate — and there was a Chancery suit at Kew that seemed to prove it. Filed in 1684. Maurice Nedham, Esq., and his wife Lettice, joint plaintiffs, suing Sir Francis Wingate of Harlington, knight, for a parcel of London-suburban land that was part of Lettice's own marriage settlement and that Sir Francis was holding back. A woman named Lettice, suing a Wingate, for her marriage portion. What would you conclude? I concluded what anybody would conclude, and I wrote it down, and I built a chapter on it, and I would have gone to press on it.

Then, in May of this year, I was looking at a photograph of two facing pages of a seventeenth-century London marriage register — St. James Duke's Place, in Aldgate, the parish that claimed exemption from the Bishop of London and sold something like forty thousand marriages by licence between 1661 and 1691 to people who had no parish of their own — and about two-thirds of the way down the right-hand page, between a marriage of the fifth of June and a marriage of the second of September, in a clerk's flat working hand, I read this:

Mauris Nedham of S[ain]t Brides London & Letisia Bowyer of Wymondley in Hertford-sheire weare married y[e] 21[s]t of June 1684 by Licence Her name was not Wingate. Her name was Bowyer.

The Chancery suit had never said otherwise. It said that Maurice had a wife called Lettice, and that a Wingate was holding land out of her settlement, and both of those things are still true, and neither of them makes her a Wingate. I had a blank where a maiden name should be, and I had a plausible name standing near the blank, and I put it in.

Which is exactly what Thomas P. de Graffenried did in his Manhattan study in the 1920s. He had a blank too. The Baron had left it for him — You may put here the Genealogie of your spouse yourself, he wrote to his grandson in 1735, and the grandson never did, and the blank came down through two hundred years to a lawyer in New York who filled it with a knight who had never lived.

The blank does not stay blank. That is the whole lesson of this book, and I did not learn it by reading about it. Somebody always fills it — an antiquarian, a herald, a grandson, a family story, me — and the only thing that empties it again is the primary source, and the primary source is almost never where the argument is. It is two lines in a parish clerk's hand in a register nobody thought to open.

So here is what I can tell you about Barbara's mother, which is all I can tell you.

Her name was Letisia Bowyer. In the summer of 1684 she was living at Wymondley in Hertfordshire, and she had not been born there. No Bowyer ever held a manor in either Wymondley parish. No Bowyer child was baptized at either Wymondley church. In the whole county of Hertfordshire, across sixty-five years and something like fifty thousand baptisms, the parish registers record exactly one Bowyer, and he was a boy named Beniamine at Little Hadham, twenty-five miles off, and he is nothing to us. She came into that county from somewhere and nobody wrote down where. She married the priory's fourth son in a City church that neither of them belonged to. She bore a daughter the following August. She was widowed at fifty-six in the cold week before Advent of 1712, and where she went afterward is not recorded either.

She comes into the record from nowhere and she goes out of it the same way. She is the first unwritten line in this family's book — a woman with a name, a wedding, a child, and no people.

And her daughter got on a ship.

Eight generations. From John in 1572 to Barbara in 1714 is one hundred and forty-two years, four wives, six wills, two heralds’ visitations, one civil war, one regicide, one Restoration, one Great Plague, one Great Fire, and one dissolved Augustinian priory in north Hertfordshire that the family had been given by Henry VIII in 1538 and held for almost two hundred years.

This book is the story of that house and the people who lived in it. It begins in the 1490s with a Derbyshire younger son named James Nedham, who walked south to seek his fortune at the Tudor Court and ended up serving Henry VIII as Surveyor of the King’s Works, building the king’s palaces from Hampton Court to Whitehall to Windsor. It ends in February 1714 on a wooden dock in Charleston, South Carolina, with a twenty-eight-year-old woman stepping off a ship she had boarded against the advice of every person she loved, including the man waiting for her on the dock.

· · ·

My younger daughter, the romantic one, reads fantasy novels by the stack. She likes heroines with sharp tongues and stubborn hearts and the willingness to defy a kingdom in service of a private commitment. My older daughter likes them too. I do not read those books myself, but I have watched my girls put them down with the kind of expression a reader gets when she has been somewhere real.

That is what I want this book to do. I want it to be the kind of book my daughters can read on a Saturday afternoon and close at five and want to talk about over dinner. I want them to feel Barbara. I want them to feel her decision. I want them to understand that she is the reason they exist and that what she did in the autumn of 1713 took a kind of courage that the family has never quite named because the family didn’t, until now, even know her name.

So the book is for them. And for the daughters they will have, and for the daughters those daughters will have, and for as many generations of Searcy women as want to know who they came from.

It is also, of course, for everyone in the family who is not a daughter. But the dedication page is to my girls.

· · ·

One last thing before we begin.

The book moves between two voices. Most of it is in what I will call the historian’s voice — the voice in which we walk through the priory in 1538 and watch James Nedham convert a former monastery into a country house; the voice in which we sit through a herald’s visitation in 1572 and watch a man sign his name to a vellum book; the voice in which we follow Eustace through the Civil War and George Senior through the Restoration. That voice is mine. It is careful, it is sourced, it is grounded in what the documents say, and it stays a respectful distance from the people it describes.

But there is a second voice, and it belongs to Barbara. It is closer than the historian’s voice. It is inside her head. It feels her body in the cold of an October night and the heat of a Carolina February. It hears her think. It is the voice the book reaches for when Barbara is on the page — first quietly, as a child in the last sections of Chapter 4, and then fully, beginning in Chapter 5, when the love story starts.

I have permitted myself this second voice because the book cannot do its work without it. A family history that keeps its distance from its protagonist is a chronicle. A family history that goes inside her is a book.

Barbara has been waiting three centuries for someone to go inside her. I am going to.
