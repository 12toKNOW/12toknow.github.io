Bridge — Charleston, February 1714

Charleston, February 1714

The Volume I → Volume II hinge: Barbara Tempest Needham comes off the Hopewell into Charleston harbor, keeps eleven days at Mrs. Brewton's on Tradd Street, and marries Christopher VI de Graffenried at the Cooper Street church on Monday 22 February 1714 — after which Christopher makes the first entry on the fly-leaf of the family Bible in formal French, and writes two words in English beneath it.

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Documented first, section by section; every AR passage flagged; Gaps & Open Items at the end. Tier key follows the volume's spine: P primary / documented on a primary instrument · S secondary or contextual · R reference/aggregator · T tradition/family record · AR authorial reconstruction. Flags only; no corrections made. The documented layer is the volume's own embedded Working Bibliography (section-keyed) and Note on Sources; the Bridge draws its heroine, its "I am coming," and its "She came" from Volume I's already-established close, so this memo also carries the Volume I apparatus forward where it bears.

The Bridge is the thinnest-documented material in the volume and the most continuous with Volume I. Its single load-bearing documentary anchor is the marriage itself — Christopher VI de Graffenried to Barbara Tempest Needham, Charleston, 22 February 1714 — and the object that records it, the Christopher VI de Graffenried family Bible, whose fly-leaf carries the formal French marriage entry. Everything around that anchor — the crossing's arrival scene, the eleven days, the supporting cast, the wedding hour by hour, and the two-word English entry — is authorial reconstruction, most of it built on Volume I's own reconstruction rather than on new record.

§The arrival — the Hopewell comes up the Charleston roads

Documented / disciplined.

  • Christopher VI de Graffenried is a documented historical person — son of Baron Christopher V de Graffenried of Worb, of the family that founded New Bern — P/S (the 1925 History of the de Graffenried Family, treated as a transcribing-and-tradition source, S; the family Bible, P). The New Bern half-built farm, the Carolina Land Office obligation, the Yamasee unrest at the south of the colony and the Tuscarora settlement at the north are period context — S.

  • The Reverend Gideon Johnston — commissary at Charleston for the Bishop of London — is a documented historical figure of the period, and the officiant named on the marriage record — P/S.

  • The family Bible carried from Worb is the documented object the whole volume is structurally about — given to Christopher VI by his father at Worb in 1710, fly-leaf clean, first entry made at the 1714 Charleston marriage — P (Working Bibliography §I).

AR — the arrival scene.

  • The entire arrival sequence is authorial reconstruction. The brigantine's half-rigged foremast and the storm of 3 January, the south-southeast wind, Mr. Eldridge at the wheel, the 9:20 a.m. landing at the Cooper Street pier, Barbara at the rail in her wool cloak, Christopher on the bench "every morning since the eighth of January," the hand raised palm-forward at a quarter-mile, "here, here, I am here" — none of this is documented. It is reconstruction on the documented fact that the two married at Charleston in February 1714. The precise arrival date (Thursday 11 February) and the thirty-five-day count on the bench are authorial.

  • The supporting cast is AR. Mr. Eldridge (the Hopewell's master), Mrs. Brewton (the Tradd Street widow-landlady), Susannah Fenwick and Mrs. Bellinger, the widow Mrs. Houghton, Mr. Pelletier, the merchant Allston, Hans the Swiss manservant, the runner Wilkes — all are reconstructed figures giving the scene its population. They carry no documentary citation and should be understood as invented texture on the documented marriage.

§The eleven days — Mrs. Brewton's on Tradd Street

Documented / disciplined.

  • The "documented things" discipline is stated inside the prose. Barbara's letters home say "no more than the documented facts required" — the Hopewell had landed, she was well, she was at Charleston, she would be married on 22 February. This is the volume's own restraint rule enacted in scene: it is AR that she wrote the letters, but the content named is confined to what the record would bear.

  • Christopher's Williamsburg lot. The prose has Christopher speak of "Lot 47, the Pitt-Dixon corner" at Williamsburg. The de Graffenried Williamsburg lot is plausibly a documentable land record but is not cited in the volume's bibliography; treat the lot number and corner as reconstruction/texture pending a Williamsburg land-record check (see Gaps).

AR — the eleven days.

  • The whole eleven-day interval is reconstruction. The "floor that holds" motif (the bed that does not move under her, learning land after 106 days at sea), the daily rhythm (Christopher at seven, the harbor master's shed at ten, the parlor at four with the door open for propriety), the fourth-afternoon "I will not be ornamental" exchange, the chapel-garden-at-Wymondley callback — all AR, and much of it draws directly on Volume I's reconstruction of Barbara and the Wymondley courtship. The 106-days-at-sea and the "one hundred and six days" count are authorial.

§The wedding — Monday 22 February 1714, the Cooper Street church

Documented.

  • The marriage is the Bridge's load-bearing fact: Barbara Tempest née Needham married Christopher de Graffenried at the commissary's (Cooper Street) church in Charleston, South Carolina, on 22 February 1714, the Reverend Gideon Johnston officiating — P (the family Bible fly-leaf entry, which the prose reproduces; the church register entry named in the prose as made "on the same day"). The formal French entry is transcribed in the 1925 History — S corroboration.

  • The formal fly-leaf entry itself is documented text — the volume reproduces it: "Christophe de Graffenried, fils du Baron Christophe V de Bernberg, époux. Barbara Tempest née Needham, de Wymondley en Hertfordshire, épouse. Mariés en l'église du commissaire à Charleston…le vingt-deuxième jour de février, 1714." This is the first entry on the Bible fly-leaf — P.

AR — the wedding as scene.

  • The wedding-day hour-by-hour is reconstruction. The 5 a.m. waking and counting the days backward, the altered dark-wool gown, the pearl earrings, the half-past-eight parlor, the witnesses walking to church, the thin cold rain, Eldridge's great-coat over her shoulders, the three-o'clock wedding-supper (Carolina ham, rice with "the new orange spice," custard, Allston's Madeira), the company leaving at six, Barbara asleep in the chair by nine — all AR on the documented fact of the wedding. The named witnesses (Mrs. Houghton and Mr. Pelletier for the bride; Hans and Allston for the groom; Eldridge in the second pew) are reconstruction.

  • The physical description of Johnston ("a slight man of thirty-eight…six years and ten months at Charleston…in poor health that winter") is AR/S texture on the documented officiant.

§The fly-leaf — the formal French entry and the two English words

Documented.

  • The Bible, the clean fly-leaf, the formal French entry, and the private English entry below it are the documented structure of the object — the volume's Working Bibliography §I states plainly that the first entry on the page is "Christopher VI's own hand at his marriage in Charleston, 22 February 1714 — the formal French entry the Baron had instructed him to write, with a private English entry below it." So the existence of a two-part fly-leaf entry (formal French above, private English below) is documented — P.

AR — and the trilogy-wide apparatus rule.

  • The writing scene is reconstruction. The cut quill, "Mrs. Brewton's ink…a slightly browner ink," the ruler he decides not to use, the memory of his father writing his mother's 1697 death entry at Worb, the meditation that "he had never…written anything in English that he meant" — all AR on the documented two-part entry.

  • ⚠ "She came" is authorial reconstruction — carried verbatim from the Volume I Chapter 6 memo. The Bridge's closing two-word English entry, "She came," is the same inscription the embedded Volume I Chapter 6 sources memo explicitly flags as invented — verbatim there: "She came, rendered as Christopher's smaller-hand entry on the Bible fly-leaf underneath the formal French inscription, is AR — it is invented," named "the chapter's single most authorially-asserted moment." The documented fact is that a private English entry exists beneath the French; that its words are "She came" is authorial reconstruction. Likewise "I am coming" — the three-word note from New Bern, 17 November 1713 — is the reconstructed close of Volume I, not a surviving document. Neither "She came" nor "I am coming" may ever be cited in any volume's apparatus as documented primary text. This is the single most important consistency rule for the whole trilogy's apparatus, and it originates here and in the Vol I Ch 6 memo.

Gaps and open items

1. ⚠ "She came" / "I am coming" are AR, trilogy-wide. The two-part fly-leaf entry (French above, private English below) is documented; the words of the English line are reconstruction, as Vol I's own Ch 6 memo states. Never cite either phrase as primary text. (Highest-priority apparatus rule; shared with Vol I and the Vol III frame.)

2. The church-register entry — verify it exists as a separate instrument. The prose asserts the marriage "was entered in the church register on the same day." The fly-leaf entry is the certain instrument; a surviving Charleston commissary's-church register entry would be independent corroboration. Confirm whether the register is in hand or is inference from the fly-leaf. (St. Philip's / commissary's-church records, Charleston.)

3. Christopher VI's Williamsburg Lot 47 ("Pitt-Dixon corner"). Named in dialogue as an anchor for the couple's planned move; not in the volume's bibliography. A Williamsburg land/lot record would document it or mark it as texture.

4. Christopher VI's death date (ODQ-V2-06). Not a Bridge fact but structurally attached to this Bible: the volume commits to an October 1742 death (for the Bible's accession to Tscharner) against the 1925 History's soft "ca. 1750." Open; affects Ch 1 §1.5 and Ch 6.

5. The Vol I continuity dates (17 Aug 1710 Baron gives the Bible at Worb; 17 Oct 1710 chapel garden at Wymondley; 17 Nov 1713 the four letters and the note at New Bern) are carried from Volume I's reconstruction, not independently documented here.

End of Bridge Sources Memo v1. Load-bearing result: the Bridge rests on one documented anchor — the 22 February 1714 Charleston marriage of Christopher VI de Graffenried and Barbara Tempest Needham, recorded in the formal French entry on the family Bible fly-leaf — and reconstructs everything around it (the arrival, the eleven days, the wedding as scene, the writing of the fly-leaf). The two carried flags: the whole scene is AR on a thin documentary base largely inherited from Volume I, and — most importantly — "She came" and "I am coming" are authorial reconstruction, never to be cited as documented text in any volume.

Chapter 1 — Lunenburg, Summer 1742

Lunenburg, Summer 1742

The elder generation and the first turning of the Bible: Anton Tscharner DeGraffenreid marries Mary Baker of Chowan (5 July 1742); his father Christopher VI dies at Williamsburg that October; the family Bible and the keeping pass to Tscharner, who enters the late marriage and his father's death on the fly-leaf; the name "Baker" comes into the line, carried forward in the son born 1744.

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Documented first, section by section; every AR passage flagged; Gaps & Open Items at the end. Tier key: P primary · S secondary/contextual · R reference/aggregator · T tradition · AR authorial reconstruction. Flags only; no corrections made. Documentary layer = the volume's embedded Working Bibliography (§§I–V, XIV) and the Searcy Ancestral Workbook v7.11.

Register note the audit must record up front: Chapter 1 is written at deliberate historian's distance. Unlike the scene-built chapters, it repeatedly refuses to reconstruct — "the historian does not stage him," "invents nothing to fill it," "the day is renderable to the country; it is not renderable to the room." So the chapter carries very little scene-level AR. Its reconstruction is almost entirely interpretive framing (the "hinge nobody could see," the meaning drawn from the documented shape), and it is honest about its own gaps in its own text. The chapter's real exposure is not invented scene — it is one load-bearing date the prose states as fact while the apparatus flags it as an unproven commitment. That is item 1 below.

§1.1 The country / §1.2 The Bakers and the match — the documented spine

Documented.

  • Anton Tscharner DeGraffenreid, b. 28 November 1722 at Williamsburg, baptized twelve days later by Commissary Blair, the Honorable Nathaniel Harrison a godfather — P/S (family record; parish/Williamsburg record as named). Only child of Christopher VI and Barbara Tempest Needham.

  • Christopher VI bought the house in 1717 on the south side of Duke of Gloucester Street, Williamsburg — S (documentable Williamsburg property record; named in the prose, treat as S pending citation).

  • The Baker descent and the colonel's standing are carried on primary and reference records — P/R/S: Lawrence Baker of Surry, will proved 1681; Henry Baker's Surry patents 1683–84 and marriage to Mary Blake (daughter of Col. John Blake of Nansemond); Col. Henry Baker II of Chowan — Bucklands (~2,500 acres), militia colonel, sheriff, captain, burgess, JP of Chowan commissioned 1731, the Henry Baker Ferry (in use before 1722). Will of Col. Henry Baker II proved 1739 — names daughter Mary — P (Working Bibliography §II). Will of Edmund Chancey, 1753 — names grandchild "Mary DeGrafinred" — P (§II), fixing Mary's mother as Ruth Chancey, daughter of Edmund Chancey of Pasquotank. Together these two wills "close the elder Mary Baker's parentage on both sides."

  • The marriage: Tscharner DeGraffenreid × Mary Baker, 5 July 1742, in Chowan, North Carolina — P (the fly-leaf entry the volume reproduces; also treated as the anchor date in the Workbook). The date-to-the-day and country-to-the-ground are documented; the room and the officiant are not (the prose says so plainly and builds neither).

  • The name "Baker" enters the line in 1742 and is carried forward in the first child, Baker DeGraffenreid, b. 6 August 1744 — P (family/Bible record).

AR / disciplined framing.

  • The tobacco-frontier country picture (the James drainage, Nottoway/Meherrin/Roanoke, Brunswick County in 1742 → Lunenburg cut from it in 1746) is period context (S), correctly handled — the chapter openly notes the seat "did not exist in the summer of 1742" and that later chapters' lane/oak/table were "neither built nor in a county that had its later name."

  • Elder Mary Baker "married at about nineteen" is inference on the family dates, correctly hedged.

  • The chapter's refusal to invent the courtship ("how the match was made the record does not say…the historian does not invent the courtday") is discipline enacted, not a flag.

  • The "no was hers to say" passage (whether the marriage was the bride's choosing) is interpretive framing that names conditions and invents no feeling — disciplined AR, correctly bounded.

§1.3 The Bible at Williamsburg; the hand-convention — the documented object

Documented.

  • The Bible object (octavo ~8×5, dark calf, given clean at Worb 17 August 1710, first entry 1714) and the fly-leaf convention (formal French, keeper's own hand, reserved page) are documented structure — P (Working Bibliography §I). The Bernberg Bible (the Baron's, at Worb; 1697 death entry of Christopher VI's mother) is the convention's origin — S (§I), and the chapter correctly keeps the form (which crossed) distinct from the object (which stayed at Worb).

  • The first fly-leaf entry — the reproduced French marriage entry of 1714 — is documented text on the Bible — P.

AR — carried flag.

  • ⚠ "She came" beneath the French is authorial reconstruction (Vol I Ch 6 memo; Bridge memo). §1.3 reproduces it — "the smaller English words She came beneath it" — and glosses it as "the family's record" of Barbara's crossing. The two-part entry is documented; the words "She came" are invented. Never cite as primary. (Trilogy-wide rule.)

§1.4 The wedding at distance / §1.5 The October accession — the load-bearing inference

Documented.

  • The wedding date and country (5 July 1742, Chowan) — P, rendered "to the country; not to the room," with the officiant and building left unnamed as the record leaves them.

  • The colonel was three years dead at the wedding (will proved 1739; Bucklands to the third Henry Baker) — P — so the chapter correctly does not stage him.

  • Baker DeGraffenreid b. 6 August 1744 — P (repeated anchor).

  • Tscharner's fly-leaf marriage entry (reproduced French: "Tscharner de Graffenried…Marie Baker, fille de feu le colonel Henry Baker…Mariés en Chowan…le cinquième jour de juillet, 1742") — P if a genuine transcribed fly-leaf entry (the 1925 History "transcribes the relevant fly-leaf entries"); see item 2 — verify it is transcribed and not reconstructed in the convention's style.

AR / the chapter's central exposure.

  • *⚠ 1. Christopher VI's October 1742 death is a volume commitment, not a documented fact — and §1.5 states it as fact. The prose opens §1.5: "Christopher VI DeGraffenreid died at Williamsburg in October 1742, on a day the surviving record does not name." But the volume's own Working Bibliography (§II and ODQ-V2-06) states the opposite of certainty: the 1925 History "places him settled permanently in Prince George County and gives a soft 'ca. 1750'; the volume commits to an October 1742 death for the Bible's accession. No hard probate date is in hand." The entire §1.5 "October accession" structure — the father dead the same autumn as the July wedding, the two entries (marriage-entered-late and father's-death) falling to the son's hand in one season — rests on this un-documented date. If Christopher VI in fact died ca. 1750 (the printed source's reading), Tscharner did not become keeper in 1742 and the chapter's climactic accession is displaced by up to eight years. This is the single largest load-bearing inference in the chapter, and the prose's flat "died…in October 1742" reads with a certainty the apparatus does not have. → The author's call: either soften the prose to name the commitment (as the bibliography does), or run the Prince George County / ECU (MC 0796) search that ODQ-V2-06 targets and settle it.*

  • ⚠ 2. The accession scene — Tscharner taking up the pen, cutting it, entering first the marriage then his father's death — is reconstruction on the commitment above. The chapter is careful ("How the book came from the father's desk to the son's keeping the record does not say…the historian does not invent the journey"), but the sequence and same-hand-same-autumn drama are authorial, and depend on the 1742 death. The death entry's wording is explicitly withheld ("The historian does not give the words of it") — good discipline. But whether Tscharner's 1742 marriage entry exists on the fly-leaf as reproduced, or is an authorial reconstruction in the convention's style, must be verified against the 1925 History's transcription or a Bible image. (Given "She came" is invented, the audit cannot assume every reproduced French entry is transcribed.)

  • The "rhyme" between the father's church-and-fly-leaf marriage and the son's roomless one is interpretive framing (AR), correctly not pressed into a claim.

Gaps and open items

1. ⚠ Christopher VI's death date (ODQ-V2-06) — load-bearing and stated as fact. Prose: "died…in October 1742." Apparatus: a commitment against the 1925 History's "ca. 1750," no hard probate. The whole §1.5 accession hangs on it. Retrieval: Prince George County VA will/probate or land record; ECU DeGraffenreid Association Collection (MC 0796). Highest-priority Ch 1 decision.

2. Verify Tscharner's 1742 fly-leaf marriage entry is transcribed, not reconstructed. Check the 1925 History transcription or a Bible fly-leaf image. Applies to the reproduced French entry in §1.5 (and, by extension, the audit's confidence in every reproduced fly-leaf entry).

3. ⚠ "She came" is AR (§1.3). Carried trilogy-wide flag; never cite as primary text.

4. The elder Mary Baker's death (ca. 1756). Not a Ch 1 event, but the bibliography flags that early drafting's "7 June 1773" was "a false precision on a wrong year and was corrected"; a primary death record is still wanted (§VIII). Confirm no residue of the wrong date survives in Ch 1's handling of the namesake-grandmother.

5. Officiant/building of the 5 July 1742 wedding — correctly left open. Named here only to confirm the prose invents neither; no action unless a Chowan parish record surfaces.

6. Williamsburg property (1717 purchase) and the baptism particulars (Blair; Harrison godfather) — name the citations at manuscript-assembly stage; currently stated as fact, plausibly documentable.

End of Chapter 1 Sources Memo v1. Load-bearing result: the documented spine is solid — two Chowan wills (1739, 1753) close the elder Mary Baker's parentage, the 5 July 1742 marriage is fixed to day and country, the Bible and its convention are documented, and the name "Baker" enters the line with the 1744 son. The chapter's discipline of refusing to stage what the record does not hold is exemplary. Its one real exposure is a date, not a scene: the prose states Christopher VI's October 1742 death as fact, while the volume's own apparatus flags it as an unproven commitment against a printed "ca. 1750" — and the chapter's entire closing accession depends on it.

Chapter 2 — The Child at Lunenburg

The Child at Lunenburg

The heroine's childhood: Mary Baker DeGraffenreid ("Polly"), born 3 September 1764 at Tscharner's south-side seat, from the floor up to her eleventh year — the household of three generations (the grandfather Tscharner and his Bible; the father Baker; the mother Sarah Vass; the brother Vincent), the death of an infant sibling, and the father's ride over the Carolina line in the autumn of 1775 that the record shows was the last time he was seen alive.

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Documented first; AR flagged; Gaps & Open Items at the end. Tier key: P primary · S secondary · R reference · T tradition · AR authorial reconstruction. Flags only; no corrections made. Documentary layer = the volume's embedded Working Bibliography (§§II–IV, VIII, XIV) and the Workbook v7.11.

Register note: Chapter 2 is the volume's first fully scene-built, close-third chapter, told from the child's sensory position ("the world came to her as the undersides of things"). It is therefore the most heavily reconstructed chapter so far — but it frames itself honestly with a Historian's Opening and Historian's Closing that carry the documented anchors and name the four-year silence without building across it. The body invents interiority on a documented household — which is the chapter's proper method — with two exceptions that invent events, not just feeling (the infant sibling's death, and the graveside naming scene). Those are the flags.

Historian's Opening & Closing — the documented frame

Documented.

  • Mary Baker DeGraffenreid ("Polly"), b. 3 September 1764 — P (Reynolds canonical record; Workbook). Named for her father's mother, the elder Mary Baker.

  • The household of three generations at the seat: the keeper-grandfather Tscharner (b. Nov 1722, ~41 in 1764); the father Baker DeGraffenreid (b. 6 Aug 1744), m. Sarah Vass; the brother Vincent ("not yet two" in autumn 1764 → b. ~1762–63); and Polly — all P/R (family record; Workbook).

  • Tscharner's marital history: first wife the elder Mary Baker "some eight years dead" by 1764 (→ d. ca. 1756); married twice more; third wife Eliza Allen Embry, m. the year before (1763) — P/R ("the record carries the marriages and the dates and little more").

  • The seat (built early 1750s on the ground taken up 1742), Lunenburg cut 1746, tobacco country — S/inference (as in Ch 1).

  • ⚠ Load-bearing (well handled): Baker DeGraffenreid's last-alive date. He "put his hand to a deed of trust in Guilford County, North Carolina, on the 20th of October 1775 — the last to set him among the living." Then silence until Sarah Vass acting as administratrix by 10 September 1779 (Lunenburg Order Book 14, p. 38). "Between…lies a silence of nearly four years the surviving sources do not fill" (ODQ-V2-01). Family tradition (Reynolds) holds he was "killed at the outset of the Revolutionary War"; the chapter "carries the tradition and has not found the proof, and names the gap rather than building across it." — P (deed, D; order book, D) + T (tradition, correctly labeled). This is model discipline: the volume's central kept silence is stated as a silence.

§2.1–§2.2 The floor and the house — reconstruction on a documented household

AR (interiority on documented frame).

  • The entire child's-eye world is reconstruction: the far ceiling and moving light, the fire, "the hands" (the mother's), being carried between warm and cold, the long table she sits under and then stands at, the oak at the head of the lane, the well, learning the order of the household day. None of the sensory content is documented; all of it is authorial close-third on the documented fact of a child growing up in that household. This is the chapter's proper and declared method.

  • The bowl with "Polly" cut on the underside (§2.2) — a lovely, entirely invented object; the name Polly is documented (diminutive of Mary), the bowl and its cut marks are AR.

  • Vincent as the elder brother "ahead of her in everything" — documented sibling (P); the characterization is AR.

  • Consistency check — Vincent's birth ~1762–63. "Not yet two" in autumn 1764. This must square with Chapter 5, where Vincent makes a substitute enlistment at Lunenburg in January 1777 "at age fourteen" (Working Bibliography §V; ODQ-V2-03). b. 1762–63 → age 14 in Jan 1777 is consistent. Confirm the birth year is fixed the same way in both chapters. (Minor; flagged for internal consistency.)

§2.3 The grandfather's room and the Bible — the graveside naming

Documented.

  • The Bible on the desk in the grandfather's front room, in Tscharner's keeping "since his father's death in 1742" — P (the object; Working Bibliography §I).

  • The elder Mary Baker's grave in the family burying-ground — P ("Family burying-ground, Lunenburg (D, by use)"; §VIII), the ground "holding the elder Mary Baker's stone, where the child meets the Bible in Chapter 2." So the ground and a grandmother buried in it are documented.

AR — invents an event, flag.

  • ⚠ The graveside scene is reconstruction of an event, not just interiority. The grandfather taking Polly out to the rail-enclosed ground, kneeling in the wet grass, letting her hold the Bible against her, telling her the stone is her grandmother's and that the name cut in it is her own — this whole scene is authorial. What is documented: the ground, the grandmother buried there, the child named for her. What is invented: that this specific instruction-at-the-stone happened, and — materially — that a legible cut headstone bearing the elder Mary Baker's name stood in the ground. The bibliography flags the elder Mary Baker's death as "ca. 1756 (P; primary record pending)" and notes an earlier "7 June 1773" was "a false precision on a wrong year and was corrected." A dated, legibly-inscribed stone is an assumption on a grave whose very date is a soft estimate. → Author's call: is the stone (and its being read to the child) supportable, or should the scene rest only on "the ground" and a grandmother in it, without asserting a cut inscription?

§2.4 The littlest — an infant sibling's death (the chapter's largest event-level AR)

AR — invents an event; the audit's principal Ch 2 flag.

  • ⚠ "The littlest" — a younger sibling who is born, sickens, dies, is laid out, and is buried in the family ground — is a fully reconstructed event, and the audit cannot confirm the child existed. The chapter builds an entire sequence: the infant in the cradle Polly once lay in; the illness (the closed back room, the kept fire, the sharp smell, the night candle); the death; the neighbor-women and kin arriving to wash and lay out the body; the wake; the burial "short among the older places" with no cut stone; and Polly, drawn into the household's labor, doing "the next thing" through it. It is powerful and it is the chapter's emotional engine — but it asserts a birth and a death. The volume's own Note on Sources sets the rule ("set the known thing down…and do not invent to fill the silence"), and the Kit's worked examples name exactly this hazard ("the birth the record tidies away"). → Author's call: is there a documented DeGraffenreid infant death at the seat ca. 1766–1774 (a Bible entry, a family record) that this scene renders? If yes, cite it. If the sibling is authorial, the reader-facing note must not let this read as a documented death — it is the clearest "invented event on a silent record" in the chapter.

  • The laying-out and wake customs, the neighbor-women, the grandfather's words over the small grave — AR on whatever the underlying fact is.

§2.5 The gathering war & the father's ride — the last-alive departure

Documented.

  • The coming Revolution reaching the household as news (more riders, court-day changed, the busier road) is period context — S, kept correctly at the child's edge-of-comprehension distance.

  • The father's ride "over the line into the Carolina country on the place's business, in the cold coming-on of the year" is the dramatization of the 20 October 1775 Guilford County deed-of-trust trip — his last documented appearance alive (P, §III).

AR / disciplined inference.

  • The departure as final farewell is reconstruction on the documented last-alive date. What the record gives: Baker was alive in Guilford on 20 Oct 1775, and by Sep 1779 his widow was administratrix. What the chapter stages: an ordinary cold-morning goodbye (the wide hand on the child's head, the horse down the lane and under the oak) that the reader knows was the last. The Historian's Closing is scrupulous that this is "the last the record holds of Baker DeGraffenreid alive" — the scene is AR, the frame is honest. The emotional freight (that this ordinary errand was the end) rests on the four-year silence, correctly named as a silence. Good discipline; flagged only so the reader-facing note keeps "last seen alive" distinct from "died on this trip" (the record does not say he died on the road, only that he was never documented alive again).

Gaps and open items

1. ⚠ "The littlest" (infant sibling death, §2.4) — verify or mark as authorial. The chapter asserts a sibling's birth and death. Confirm a documented DeGraffenreid infant death ca. 1766–1774, or ensure the reader-facing apparatus does not present this as documented. Highest-priority Ch 2 flag (an invented event, not interiority).

It is the culmination of the volume's method — and it carries the audit's most significant cluster of findings: the retired "June 1773" grandmother-death date recurs here three times (against the chapter's own Historian's Opening), the closing scene presents the invented refrain "we continue" as an actual Bible inscription, and the enslaved household reaches its fullest named presence with no documentary citation. These are items 1–3.

The documented frame (Historian's Opening / Closing / interludes)

Documented.

  • The household and its dates: Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson (20 in July 1785); Gideon holding the cleared land under Sarah Vass's deed of attorney of 19 Dec 1783; the Caswell grant of 13 Nov 1779 (400 acres, Guilford → Rockingham County, created 1785, seat Wentworth); Sarah Vass administering Baker's estate since Sept 1779 — all P/R (§§III, IV).

  • The daughters: Sarah (b. 9 Oct 1780), Mildred Baker (b. 11 Nov 1782), Nancy (b. 14 July 1784), Ursula (b. ~13 Dec 1787 — Chadwell Bible p. 93, §I), Polly Baker (b. Apr 1790); ten children to adulthood — P/R.

  • Tscharner's death: second week of February 1794, at Lunenburg, age seventy-one, cause and exact day unrecorded — D/P (§VIII). The chapter renders it faithfully: he "went in his sleep… at his own parlor desk," found by Liza; "seventy-one years and two months." Consistent with the bibliography.

  • The Bible's passage south to Mary Baker (spring 1794) — P by structural fact, not instrument (§I; ODQ-V2-06): "not by a documented instrument… but by the plain circumstance that Baker DeGraffenreid was twenty years dead with no grave, and his daughter was the one who would keep the record." The Keeping Forward interlude states this honestly.

  • The national interludes (the Constitution: signed 17 Sept 1787, Hillsborough declines 184–83 in 1788, Fayetteville ratifies 21 Nov 1789; St. Clair's defeat and the FitzSimons inquiry; the French news of 1793; the Philadelphia yellow fever) are period history — S (§XI) — correctly kept as fragments arriving at the household.

  • The Vol II → III handoff (Keeping Forward): the household moves to Williamson County, Tennessee (a state 1796) by 1810; Mary Baker dies there January 1823, age 58, the date disputed by six days (Chadwell 1 Jan / Reynolds 7 Jan) — P (§VIII). Sarah Johnson (→ Sarah Hubbard), Cumberland County NC (§6.7 parenthetical) writes her mother's 1823 death entry — consistent with the Volume III Bridge chain (Sarah Johnson → Hubbard → Rorie). Cross-volume anchors agree.

⚠ 1. The retired "June 1773" grandmother-death date recurs three times — against the chapter's own Historian's Opening

This is the same error flagged in the Chapter 4 memo, and Chapter 6 shows it is systematic, not a one-off — the correction reached the bibliography and some prose but not all of it. Within Chapter 6:

  • Correct (Historian's Opening): the elder Mary Baker "had died in the mid-1750s, before the granddaughter who would carry her name was born."

  • Wrong (§6.6 dialogue, to Ursula): buried "beside the place where your grandmother Mary Baker DeGraffenreid lies, who died in the June of 1773 a year before I was born." (Doubly broken: the date is the retired one, and "a year before I was born" is impossible — the heroine was born in 1764.)

  • Wrong (§6.7): "beneath the entry he had made for his wife Mary Baker in June 1773."

  • Wrong (Keeping Forward): "buried in the family ground beside the elder Mary Baker, who had gone before him in June 1773."

Combined with the two Chapter 4 occurrences, the retired date appears at least five times across the volume, while the corrected "ca. 1756 / mid-1750s" appears in Ch 2, the Ch 6 Historian's Opening, and the bibliography (which states outright the "7 June 1773" was "a false precision on a wrong year and was corrected"). → The single highest-priority correction in the Vol II audit: sweep every "1773" reference to the elder Mary Baker's death and reconcile to the record the volume itself commits to (ca. 1756). The §6.6 "a year before I was born" line is additionally self-contradictory and cannot stand as written. No correction made.

⚠ 2. The closing image presents "we continue" as an actual Bible fly-leaf inscription

  • The accession scene (§6.7) has Mary write the French death entry and then, beneath a blot, "we continue" in English on the fly-leaf — and the Keeping Forward interlude, in the historian's own voice, treats this as fact: "the two English words she wrote beneath the blot were the first words in any language not French to go onto the family record's leaves at all," and "That she wrote them in the Bible only once, and only there… is the reason the page can carry the weight it carries."

  • This is the trilogy's strongest presentation of the refrain as a documented inscription — not a character's thought but the narrator asserting the Bible fly-leaf bears "we continue." Per the established trilogy-wide rule (Vol III Bridge/Frame memo; Ch 3 memo): "we continue" is authorial reconstruction; there is no surviving letter, journal, or Bible page bearing it. The Ch 3 Historian's Closing itself says the two words are "not in the Bible." Ch 6's closing interlude, read straight, says the opposite. → Author's call: this is the most important consistency item for the whole trilogy's apparatus. The reader-facing Note must never cite "we continue" as documented fly-leaf text, and the Keeping Forward interlude's assertion that the Bible carries the words needs reconciling with the Ch 3 statement that it does not. (The dramatic device is legitimate as reconstruction; the danger is the historian-voice interlude reading as documentation.)

⚠ 3. The enslaved household — fullest presence, still uncited (carried from Ch 5)

  • Chapter 6 names Old Hetty (at the hearth from five each morning, in nearly every scene), Young George (at the lot), Daniel ("Old Hetty's grandson," who carries letters), and Liza (at Lunenburg, who finds Tscharner dead). With Phillis and Hetty from Ch 5, the volume's enslaved cast is substantial and recurring.

  • As in Ch 5: no documentary citation, no bibliography section, no accounting in their own right. These are the people whose labor runs the household the chapter is about, and the apparatus that documents the salt-price to the shilling and the pension file to the captain is silent on them. Tscharner's 1794 will and inventory (§II) is the obvious instrument that would name enslaved people held; it is cited for the estate but not mined for them. → Carried volume-level flag (see the Summary memo): document these names against the 1794 inventory / Baker's estate, or mark them authorial, and decide whether Vol II owes the enslaved people the kind of accounting Volume III makes central.

The reconstruction (the chapter's method)

AR — the domestic and economic scenes.

  • §6.1 (Nancy's first birthday), §6.2 (the January letter), §6.3 (the drover and the salt-price error), §6.4 (the lamp-reading), and the §6.5–§6.7 domestic frame are reconstruction on the documented dates. The salt-price sub-plot (Sumner's April figure, Halsey's correction, the thirty-shilling cost absorbed) is invented economic texture illustrating "the household priced each fragment and proceeded." None of the incident is documented; it dramatizes the volume's thesis.

  • "we continue" recurs as written in §6.2 (the 1788 letter) and §6.5 (the Oct 1793 letter) as well as §6.7 — all AR (see item 2).

  • The grandfather's letters (the Cicero, the French news, "je me porte bien," "your grandfather who loves you") are invented artifacts — no such letters survive; the "habit" device again. Honest, but not documents.

  • The accession scene (waking in the small hours, the blot, the two words) is the volume's closing reconstruction on the documented facts (Tscharner's death; the Bible passing to Mary). The "keeping comes up after the hand moves" motif is the volume's authorial thesis.

  • Minor: the Keeping Forward interlude calls Mary's entry "the first entry… made by a woman's hand since Barbara" — but the Bridge shows Christopher, not Barbara, writing the 1714 entries (Barbara was asleep). "Since Barbara" implies Barbara wrote in the Bible; confirm the phrasing is intended, or it is a loose reference.

Gaps and open items

1. ⚠ Sweep the retired "June 1773" grandmother-death date (highest priority). Recurs in §6.6, §6.7, and the Keeping Forward interlude — against the chapter's own Historian's Opening and the bibliography's ca. 1756. The §6.6 "a year before I was born" line is also internally impossible. Combined with the two Ch 4 occurrences, five in all. Reconcile the whole volume to ca. 1756.

2. ⚠ "we continue" presented as a Bible inscription (§6.7 + Keeping Forward). The historian-voice interlude asserts the fly-leaf bears the English words; the trilogy-wide rule and Ch 3's own Closing say it does not. Reconcile so the reader-facing apparatus never cites it as documented text.

3. ⚠ The enslaved household (Old Hetty, Young George, Daniel, Liza; + Phillis, Hetty). Named throughout, uncited. Mine Tscharner's 1794 inventory / Baker's estate; decide the volume's accounting. Volume-level structural flag.

4. Vincent's homecoming and service length — internal inconsistency with Ch 5. Ch 6 Historian's Opening: "seven years' service… walked back from Yorktown to Lunenburg in the spring of 1782." Ch 5 §5.7: home first week of December 1781, ~4y10m service. The two chapters disagree on both the date and the length (Ch 6 matches the pension file's "roughly seven years"; Ch 5 does not). Reconcile (ODQ-V2-03).

5. Tscharner's death (documented) vs. the accession scene (reconstructed). The death — second week Feb 1794, age 71, cause/day unrecorded — is documented; the deathbed-by-the-desk detail (found by Liza) and the whole Rockingham accession night are reconstruction. Keep distinct in the reader-facing note.

6. "Since Barbara" (Keeping Forward). Confirm the phrasing — the Bridge has Christopher, not Barbara, making the 1714 entries.

7. Cross-volume handoff confirmed: Williamson Co. TN by 1810; Mary Baker d. Jan 1823 (Chadwell 1 Jan / Reynolds 7 Jan); Sarah Johnson → Hubbard writes the entry — agrees with the Volume III Bridge chain. No action; noted.

End of Chapter 6 Sources Memo v1. Load-bearing result: the documented frame is strong and consistent with Volume III's opening — the deed of attorney, the Caswell grant, the daughters' births, Tscharner's Feb 1794 death, the Bible's structural passage south, and the 1823 Tennessee handoff. But the volume's closing chapter carries its heaviest audit load: the retired "June 1773" grandmother-death date recurs three times here (against the chapter's own Historian's Opening), the closing interlude presents the invented refrain "we continue" as an actual Bible inscription in the historian's own voice, and the enslaved household reaches its fullest named presence (Old Hetty, Young George, Daniel, Liza) with no documentary citation. All three are the author's to resolve; nothing was corrected.

The Epilogue — We Continue (audited here)

The Epilogue is the author's first-person present-day voice, matching the Vol I and Vol III epilogue register. It is authorial reflection (AR) built on a faithfully recapped documented spine: Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson as the keeper; Baker DeGraffenreid's absent grave ("There is no grave. There is a date he was last seen alive and a date by which his wife was signing herself his administratrix, and between those two dates there is a silence about four years deep") — a scrupulous statement of ODQ-V2-01; the Caswell grant that "stood in a woman's name"; and the line's descent by daughters into the volume that follows. It invents no fact. It does, however, restate "we continue" as inscriptions Mary wrote "twice that night… and… a third time, under his death," carrying the same trilogy-wide flag: the refrain is authorial, and the reader-facing apparatus must never cite it as documented text (see item 2 below).

The overall result

The volume's discipline is real and, in its framing apparatus, exemplary. Across the Bridge, six chapters, and the Epilogue:

  • The documentary spine is solid and cross-checks with Volume III. Two Chowan wills close the elder Mary Baker's parentage; the 1742 and 1779 marriages, the 1775 Guilford deed and 1779 administratrix appointment (the four-year silence), the 1779 Caswell grant, the daughters' births, Vincent's pension-file engagements, and Tscharner's Feb 1794 death are documented, and the handoff daughter Sarah Johnson (b. 9 Oct 1780 → Hubbard) matches the Volume III Bridge chain exactly.

  • The reconstruction invents scene, not fact — with the flagged exceptions. The child's-eye childhood, the war interiors, the granular wedding morning, the thirteen years of keeping are close-third on documented frames.

  • The Historian's Openings/Closings are model discipline. Chapters 2, 3, and 6 state the central silence (Baker's unaccounted death) as a silence, label tradition as tradition, and refuse to build across the gap.

The audit's value is the handful of places where a claim outruns — or contradicts — the record. Those are the ⚠ items.

⚠ Items requiring the author's decision (ranked)

1. The elder Mary Baker's death — a retired date still printed at least five times (the volume's Reuben-age-class error). The prose gives her death as "June 1773" in Ch 4 (§4.4, twice) and Ch 6 (§6.6, §6.7, and the Keeping Forward interlude) — while the bibliography (§VIII) says that exact "7 June 1773" "was a false precision on a wrong year and was corrected" to ca. 1756, and Ch 2 and the Ch 6 Historian's Opening use the correct mid-1750s reading. The §6.6 line — "June of 1773 a year before I was born" — is additionally impossible (the heroine was born 1764). Internal contradiction + against the corrected record + load-bearing for Ch 2's whole childhood frame. → Sweep every "1773" grandmother-death reference to ca. 1756. Top correction.

2. "we continue" is authorial — and the closing presents it as a Bible inscription. The refrain is invented (no surviving letter, journal, or Bible page bears it); Ch 3's own Historian's Closing says it is "not in the Bible." But Ch 6 §6.7 and the Keeping Forward interlude, in the historian's own voice, assert the fly-leaf carries "we continue" ("the first words in any language not French to go onto the family record's leaves"), and the Epilogue restates it as written "twice… and a third time." → Reconcile so no volume's apparatus ever cites "we continue" (or "She came" / "she held the line") as documented primary text. Trilogy-wide rule; this is its most exposed instance.

3. The enslaved household — named throughout, absent from the apparatus. Phillis, Old Hetty, Young George, Daniel, and Liza are named and depicted as "owned by the place," their labor load-bearing — but the Working Bibliography has no section on enslaved people and cites none of them, in a volume documented elsewhere to the deed and the muster roll. → Mine Tscharner's 1794 will and inventory (§II) and Baker's estate for their names; cite where documented, mark where authorial; decide whether Vol II gives the enslaved people the accounting Volume III makes central. The trilogy's "not her metaphor" discipline applies.

4. Christopher VI's October 1742 death — a commitment stated as fact (Ch 1; ODQ-V2-06). §1.5 opens "Christopher VI DeGraffenreid died at Williamsburg in October 1742" as flat fact; the bibliography flags it as the volume's commitment against the 1925 History's "ca. 1750," "no hard probate date in hand." The whole Ch 1 "accession" turns on it. → Soften to the commitment, or run the Prince George Co. / ECU (MC 0796) search.

5. Gideon Johnson's service status at the November 1779 wedding (ODQ-V2-02). Ch 3 (softly) and Ch 4 (flatly, "home since February") present his soldiering as concluded; the record leaves open whether he served in the nine-month gap or in the southern campaign after. → Decide the reading; apply to both chapters.

6. Vincent's service length and homecoming — internal contradiction between Ch 5 and Ch 6 (ODQ-V2-03). Ch 5: home first week of December 1781, ~4 years 10 months. Ch 6 Historian's Opening: "seven years' service… walked back… in the spring of 1782." The pension file (R.2845) references "roughly seven years," so Ch 6 matches it and Ch 5 does not. → Reconcile the length and the date; confirm the line named (Virginia vs. North Carolina) across Ch 3/4/5.

Lesser items to verify (per the memos)
