The Heroine and the Woman

The Russian woman of the Imperial age, in the novels and outside them. Starting from Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time.

Near the end of "Princess Mary," Lermontov's Pechorin stops his diary to explain women. He claims to understand them better since he stopped fearing them, and to know their little weaknesses and their peculiar logic. He boasts that he has never been the slave of a woman he loved, and that he always gained power over her will. Then the book shows us four women, and every one of them is there to tell us something about him. That pattern runs through the whole Russian canon. The novels keep writing one woman, an ideal held up to judge the men. The record keeps turning up a different one: she owned her own estates, followed her husband to Siberia, earned a doctorate in mathematics, rode with the hussars, and shot the governor of St. Petersburg. This essay is about the distance between the two, and about how often the second woman used the first one's image to get where she was going.

The puzzle: four women and a mirror

A Hero of Our Time (1840) has four women in it, and each has a job to do.

Bela, the Circassian girl in the first story, is traded by her brother for a horse and then won over by Pechorin's patience and gifts. Once she loves him he grows bored, and she dies. The smuggler girl in "Taman" flirts with him and tries to drown him. Princess Mary, the young society beauty, is courted as a game to annoy a rival and is then told coldly that she was never loved. Vera, married, ill, and the one woman who sees him clearly, loves him anyway. Her farewell letter sends him galloping after her until his horse drops dead under him.

Each woman tests Pechorin, or reveals him, or suffers for him. Not one of them is given an inner life of her own that the novel cares to follow. That isn't a flaw in Lermontov so much as the design. The book is a portrait of a man, and the women are the mirrors he is reflected in. Vera has a real original. Varvara Lopukhina was, in his biographer Walter Vickery's words, Lermontov's most serious attachment. He fell for her in Moscow when he was a student, and she returned the feeling. Then he left for Petersburg, and in 1835, probably under pressure from her parents, she married N. F. Bakhmetev, a much older man. The marriage was unhappy, and Lermontov never forgot her. He sent her copies of his poem Demon, and in his unfinished novel Princess Ligovskaya, written in 1836, he gave his Pechorin a Vera who had sworn to wait for him and married an older husband instead. Vickery reads that Vera as Lopukhina outright. The Vera of A Hero of Our Time carries the same history: the old love, now married to someone else, whom Pechorin can neither have nor let go. That makes even the most sympathetic of the four women a man's memory of a woman.

Pechorin's speech about women is a generalization about women everywhere. But the women Lermontov knew were Russian. The question is whether a distinct "Russian woman" emerges from the books he and his successors wrote, and whether she matches the women who actually lived in the empire.

The heroine the men wrote

The canonical Russian heroine has a family tree, and it starts with Pushkin.

Tatiana in Eugene Onegin (1833) is a provincial girl who reads novels, falls in love, and writes the first letter. Onegin lectures her and walks away. Years later he finds her married to a general and the queen of Petersburg society, and now he is the one writing letters. She admits she still loves him and tells him she will stay faithful to her husband. That refusal made her the founding figure. In his famous Pushkin Speech of June 1880, Dostoevsky called Tatiana the apotheosis of the Russian woman: rooted in the soil and the faith, morally firmer than the rootless, Europeanized man who rejected her. It is a striking moment. A male novelist, fifty years later, takes a male poet's character and declares her the national type.

From there the line runs through the rest of the canon.

Turgenev's heroines. Readers spoke of the "Turgenev girl": quiet, serious, and braver than the men around her. Liza Kalitina in A Nest of the Gentry gives up love for a convent. Elena in On the Eve leaves Russia with a Bulgarian revolutionary because no Russian man she knows is equal to her. In Fathers and Sons, Anna Odintsova is the cool, self-possessed widow who refuses Bazarov and survives him. The same novel gives us Kukshina, the "emancipated woman," played for laughs: she smokes, talks chemistry badly, and has read George Sand.

Tolstoy. Natasha Rostova is the most alive character in War and Peace, and the epilogue turns her into a contented mother who has stopped caring how she looks. Tolstoy plainly approves. Anna Karenina sets Anna, who leaves her husband for love and is destroyed, against Kitty, who marries Levin and finds meaning in children and the estate. In Resurrection, Katyusha Maslova is seduced by the hero as a servant girl and ends up a prostitute. The novel is about his conscience, and she is how he finds it.

Dostoevsky. He tends to split women into two types. One is the meek sufferer who redeems: Sonya Marmeladova in Crime and Punishment, who works on the "yellow ticket" (the prostitute's license) to feed her family and then follows Raskolnikov to Siberia. The other is the proud, wounded woman who destroys and is destroyed: Nastasya Filippovna in The Idiot, and Grushenka and Katerina Ivanovna in The Brothers Karamazov. Demons adds Varvara Petrovna Stavrogina, the formidable provincial matriarch, and a whole gallery of younger women. (No spoilers here for anyone still listening.)

Nekrasov. The poet gave the type its anthem. In Frost the Red-Nosed (1863) he describes the peasant woman of the Russian village who will stop a galloping horse and walk into a burning hut. In 1872–73 he published a long poem with the title that sums up this essay: "Russian Women" (Russkie zhenshchiny). It is about two Decembrist wives.

What this heroine has in common is clear. She is morally stronger than the man. She is defined by sacrifice: she stays, she follows, she suffers, she redeems. And her strength always serves the story of a man, whether that's Onegin's comeuppance, Levin's contentment, Raskolnikov's resurrection, or Nekhliudov's. Gogol's Dead Souls has almost no women at all, apart from the miserly widow Korobochka and some gossiping ladies of the town.

The American scholar Barbara Heldt gave this figure a name in Terrible Perfection (1987). Her argument is that the "strong Russian heroine" was largely a male creation, a figure of perfection whose job was to measure and redeem men. Women writing about themselves in the same period, Heldt says, told a different story, one of confinement, boredom, and frustrated work.

The women the record shows

Step outside the novels and the picture changes in ways the novels hardly hint at.

The law was a paradox. On one side, the Russian wife was legally bound to obey her husband as head of the family, to live where he lived, and, until a law of March 12, 1914, she needed his permission to get her own internal passport. Without that passport she could not legally travel, take a job, or live apart from him. Divorce went through the Church and was close to impossible. On the other side, Russian noblewomen had property rights that women in England and France would not win until the second half of the nineteenth century. From 1753 a married noblewoman could buy, sell, and mortgage her own estates without her husband's consent. The historian Michelle Marrese, in A Woman's Kingdom (2002), showed that noblewomen used those rights constantly, as active players in the land market. A woman who could not leave her husband's house without his say-so could still sell the estate it stood on, if it was hers.

Peter opened the terem. Before Peter the Great, elite Muscovite women lived largely secluded in the women's quarters of the house, the terem. In 1718 Peter required them to attend his new "assemblies," mixed social evenings modeled on the West. Within a few years of his death Russia entered what is sometimes called the century of empresses. Catherine I, Anna, Elizabeth, and Catherine II ruled for almost seventy of the years between 1725 and 1796. Catherine II founded the Smolny Institute in 1764, the first state school for girls of noble birth, and in 1783 she made Princess Ekaterina Dashkova director of the Academy of Sciences, which made Dashkova the first woman known to have headed a national academy.

The Decembrist wives made the myth real, and then the myth made them. After the failed revolt of December 1825, eleven women followed the convicted officers to Siberia. Strictly, nine were wives. The other two were fiancées who married their men in exile: Pauline Gueble, a Frenchwoman who left her baby daughter with Ivan Annenkov's mother to join him, and Camille Le Dantu, a governess's daughter, who married Vasily Ivashev. Fifteen wives asked the Emperor's permission to go; only nine went. Nicholas I set the terms: they gave up their noble status, and those who went could not take their children or return. Maria Volkonskaya, twenty years old and daughter of a hero of 1812, left her baby son behind; he died soon after. Ekaterina Trubetskaya was the first to go. Orlando Figes, in Natasha's Dance, uses the Volkonsky family to trace this story. The women chose the act themselves. Within two generations Nekrasov had turned them into "Russian Women," and they became the model that later radical women set themselves to live up to. This is the closest the record comes to the canonical heroine, and it is worth noticing that it was the women who lived it first and the poet who wrote it down afterward.

The cavalry maiden. Nadezhda Durova cut her hair, put on a man's clothes, and in 1806 enlisted in a cavalry regiment. She served in uhlan and hussar regiments through the Napoleonic wars and fought at Borodino. When her secret came out, Alexander I let her stay in the army under the name Aleksandrov. Pushkin published her memoir in his journal in 1836. She is the strongest counterexample to the Pechorin view of women, and she fought in the same war that Tolstoy wrote about.

The peasant woman carried the empire. Most Russian women were peasants, and their lives looked nothing like Tatiana's. A peasant bride moved into her husband's family's household, where she was at the bottom of the order beneath her mother-in-law and subject to the household head, the bolshak. She did the field work alongside the men, then the house work besides. The essay on the household, "The Icon and the Rod," has the darker side of this world through Gorky's My Childhood: a grandfather who beat his wife and children, and a son who beat his own wife to death. It also has its center, Gorky's grandmother Akulina, the person who taught him to love the world. After emancipation in 1861, and above all after the 1890s, peasant women moved in growing numbers to the cities as servants, factory workers, and wet nurses. Barbara Engel's Between the Fields and the City (1994) follows them.

The "nihilist girl" was not a joke. Turgenev made Kukshina a parody of the emancipated woman, but the real ones were serious. Chernyshevsky's novel What Is to Be Done? (1863), written in a prison cell, gave them a heroine: Vera Pavlovna escapes her family through a "fictitious marriage" and sets up a sewing cooperative. Real women copied the plan. In 1868 Sofia Kovalevskaya contracted a fictitious marriage so that she could study abroad, since Russian universities would not admit her. In 1874 she received a doctorate in mathematics from Göttingen, and she later became a professor at Stockholm, one of the first women in Europe to hold a full chair. So many Russian women went to study at Zurich that in 1873 the government ordered them home, fearing they were being radicalized. At home, the state allowed "higher courses for women" outside the universities: the Guerrier Courses in Moscow (1872) and the Bestuzhev Courses in St. Petersburg (1878).

The revolutionaries. The radical movement of the 1870s drew women in a proportion no Western movement matched. Richard Stites, in The Women's Liberation Movement in Russia (1978), and Barbara Engel, in Mothers and Daughters (1983), both show how many of them there were. In January 1878 Vera Zasulich walked into the office of the governor of St. Petersburg, General Trepov, and shot him for having a political prisoner flogged. That March a jury acquitted her, and the crowd outside cheered. Sofia Perovskaya, the daughter of a former governor of St. Petersburg, directed the bomb-throwers who killed Alexander II on March 1, 1881. She was hanged a month later, the first woman executed in Russia for a political crime. Vera Figner of the same organization spent twenty years in the Shlisselburg fortress. These women had read the novels. Their asceticism and self-sacrifice look a great deal like the canonical heroine's, turned to a new purpose.

Was the "Russian woman" real?

The two halves of this essay don't simply contradict each other. They tangle.

Heldt's case is strong as far as it goes. The heroine of the great novels exists mostly to measure, judge, and redeem men. Her author is almost always a man, and her inner life usually ends where his story needs it to. Pechorin's women are the purest case, and Lermontov at least is honest about it: the book's title is ironic, and the man at its center is the problem.

But historians who work on the women themselves complicate the picture. Stites and Engel both show that the ethic of self-sacrifice was not only something men imposed. Radical women took it over, often from the religious models they grew up with, and turned it into a way to leave home, study, organize, and in some cases kill. The Decembrist wives acted before the poem about them was written. Kovalevskaya and dozens of others copied Vera Pavlovna's fictitious marriage because it worked. The image was partly a male invention and partly a tool women picked up and used. And the most famous examples of the type were then written back into literature, completing the loop.

The record also shows things the novels almost never do. The noblewoman managing her estates and haggling over land is hardly anywhere in the canon. Neither is the peasant woman as anything more than a symbol, the factory girl, or the university student who wasn't a parody. The literature captured the Russian woman's moral weight and missed most of her work.

Coda: February 23, 1917

The Imperial age ended, fittingly, on International Women's Day. On February 23, 1917 (March 8 in the Western calendar), women textile workers in Petrograd walked out of their mills and joined the bread lines, calling the men out of the factories. Within a week the monarchy was gone. That July the Provisional Government gave Russian women the vote, ahead of Britain, France, and the United States. The bread-line essay, "The Bread Line and the Scapegoat," tells the story from the other side. The women who started it were not Tatianas. They were the women the novels had mostly left out.

Where this leaves the reader

I read Pechorin's little sermon on women, his boredom with the scripts he thinks they all follow, as one more tell about who Pechorin really is, or at least who Lermontov thought he was. It shows a man's arrogance: the belief that he has women figured out, down to their small maneuvers, so thoroughly that the chase no longer offers any challenge. That is the portrait of a man the book doesn't admire. But I don't think it is only that. Lermontov wrote it, and you cannot write what you do not know. Some part of Pechorin's view of women was probably Lermontov's own.

Of all the women in my reading, the one who has fascinated me most is not from the novels at all. She is Tania Miagkova, from Yuri Slezkine's The House of Government: a Bolshevik whose love for her family, strength, and belief in her cause held through exile, prison, and Kolyma. She impressed me enough that she has an essay of her own on this site, The Believer Who Could Not Prove It. She was born in 1897, a subject of the Tsar, and raised by a revolutionary mother. She belongs to the second half of this essay: the women the record shows, carried past 1917 into a world that asked even more of them.

From the literature, I agree with Dostoevsky about Pushkin's Tatiana. But I don't think you see who she really is until the end, years later, when she and Onegin meet again. The girl who wrote the letter could be any reader of romances. The woman who refuses him, still loving him and still choosing her word over her heart, is someone else entirely. That is the moment she stops being Onegin's mirror and becomes herself, and it is the scene that earned her Dostoevsky's tribute.

A note on certainty: the legal history is documented. The 1753 property ruling and the 1914 passport law are matters of record, and Marrese's work on how noblewomen used their property rights is the standard account. The Decembrist count (nine wives and two fiancées, out of fifteen wives who asked to go) follows Biel's dissertation. The dates and outcomes of the Decembrist wives, Durova, Kovalevskaya, Zasulich, and Perovskaya are well established. Vera's link to Varvara Lopukhina follows Vickery, who makes it explicitly for the Vera of Princess Ligovskaya; carrying it over to the Vera of A Hero of Our Time is the standard reading, but no document from Lermontov states it. The argument in Sections B and D is interpretation. Heldt reads the strong heroine as a male construction; Stites and Engel give more weight to women's own use of the ideal of self-sacrifice; and some historians who dismissed noblewomen's property rights as meaningless in a patriarchal system are answered by Marrese's evidence that women used them. This essay leans toward the middle position, that the image was both imposed and appropriated, and presents that as argument, not fact. Like the other essays, it grew out of questions put to Claude and was drafted with Claude's help; the reading and the judgments are the author's.
