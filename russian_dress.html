A Companion to the Atlas

Who Wore What

A spotter's guide to the dress of Imperial Russia — how to tell a hussar from an uhlan, a Don Cossack from a Kuban one, a monk from a parish priest, and a nihilist from everybody else.

The Russian novelists assumed their readers could read clothes. When Tolstoy puts Nikolai Rostov in a hussar's pelisse, when Lermontov has Pechorin ride out dressed as a Circassian, when Turgenev gives Kukshina cropped hair and a cigarette, the costume is doing half the characterization. In an empire where rank was law, dress was a public label: it told you a man's regiment, his class, his faith, his province, and sometimes his crime. This page is a key to those labels. Each entry gives what to look for — the details that tell one type from its neighbors — and where you meet it in the books.

The pictures are public-domain works on Wikimedia Commons — Viskovatov's uniform plates (1841–62), Nikolai Zaretsky's Russian Army in 1812 series (1911), and Prokudin-Gorsky's color photographs (1905–15) among them. The spotter's key

Start with the head and the shoulders — that is where most of the difference lives.

If you see……it's probably
A square-topped cap and a lance with a pennonAn uhlan (lancer)
A jacket covered in horizontal braid, with a second fur-trimmed jacket slung off one shoulderA hussar
A steel or black breastplate over a white coat, a crested helmetA cuirassier or Chevalier Guard
A long coat with rows of cartridge tubes across the chest, a tall fleece hat, a dagger at the beltA Caucasian highlander — or a Kuban/Terek Cossack dressed like one
A colored stripe down the trouser leg, no spurs, a short whipA Cossack; the stripe's color names the host
A sky-blue uniformThe Corps of Gendarmes — the political police
A long kaftan in a solid regimental color, a fur-trimmed cap, a crescent-bladed axeA strelets, before Peter
A black tall hat with a veil falling behindA monk (and if the hat is white, a metropolitan)
A shirt buttoned at the side of the neck, worn outside the trousers with a beltA peasant or workman
A sleeveless pinafore dress over a white shirt — or a checked wrap skirtA peasant woman: the pinafore (sarafan) from the north and center, the wrap (poneva) from the south
A full beard, a long dark coat cut tight at the waist, high boots, in townA merchant
A young woman with cropped hair, a plain dark dress, no crinolineA nihilist of the 1860s

Before Peter — the Muscovite army

Long robes, fur, and color by regiment. Peter would sweep all of it away.

Streltsy

Musketeers · c. 1550 – 1698

Ivan the Terrible's standing infantry, Muscovy's first. They lived in their own suburbs, traded on the side, and grew into a hereditary caste that made and unmade rulers. After their revolt of 1698 Peter had more than a thousand executed and broke up the corps.

Look for: an ankle-length kaftan in a single bright color — each regiment had its own (red, crimson, green, yellow, blue…) with buttons and collar in a contrasting shade; a fur-trimmed cap; high colored boots; a matchlock musket and the berdysh, a long crescent-bladed axe used as a musket rest.

Where you meet them: Massie's Peter the Great — the 1682 uprising Peter watched as a boy, and the 1698 executions.

Boyar & Muscovite noble

Court dress · to c. 1700

The old elite dressed in layers of long robes, the richer the deeper. Status was worn in fur and height: a boyar's tall cylindrical fur hat announced his rank before he spoke. Beards were sacred.

Look for: a long robe with sleeves reaching past the hands (the feriaz or okhaben, sleeves sometimes tied behind), a tall sable or fox gorlatnaya hat, a full beard, staff in hand.

Where you meet them: Massie, again — Peter shaving the boyars' beards with his own hands on his return from Europe in 1698, and taxing beards from 1705.

Peter's army & the Guards

Peter put Russia in Western uniform — and the color he chose, dark green, stayed the army's color for nearly two centuries.

Preobrazhensky & Semyonovsky Life Guards

The senior Guards · from the 1690s

Born as Peter's boyhood "play regiments" in the villages of Preobrazhenskoe and Semyonovskoe, they became the first two regiments of the Guard and the kingmakers of the eighteenth century — every empress from Catherine I to Catherine II came to the throne with Guards bayonets behind her.

Look for: the dark green coat of all Russian infantry. To tell the two apart, look at the collar and facings: red for the Preobrazhensky, blue for the Semyonovsky. In the Napoleonic era, a tall shako; the Guard's gold or yellow braid on the collar.

Where you meet them: Massie's Peter and Catherine; War and Peace (Boris Drubetskoy is in the Semyonovsky); the February 1917 mutiny began in the Pavlovsky and Volynsky, but the Preobrazhensky followed.

Line infantryman, 1812

The army of War and Peace

The conscript serving twenty-five years, drawn by lot from his village — for his family, almost a funeral. He is the man at Borodino and Smolensk, and the silent hero of Tolstoy's battle chapters.

Look for: a dark green double-breasted coat cut short at the front; white trousers in summer, grey or dark green in winter; a tall black shako with a brass badge; a long grey greatcoat (shinel) rolled across the body on the march. Grenadiers wore a flaming-grenade badge; the shako plume marked the elite companies.

Where you meet him: War and Peace, Platon Karataev before his capture; the Poltava and 1812 material in the guide.

The cavalry — Napoleonic & Caucasus age

The most dressed-up men in Europe. In the novels the branch of cavalry is a character note: hussars are dashing and broke, Guards cuirassiers are rich, dragoons are working soldiers.

Hussar

Light cavalry · Hungarian style

Light horsemen for scouting and raiding, borrowed from Hungary along with their clothes. The hussar's reputation — reckless, gallant, in debt, drinking from the bottle on a windowsill — was half the appeal. The poet Denis Davydov, a hussar and partisan leader of 1812, built a whole literary persona on it.

Look for: the dolman, a short jacket covered across the chest in horizontal braid (frogging); the pelisse, a second fur-trimmed jacket worn slung off the left shoulder; a sabretache (flat leather case) hanging from the sword belt; tight braided breeches; a shako or fur busby. Each regiment had its own color scheme — the Life Guard Hussars wore red.

Where you meet him: Nikolai Rostov and Vaska Denisov in the Pavlograd Hussars, War and Peace. Lermontov himself was commissioned into the Life Guard Hussars at Tsarskoe Selo in 1834.

Uhlan (lancer)

Light cavalry · Polish style

The lancers came from Poland, and so did the look. The lance gave them reach against infantry and other horsemen; the pennon on it was there to frighten the enemy's horses.

Look for: the square-topped Polish cap (czapka), flaring into a flat square board on top; a short double-breasted jacket (kurtka) with a colored front panel (the plastron) buttoned across the chest; a lance with a two-colored pennon.

Where you meet him: Nadezhda Durova, the "cavalry maiden," served in uhlan and hussar regiments in disguise through the Napoleonic wars — see the essay The Heroine and the Woman.

Dragoon

Mounted infantry · the workhorse

Originally infantry who rode to battle and fought on foot, dragoons became the all-purpose cavalry of the empire and the bulk of the mounted troops in the Caucasus war. In 1882 Alexander III turned nearly all the army's hussars and uhlans into dragoons, and the old names did not come back until 1907.

Look for: the infantry's dark green coat, but with cavalry boots and a sword; a helmet in the early century, later a shako or cap; a carbine as well as a saber — the giveaway that he expects to fight on foot.

Where you meet him: Lermontov was exiled in 1837 to the Nizhny Novgorod Dragoons in the Caucasus for his poem on Pushkin's death. The Caucasus of A Hero of Our Time is a dragoon's country.

Cuirassier & Chevalier Guard

Heavy cavalry · the richest regiments

Big men on big horses for the shock charge. The Chevalier Guard and the Horse Guards were the most aristocratic and expensive regiments in the army — an officer needed a private fortune to serve in them — and they also furnished the palace guard.

Look for: a white coat (the kolet); a cuirass — breastplate, blackened or polished steel — over it; a helmet with a tall crest of black horsehair. On palace duty the Chevalier Guard wore a red tunic (supervest) over the white, with a star on the chest, and later a helmet topped with a silver or gilded double eagle.

Where you meet him: War and Peace — the Chevalier Guards' charge at Austerlitz; Vronsky in Anna Karenina is a Guards cavalry officer of exactly this world.

Cossacks

Not regiments but whole peoples under arms — the "hosts" (voiska), each with its land, its elected or appointed ataman, and its own color. Two tells for any Cossack: no spurs (they used the whip, the nagaika) and the shashka, a saber with no guard, worn edge-up.

Don Cossack

The senior host · the steppe

The largest and oldest of the hosts, on the lower Don. Don Cossacks harried Napoleon's retreat in 1812 and rode into Paris in 1814; the Cossack on a shaggy pony with a lance became Europe's image of Russia.

Look for: a dark blue coat (the chekmen or kaftan); broad trousers with a red stripe (lampas) down the leg; a tall cap (later a fleece papakha); lance, shashka and nagaika.

Where you meet him: Denisov's partisans in War and Peace; Poltava, where Mazepa's Cossacks split; the Cossacks breaking up crowds — or refusing to — in February 1917.

Kuban & Terek Cossacks

The Caucasus line

The Cossacks who held the Caucasus frontier fought the highlanders for generations and ended up dressing exactly like them. Their look later became the uniform of the Tsar's own escort.

Look for: the highlander's cherkeska (see below) with cartridge tubes across the chest, a beshmet undercoat, the fleece papakha, a felt burka cloak, the kinzhal dagger. Usually given as: Kuban — black cherkeska over a red beshmet; Terek — grey or black over a light blue one. No trouser stripe — that's the quick way to tell them from Don Cossacks.

Where you meet them: the background of A Hero of Our Time; Tolstoy's The Cossacks and Hadji Murat.

The eastern hosts

Ural · Orenburg · Siberia · the Far East

As the empire pushed east, new hosts were formed to hold the line — the Ural (Yaik) Cossacks, who rose with Pugachev in 1773; the Orenburg; the Siberian; and late in the century the Transbaikal, Amur and Ussuri hosts on the Chinese frontier.

Look for: the Don Cossack's cut of coat and the trouser stripe — the stripe's color tells the host. Commonly given as: Ural — crimson; Orenburg — light blue; Siberian — red; Astrakhan and the Far Eastern hosts — yellow.

Where you meet them: Pushkin's The Captain's Daughter (Pugachev); the Siberia companion.

His Majesty's Own Convoy

The Tsar's Cossack escort

The bodyguard that rode beside the emperor, recruited from the Kuban and Terek hosts. Its men stood behind Nicholas II at nearly every public appearance.

Look for: the cherkeska in scarlet for full dress — the most conspicuous uniform in any palace photograph — with a white or red beshmet and a black papakha.

Where you meet them: photographs of the last Romanovs; Rappaport, Massie's The Romanovs.

The late empire, 1855–1917

After the Crimean defeat the gorgeous uniforms began to go. Alexander III dressed the army as Russians — and then khaki arrived.

The "Russian" uniform of Alexander III

From 1881–82

Alexander III hated Western frippery and loved economy. He stripped the line army of plumes, braid and colored facings and dressed it in a plain double-breasted kaftan fastened with hooks, wide trousers tucked into high boots, and a low round lambskin cap. Officers grumbled that they looked like coachmen. The Guards kept much of their old finery for parade.

Look for: beards (newly permitted), no buttons down the front, the low fleece cap — the army of the 1880s looks like a peasant army, which was the point.

Where you meet it: the reign of the counter-reforms; Stolypin's and Witte's Russia.

Soldier of 1914–17

The Great War

The army that marched into East Prussia and Galicia and walked home in 1917. Its look is the one that passed, almost unchanged, to the Red Army and the Whites alike.

Look for: the khaki-green gymnastyorka, a loose pull-over tunic worn belted; the long grey greatcoat; a peaked cap (furazhka) in summer and a papakha in winter; shoulder boards with the regiment's number.

Where you meet him: Doctor Zhivago; Solzhenitsyn's March 1917; the Galicia and Second Order essays.

Gendarme

The political police · 1827–1917

Nicholas I created the Corps of Gendarmes in 1827, under Count Benckendorff, as the uniformed arm of the Third Section, the secret police. It watched writers, students, sectarians and officers, and its color became a byword.

Look for: sky blue — a light blue uniform no one else wore, with white or silver trim and, for officers, a silver aiguillette. "The blue uniforms" meant the police.

Where you meet him: the short poem "Farewell, unwashed Russia," traditionally attributed to Lermontov (the attribution is disputed), names the "blue uniforms" and the people who obey them; the radicals' trials of the 1870s.

Sailor

The fleet · from Peter on

The navy was Peter's other child. By the twentieth century its sailors were the most radical men in uniform — the Potemkin mutiny of 1905, Kronstadt in 1917 and again, against the Bolsheviks, in 1921.

Look for: the blue-and-white striped undershirt (telnyashka, from 1874) under a wide square sailor's collar; a peakless cap (beskozyrka) with two black ribbons hanging behind, the ship's name on the band.

Where you meet him: A People's Tragedy — the Kronstadt sailors; Trotsky and Kronstadt in the Trotsky essay.

Church & state

Not only soldiers wore uniforms. Clerks and priests were labeled as precisely as hussars.

Civil servant (chinovnik)

The Table of Ranks in cloth

Peter's Table of Ranks made the bureaucrat a uniformed man. Each ministry had its own uniform, and his class on the Table could be read in the embroidery. Officials wore uniform to the office, often to church, and at every formal occasion.

Look for: a dark green or dark blue tailcoat with a high collar; buttons stamped with the ministry's device; embroidered oak or laurel leaves on collar and cuffs — the more embroidery, the higher the class; a bicorne hat and a small sword for full dress.

Where you meet him: Gogol's Akaky Akakievich, whose new overcoat is his whole life ("The Overcoat"); Karenin in Anna Karenina; Ivan Ilyich.

Parish priest (the "white" clergy)

Married village and town clergy

The Orthodox parish priest had to marry before ordination, and the priesthood ran in families — a hereditary estate of its own. "White" means married, not the color of the cloth.

Look for: a long cassock (podryasnik) under a wide-sleeved outer robe (ryasa); a large pectoral cross; long hair and a beard; a soft dark hat or a purple kamilavka (a brimless cylinder) as an honor. A married man, often seen with his wife (matushka) and children.

Where you meet him: the village priest in Gorky; Avvakum, a parish priest before he was a martyr — see The Two-Fingered Man.

Monk & bishop (the "black" clergy)

Monasteries and the hierarchy

Monks took vows of celibacy, and because only monks could become bishops, the black clergy ran the Church. An elder (starets) was a monk whose holiness drew pilgrims from all over Russia.

Look for: black from head to foot; the klobuk, a tall black cylinder hat with a black veil falling behind the shoulders. Rank in the head-dress: a bishop's klobuk is black; a metropolitan's is white; the patriarch's is white with a cross on top. A bishop also carries a staff and wears a medallion icon (panagia).

Where you meet him: Father Zosima in The Brothers Karamazov; the Synod essay; Rasputin, who was not a monk though often taken for one.

Old Believer

The schism of 1666 and after

The faithful who refused Nikon's reforms kept the old rites — and, deliberately, the old clothes. Many became merchants and industrialists, and their dress marked them out in any town.

Look for: an untrimmed beard (shaving was a sin); a long, plain, old-style kaftan; for women, a dark sarafan and a large shawl pinned under the chin, hair always hidden; in prayer, the cross made with two fingers, not three.

Where you meet him: The Two-Fingered Man; Rogozhin's merchant family in The Idiot has Old Believer roots.

The village

Four Russians in five were peasants. Their clothes were homespun, regional, and full of signals — above all, a woman's head-dress told you at a glance whether she was married.

Peasant man (muzhik)

Everywhere · unchanged for centuries

The dress Tolstoy put on in old age to make a point, and the dress the Slavophiles praised as the true Russia.

Look for: the kosovorotka, a long shirt that buttons at the side of the neck rather than the center, worn outside the trousers with a belt or sash; loose trousers; feet wrapped in cloth strips (onuchi) inside bast shoes (lapti) woven from lime bark, or boots for the better-off; a coarse cloth coat (armyak) or a sheepskin (tulup) in winter; a beard and hair cut "under the pot."

Where you meet him: Gerasim in The Death of Ivan Ilyich; Platon Karataev; Levin mowing with his peasants in Anna Karenina.

Peasant woman of the north & center

The sarafan

The image of "Russian costume" the world knows comes from here — the forest north and the provinces around Moscow.

Look for: the sarafan, a long sleeveless pinafore dress, over a white linen shirt (rubakha) with embroidered sleeves; an apron. On holidays a married woman wore the kokoshnik, a stiff head-dress rising in a crescent or a peak above the forehead, often pearled.

Where you meet her: Prokudin-Gorsky's photographs; Natasha's dance at her uncle's in War and Peace — the scene that gave Figes his title — is a noble girl dancing the peasant way.

Peasant woman of the south

The poneva

South of Moscow — Ryazan, Tula, Tambov, Voronezh — an older costume survived, and it looked nothing like the sarafan.

Look for: the poneva, a wrap skirt of dark wool, usually checked in dark blue, black or red, tied at the waist over the shirt; an apron; for married women a horned or "magpie" head-dress (kichka, soroka). A girl put on the poneva when she came of age — it was a sign she could marry.

Where you meet her: the Tambov countryside of the Tania essay; Tolstoy's Tula estate at Yasnaya Polyana lay in poneva country.

Girl or wife? The head tells you

A rule across the whole village

The single most important signal in village dress was hair. A married woman's hair was never seen in public, and to uncover it — or to have it uncovered by someone else — was a deep disgrace.

Look for: one braid hanging down the back, the crown left open or circled by a band (povyazka) — an unmarried girl. Two braids wound up under a cap (povoinik) and covered by a kerchief or head-dress — a married woman. The wedding rite included "unbraiding" the bride's single braid into two.

Where you meet it: the household in The Icon and the Rod; Katyusha Maslova's transformation in Resurrection.

Town & salon

After Peter, the nobility dressed as Europeans and spoke French. Everyone else, at first, did not — so clothes drew the line between the two Russias.

Merchant & his wife

The guilds · Moscow above all

The merchants kept the old Russian look long after the nobles dropped it — partly faith (many were Old Believers), partly pride. Their wives were famous for spending on dress.

Look for: a full beard; a long dark coat fitted at the waist and flaring below (poddyovka or sibirka); a waistcoat with a watch-chain; high polished boots, trousers tucked in; a peaked cap. His wife: bright silks, a costly shawl, a silk kerchief on her head, and pearls — the merchant wife's head was covered even when her dress was Parisian.

Where you meet them: Rogozhin in The Idiot; Ostrovsky's plays; the rich Moscow merchants of the essay on the bottle.

Nobleman

European dress · from 1700

Peter's decrees of 1700–05 ordered the nobility into "German" and Hungarian dress and taxed the beard. Within a century the Russian gentleman looked like a gentleman anywhere in Europe — except that half the time he was in uniform, since nearly every noble served.

Look for: clean-shaven (or, in the 1800s, side-whiskers and moustache — a full beard on a noble was a statement); a tailcoat or frock coat; the uniform worn as everyday dress, often of a regiment he had left years before. In the country, a dressing-gown (khalat) all day was the sign of a gentleman gone to seed.

Where you meet him: Onegin the dandy, with his three hours at the mirror; Oblomov in his dressing gown; Pechorin, careful about his clothes and contemptuous of Grushnitsky's.

Lady at court

The "Russian dress" of 1834

Here the story turns back on itself. In 1834 Nicholas I fixed the dress for ladies at court — and made it a stylized peasant costume. The noblewoman who spoke French and wore Paris fashions everywhere else had to appear before the Tsar in a version of the sarafan.

Look for: a velvet over-dress open down the front, with long hanging sleeves and a train, over a white satin gown — the sarafan, translated into silk and gold embroidery; on the head a jeweled kokoshnik with a veil. The color of the velvet and the embroidery could mark a lady-in-waiting's rank.

Where you meet her: the 1903 costume ball at the Winter Palace, when the whole court dressed as seventeenth-century Muscovites; the last Empress, Alexandra, in Rappaport and Massie.

Nihilist & student

The 1860s and after

The young radicals rejected their parents' world, and wanted it seen. Dress was a manifesto: plainness, practicality, and a calculated disregard for appearance.

Look for: men — long hair, a beard, a plaid thrown over the shoulders, high boots, blue-tinted spectacles. Women — cropped hair (the scandal), a plain dark woolen dress with white collar and no crinoline, spectacles, a cigarette. University students, from 1885, were required to wear a uniform — which the radicals wore as defiantly as anything else.

Where you meet them: Bazarov and Kukshina in Fathers and Sons; Raskolnikov, the ex-student in rags; the young people of Demons; The Heroine and the Woman.

The empire's peoples

By 1897 ethnic Russians were less than half the empire. A few of the costumes a Russian officer or exile would have met — a sample, not a survey.

Caucasian highlander

Circassians, Chechens and their neighbors

The costume that fascinated every Russian who served in the Caucasus — so much so that Russian officers and Cossacks adopted it wholesale. In A Hero of Our Time, Pechorin rides out in it and is pleased to be taken for a Kabardian.

Look for: the cherkeska, a long, close-fitting, collarless coat flaring below the waist, with rows of cartridge tubes (gazyri) sewn across both sides of the chest; a tall fleece papakha; a hooded bashlyk; a stiff felt burka cloak, square at the shoulders, that doubled as a tent; the kinzhal, a long straight dagger, at the belt; soft leather boots without heels.

Where you meet him: Kazbich and Azamat in "Bela"; Tolstoy's Hadji Murat.

Volga Tatar

Kazan and the Volga · Muslim

The heirs of the Golden Horde and of the khanate Ivan the Terrible conquered in 1552 — by the nineteenth century, an urban Muslim people of traders and scholars across the empire.

Look for: on men, a small embroidered skullcap (tubeteika) or a fez-like cap, a long robe or kazaki coat, soft leather boots; on women, a small velvet cap (kalfak) with a shawl over it, and heavy silver jewelry.

Where you meet him: the Tatar servant is a stock figure in nineteenth-century Petersburg novels; St. Basil's commemorates Kazan's fall — see the Landmarks.

Peoples of Siberia & the north

Buryat, Yakut, Nenets, Evenk and others

The native peoples the Cossacks and fur-traders met as they crossed Siberia, who paid the fur tribute (yasak) to the Tsar. Their clothing was built for cold no European costume could handle.

Look for: fur worn in layers, often fur-in and fur-out; the reindeer-skin parka pulled over the head (malitsa among the Nenets); beadwork and dyed-leather appliqué; among the Buryats, Mongol-style robes with a high collar and a pointed hat — Buddhists, not Orthodox.

Where you meet them: the Siberia companion; the exiles' memoirs from Avvakum to Kolyma.

Marked men

The state dressed its prisoners too — to be seen, and to be caught.

Convict (katorzhnik)

Penal servitude · Siberia

The hard-labor convict was meant to be unmistakable, so that an escaped man could be spotted anywhere. The marking was harsh and deliberate.

Look for: a coarse grey coat, often in two colors (one half grey, one half black or brown), with a cloth diamond sewn on the back — the "ace of diamonds" (bubnovyi tuz), which became slang for a convict; half the head shaved, front to back, renewed regularly; leg irons on the march. Until the 1860s, the face could also be branded.

Where you meet him: Dostoevsky's Notes from the House of the Dead, from his own four years at Omsk; Maslova and the convict column in Resurrection; the punishment page; the Gulag inherited the column, not the costume.

Why the clothes mattered

Two things run under this whole page. The first is Peter's split: after 1700 the nobility dressed as Europeans and everyone else stayed Russian, so that a glance told you which of the two nations a person belonged to. The second is how often the rulers then reached back across that line — Nicholas I putting court ladies in the sarafan, Alexander III dressing the army in kaftans, the Kuban Cossacks guarding the Tsar in the costume of the highlanders they had fought. Dress in Imperial Russia was never just fashion. It was the state's way of saying who you were — and, now and then, of pretending to be someone else.

A note on certainty: Russian uniforms changed constantly — sometimes every few years, and in the 1800s with nearly every reign — and each regiment had its own variations. The descriptions here are the type at a stated period, not a regulation-by-regulation record. The colors given for particular regiments and Cossack hosts follow the standard references (Viskovatov's Historical Description of the Clothing and Arms of the Russian Troops, 1841–62) and should be checked against the plates before images are added. Folk dress varied village by village; the sarafan/poneva and one-braid/two-braid rules are the broad pattern, with many local exceptions.
