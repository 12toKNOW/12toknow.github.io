The daughter who stayed

By the summer of 1860 she had been waiting so long that the waiting had stopped feeling like waiting and started feeling like the weather.

She was twenty-seven years old. She had been born on the twenty-first of November of 1832, in the cold end of that year, in Tennessee, and she had come down into Mississippi as a girl of fourteen or fifteen with the rest of them, and she had lived on the Rorie ridge ever since, three miles north of the post road, in the house her father had raised out of the cleared land, and nothing that the world would call an event had happened to her in all that time. She had not married. She had not gone anywhere. She had not buried a child of her own or borne one or stood up at her own wedding in the parlor the way her older sisters had stood up at theirs. She had gotten older, which is not an event. She had gotten older in the same house, on the same ridge, doing the same work, so slowly and so evenly that there was no day she could have pointed to and said, there, that was the day something changed, because nothing had changed, and the not-changing had gone on for ten years, and she was twenty-seven.

The house was not a poor house and it was not an unhappy one, and that was the strange part, the part she could not have explained to anyone who had not lived inside it. Her father was a man of means. Reuben Rorie was sixty that year, gray at the temples and slow at the one knee a horse had ruined in his forties, but the mill he had built had run all through the decade her sisters were marrying and it had made him money the whole time, so that by the summer of 1860 he was worth, by the reckoning the government would shortly take down, five thousand dollars — a great deal of money in that country, a standing-man's money, the kind of money that put a family at the front of the meeting-house and made a father's opinion worth the asking at the crossroads store. Her mother kept a full larder and a stocked press. There was coffee in the house that summer and sugar in the crock. The Rories were doing well. Everyone said so.

And a part of what they were worth, that summer, was people. The five thousand dollars was not all mill money, though the family would have called it mill money and Mary Jane, had anyone asked her, would have called it that too. Some of it was three human beings. The same government that was about to write the household into its book kept a second book that year, the one it kept for slaves, and on that one, set down against her father's name, there stood a woman of sixty-five, a man of thirty-six, and a girl of seven — three people held on the Rorie place, in a house that stood in the yard for the purpose — and their worth, reckoned in dollars by men who reckoned such things, was folded into the five thousand that seated the family at the front of the meeting-house. This account will not tell you Reuben Rorie's money was clean money, because it was not; it cannot ask what the man was worth and decline to say what he owned. The three people on that place have their own accounting to come, in their own chapters, and are not Mary Jane's to carry and will not be made hers. But the figure is named in this chapter, and the figure has people in it, and the book says so here, so that it never again sets down five thousand dollars as though the words meant only the mill.

Everything about the household was going well except the one thing, and the one thing was Mary Jane, and after her, Nancy.

She thought about it in the mornings, mostly, before the heat came up and while her hands were busy with the first work of the day and her mind was free to go where it went. Her hands, that summer, knew every task the house had to give and did them without her watching — the churn, the wheel, the loom in the cold months, the garden in the warm, the long turn of the wash, the bread, the milk, the care of her mother's chickens and her father's table. They were a grown woman's hands. They had been a grown woman's hands for years. They were not a bride's hands and they were not a mother's hands, and there was no work in the house that was waiting for them to become either, and they went on doing the work of a daughter in her father's house the way they had done it at seventeen and at twenty and at twenty-four, except that now the woman they belonged to was twenty-seven, and twenty-seven was a number that had come to mean something in that country that seventeen had not.

She knew the number. That was the thing. She knew it exactly. She could have told anyone who asked her the day and the year of her birth, because her mother had written it into the Bible on the marriages-and-births page in the careful keeping-hand, and the Bible sat on the shelf in the parlor where it had always sat, and Mary Jane had grown up reading her own line in it — Mary Jane, born the twenty-first of November, 1832 — the way a person reads a thing so often that the reading wears smooth and stops registering. Twenty-seven years and a little more than half a year. She did not have to count it. But she counted it anyway, the way you press a sore tooth with your tongue to see if it is still sore, and it was always still sore, and the number was always still twenty-seven.

Nancy was four-and-twenty. Nancy was the other one. The two of them were what was left of the daughters in the house, the two the house had not been able to marry off, and they knew it about each other and about themselves and they did not, as a rule, speak of it, because there was nothing to say that would not make it worse. The older girls were gone — Elizabeth married and away years ago, Louisa Adeline married to Alexander Houston and settled a little distance off with her own children coming up, the others paired and placed the way daughters were supposed to be paired and placed. The brothers were gone to their own roofs. William Hubbard had married and set up his own household in the very next place down the ridge, a wife and two small boys of his own now, and his voice carried over of an evening the way a married man's voice carries from his own dooryard. The house on the ridge that had once held a loft full of Rorie children held, in the summer of 1860, an aging father and an aging mother and two grown daughters that the aging father and mother could not, for all their five thousand dollars, get married.

It was not that no one would have them. That was not it, or not exactly. It was harder than that, and Mary Jane had had years to work out the shape of it and had worked it out and did not like the shape. A girl of eighteen with a landed father was a match. A girl of twenty-two with a landed father was a match a man might still make if the man was steady and the season was right. But a woman of twenty-seven, unmarried, in her father's house, was not a girl any longer and was not a widow either and was not anything the arithmetic of that country had a comfortable place for. She was past the age. Everyone knew what past-the-age meant. It did not mean unwanted, exactly, and it did not mean unlovely, and it did not mean anything you could put a finger on and argue with. It meant only that the young men who were laying out places and looking for wives looked, when they looked, at the girls of eighteen, because that was who a man looked at, and the woman of twenty-seven stood a little to the side of where the looking happened, and had stood there long enough that she had learned the exact width of the margin she stood in.

She was not bitter about it. She would have told you that, if you could have gotten her to speak of it at all, and it would have been mostly true. She was not a bitter woman. She had her mother's steadiness and something of her father's way of doing the work in front of him without complaining of it, and she had made, without ever deciding to, a kind of peace with the ridge and the house and the days that turned over on it. She loved her mother. She loved Nancy in the wordless way of a sister who has shared a loft and a fate. She was useful, and she knew she was useful, and there is a kind of standing that comes to a woman who is the one the house cannot do without, even a woman the house cannot marry. She had that standing. She was not miserable.

But she was twenty-seven, and she knew she was twenty-seven, and the knowing sat under everything like a stone under a field — you could plant over it and walk over it and forget it was there for weeks at a stretch, and then the plow would come down on it of a morning and the whole arm would ring with it, and there it was, the same stone, exactly where it had always been.

The summer went on hot and long. The cotton came up green on the places all down the ridge. Her father sat of an evening at the dooryard and talked to whoever came by about what cotton was doing at Corinth, which was a town thirteen miles north that had not existed when Mary Jane was a girl and now had rails running through it four ways and a name off a city on the other side of the world. Her mother put up the summer's peaches. Nancy and Mary Jane did the work of the house between them, two grown women in a house with no children in it, and the days came round and were done and came round.

And then, in the late summer, a man came up the ridge road with a book, and wrote her age down wrong.

The man with the book

His name was Porter Walker, and he was the assistant marshal for that district in the census of 1860, out of the post office down at Jacinto, and he was doing the government's counting that summer the way it was done every ten years — house to house, up one road and down the next, with a ledger under his arm and a set of ruled columns to fill and no particular hurry, because the counting of a county took a man the better part of a season and there was no use rushing it.

He was the second man with a book to come up that ridge that Mary Jane could remember. The first had come ten years back, when she was a girl of seventeen at the loom, and had written the household down and ridden on, and she had thought nothing of it, because at seventeen there is nothing to think. This one came when she was a woman of twenty-seven at the wash. The whole of what had happened to her between the one book and the other was the distance between those two numbers — which is to say that nothing had happened to her at all except the ten years themselves, and the ten years had done to a girl of seventeen the one thing a girl of seventeen never troubles to imagine the years will do, which is to make her twenty-seven while she is not looking.

Mary Jane saw him before he reached the dooryard. She was at the side of the house with the wash when his horse came up the ridge road at a walk, a stranger's horse at a stranger's pace, and she straightened up from the tub with her forearms wet to the elbow and a strand of hair come loose at her temple and watched him come the way you watch any rider you do not know come up your road — not alarmed, only attentive, filing him. A man in a town coat, which was strange for the ridge in August. A book under his arm. She knew before he had said a word what he was, because the talk of the counting had been going up and down the ridge for a fortnight ahead of him — the Vanderfords had been counted, and the Rineharts, and the count was working its way along — and she thought, drying her arms on her apron, it is the census man, he has got to us, and went in to tell her mother, and did not think anything further of it, because there was nothing further to think. The census man came every ten years. He wrote down the house. It was nothing.

She was wrong that it was nothing, but she had no way of knowing that, and neither did he.

He sat with her father at the dooryard, because that was how it was done — the head of the house gave the answers, and the marshal wrote them, and the women of the house went on with the day and were called on only when the head of the house wanted a thing confirmed. Mary Jane brought the two men water and went back to the wash and could hear, across the dooryard in the still hot air, the low back-and-forth of the counting: her father's voice, unhurried, giving the marshal what he asked for, and the marshal's pen going, and the small pauses while he wrote.

She heard him get her father's name wrong, and it was so ordinary a wrongness that she barely marked it. The marshal asked the name, and her father said it — Rorie — and the marshal wrote something, and it was not Rorie, because it never was; the name had come into Mississippi off a Carolina tongue and had been getting written down wrong by clerks and marshals her whole life, and her father had long since stopped correcting it, because a man does not spend his morning teaching a government clerk to spell. She did not hear what the marshal put down. She learned much later, when such things came to be looked at, that he had put down William Roria — not even the right given name, a William where a Reuben stood, so that her father, the man who had come off the Yadkin and built the mill and made the whole standing place they lived on, went into the book of 1860 under a name that was not his in either half. But she did not know that at the tub in the August heat. She knew only that the counting was going on at the dooryard the way the counting went on, and that her arms were wet, and that the wash still had to be wrung.

Then the marshal came to the ages.

She heard her father give his own, sixty, which was right. She heard him give her mother's, five-and-fifty, which was right — her mother was fifty-five that year, to the year, and it went into the book at fifty-five, plain and true. The two old people of that house went into the government's book at their right ages, because there was no reason on earth to give them anything else.

And then the marshal asked after the daughters, and there was a small pause, the kind of pause that is nothing at all if you are not the person the pause is about, and Mary Jane, at the tub, was the person the pause was about, and she felt it before she understood it, the way the body feels a thing a half-second before the mind will let it be named.

She did not hear which of them said it. It might have been her father. It might have been her mother, come to the dooryard by then with her own errand. It might even — and this was the thought she would turn over afterward and never settle — have been that no one decided it aloud at all, that it was simply the number the house had come to give when the house was asked, the way a house comes to give a certain answer to a certain question without ever holding a meeting about it. But the number that went across the dooryard in the hot still air, the number the marshal's pen took down for Mary Jane Rorie in the summer of 1860, was twenty-five.

She was twenty-seven. She was standing not thirty feet away with her arms wet to the elbow, and she was twenty-seven years old, and she heard the house say twenty-five, and she said nothing.

That was the thing she would come back to. Not the number. The nothing.

Because there was, in the half-second after she heard it, a whole small storm in her that no one at the dooryard could have seen — she went on wringing the shirt in her hands, her face did not change, her hands did not stop — and the storm was made of several things at once and she could not, then or later, have said which of them was strongest. There was the plain hot shame of it, first, the shame of being a woman whose age had to be managed, whose two years had to be quietly taken off and hidden the way you hide a stain by turning the cloth. There was, under the shame and stranger than it, something that was almost gratitude — because whoever had said twenty-five had said it to spare her, had reached out in the one way the moment allowed and made her, on the one page that would outlast every soul at that dooryard, two years less late than she was; and there is a particular ache in being loved that way, in having the people who love you best agree, without ever saying so, that the truth about you is a thing to be softened before it is handed to a stranger. And there was, under both of those, the plainest fact of all, which was that she could not correct it. A daughter did not correct her father to a marshal. A woman of twenty-seven did not step forward across her own dooryard and say to a man with a pen, no, sir, I am twenty-seven, write it down, because to do that was to say the number aloud, to a stranger, in front of her father, and there was not enough in her — there was not enough in anyone she had ever known — to do that. So she did the only thing there was to do, which was the nothing. She wrung the shirt. She let it stand.

And she heard, a moment later, the house do the same thing for Nancy — twenty-two, when Nancy was four-and-twenty — the same two years, off the same kind of daughter, in the same breath, and she understood that whatever had happened had happened to both of them, the two who stayed, shaved by the same hand by the same amount on the same afternoon, while the two old people who had made them were set down true. And she thought, wringing the wash, a thought she was a little ashamed of and could not help: at least I am not the only one. Nancy was three feet behind her at the line, pinning up a sheet, and Mary Jane did not turn to look at her, and Nancy did not turn to look at Mary Jane, and the two of them went on with the wash in the hot afternoon while the marshal at the dooryard finished the Rorie household and packed his book and got back on his horse and rode on up the ridge to count the next family, having set two small untruths into the permanent record of the United States and knowing nothing about it at all.

He had not made the wound. He had asked the question a marshal asks and written the answer a household gave, which is the whole of a marshal's office, and he rode off up the ridge with no more idea that he had softened a woman's age than the plow has when it comes down on the stone. That particular wound was not his; it had been in the house a long time before he came, and he had only, passing through, written it into a book. But it will not do to leave the man himself down as merely decent, and the book that means to tell the truth about that summer has to say why. Porter Walker held enslaved people — two of his own, ten years earlier, down in the southern half of the county — and all that same summer of 1860, on the long round that had brought him to the Rorie dooryard, he was filling the government's other book as he went, the one it headed for slaves; and before he was through with the county he would write onto it, against Reuben Rorie's name, the woman of sixty-five and the man of thirty-six and the girl of seven. He was not a clerk standing outside the thing he was counting. He owned in it, and he counted it, and he rode home at night to it. That belongs in the record beside the smaller, softer thing he did at the Rorie dooryard — not because the two are the same, for they are nothing like the same, but because a book that would tell you the man was decent has to tell you the rest of what he was, and let you hold both.

What the record could not soften

She thought about it that night, in the bed she shared with Nancy, after the candle was out and the house had gone quiet and there was nothing to do with her hands and nowhere for her mind to go but where it went.

She was not angry at her mother, if it had been her mother. She was not angry at her father, if it had been her father. She had worked out, lying there, that being angry at them was like being angry at them for the weather — they had not made the thing that made the number shameful; they had only, standing in it with her, done the small kindness the moment allowed, and taken two years off a daughter they could not otherwise help, and if the kindness stung it stung the way iodine stings, which is not the fault of the hand that pours it. She loved her mother. She knew, better than her mother thought she knew, exactly how much it had cost her mother across these years to have two grown daughters she could not settle — knew it from the particular way her mother had stopped, somewhere back around Mary Jane's twenty-fifth year, asking either of them any questions at all about who had been at the meeting or who had ridden by; because the questions had gone unanswered too many times and a mother learns to stop asking a question whose answer only hurts the person she is asking it of. Her mother had stopped asking. That had been its own kind of mercy and its own kind of grief, and Mary Jane had felt both of them and had never said so, and now her mother, or her father, had shaved two years off her for the government, which was the same mercy and the same grief in another form.

What she kept coming back to, in the dark, was the strangeness of the record itself — that a thing made of ink on paper, a thing that did not know her and did not care about her and would go into a courthouse drawer and be forgotten, had nonetheless just told a lie about her, and that the lie was the tenderest thing anyone could do for her, and that both of those were true at once. The last book had gotten her right, ten years back, and she understood now, lying in the dark, why it had and this one had not. Seventeen was a number nobody had needed to be careful with; a girl of seventeen was only a girl of seventeen, and there was no shame in the plain true number of her, and so the record had been let to tell the truth about her, because the truth cost nothing. Now that the truth cost something, the record had learned, like everyone else who loved her, to soften it.

She lay in the dark and did the sum she was always doing. Ten years. In ten years she had gone from a girl the record could tell the truth about to a woman the record had to lie about to be kind. Nothing had happened to her in the ten years. No wedding, no child, no death, no removal, nothing the world would name. Only the years, turning over her the way they turned over the ridge, green and then brown and then green, and carrying her from seventeen, which was nothing, to twenty-seven, which was a thing that had to be managed. She had not changed. The number had. And the number had become, without her ever agreeing to it, the truest and the most shameful thing about her, the thing her own house took off her two at a time and hid before it handed her to a stranger.

Beside her, Nancy's breathing had gone slow and even. Nancy could do that — go down into sleep out of anything, the way she had done since she was small. Mary Jane envied her it and did not begrudge her it. She lay a while longer with her hands folded on her chest the way her mother had taught her to fold them when she was a child, and she listened to the house, and after a while she thought a thing that she did not often let herself think, which was that she did not know what was going to happen to her. She was twenty-seven. The two roads a woman of that country could take had both, as far as she could see, closed behind her without her having taken either — she had not married the man of eighteen, because no man of eighteen had come, and she was past the age at which the men of eighteen came looking; and she had not resigned herself, either, to the other thing, the spinster-daughter thing, the aunt-in-the-corner thing, because she was only twenty-seven and it was too soon to lie down in that and she knew it. She was in between. She had been in between for years. She did not know how long a person could stay in between, or what was on the other side of it, or whether there was another side of it at all.

She did not know — she could not have known, no one lying in a bed in the summer of 1860 could have known — that there was in fact another side of it, and that it was seven years off, and that it would come to her by a road so plain and so unromantic that no girl of eighteen would have recognized it as a road at all. She did not know that this shaved number was only the first of the wrong ones, that the record having once learned to soften her would keep at it the rest of her life. She knew none of that. She knew only that it was late, and that she was twenty-seven, and that the marshal had ridden off up the ridge with the wrong number in his book, and that the wrong number was a kindness, and that she had let it stand. After a while she slept.

A door from her sister

There was a thing about that summer's counting that Mary Jane did not know and could not have known, and it belongs in this chapter even though she was blind to it, because the whole of what was going to happen to her was already lying on the same road she lived on, a few dozen doors up, waiting seven years to matter.

The marshal, on his long walk up the ridge and along the connecting roads, had already been to another house before he came to the Rories — had been there some weeks and some three dozen dwellings back, on the same continuous round through the same neighborhood — and had counted, in that other house, a man Mary Jane would one day marry. His name was Freeman Searcy. He was thirty years old that summer. He was a day-labourer, which is to say a man who worked other men's ground for a wage because he had none of his own, and the marshal had drawn the pen down the columns beside his name and left every one of them empty — no land, no property, nothing at all, a grown man of thirty who owned the strength of his back and not one foot of the earth he worked it on. He was living in his elder brother's house, a landless bachelor boarding among his own blood, and the marshal had written him down there among the Searcys with the blank columns beside him and moved on.

And the house the marshal had counted just before he counted the Searcys — the very next dwelling up the same page — was the household of Alexander Houston and his wife. And Alexander Houston's wife was Louisa Adeline Rorie. Mary Jane's own married sister.

That was the thing. That was the thing that undoes the easy telling of it. Freeman Searcy, the poor man Mary Jane Rorie would marry in the January of 1867, was not off in some far quarter of the county in the summer of 1860, a stranger her people had never met, waiting for the war to introduce him to her. He was living one door from her sister. He was a neighbor — a neighbor of the Rories the way half that end of the county was neighbors, the Houstons and the Rineharts and the Vanderfords and the Searcys and the Cheeks all set down the same roads in the same weeks of the same summer, one long neighborly cluster of families who had lived beside one another for twenty years and would go on living beside one another for fifty more. Whatever Mary Jane did or did not know of the particular poor man boarding in his brother's house a door past Louisa Adeline — and she likely knew him the way you know everyone in such a neighborhood, by sight and by name and by nothing more — the two of them were not moving in separate worlds that summer. They were moving in one world, a few dozen doors apart, and had been the whole time.

And they did not marry. That is the part worth sitting with, because it is harder and truer than the stranger's version would have been. It was not distance that kept Freeman Searcy and Mary Jane Rorie apart for seven more years. There was no distance to speak of. It was the same arithmetic that had shaved two years off her age at the dooryard — the plain, unarguable arithmetic the neighborhood ran on everybody, and the arithmetic said no. She was a woman of twenty-seven in a landed house, and by that reckoning she was too old for a young man laying out a place. He was a man of thirty with nothing, and by the same reckoning he was too poor to be offered any landed man's daughter. The two of them were, that summer, the two people on that whole stretch of road best placed by nearness to have found each other — and the neighborhood that had them living in each other's laps did not put them together, because putting them together was not what you did with a woman too old and a man too poor. You left them where they were. They were the two the sorting set aside, each of them exactly the kind of person the other one's world had finished with.

What the sorting could not know — what nothing in the summer of 1860 could know — was that the war was one year off, and that the war did not care for any of the sorting; that it was coming down the very roads the marshal was walking, toward the Rories and the Houstons and the Searcys and the Cheeks and every family in the cluster at once; and that when it had come through and taken what it took and leveled what it leveled, the arithmetic that had kept the too-old woman and the too-poor man apart would be so much changed that late and poor and past-it would stop being a bar between two people and become, instead, the simple common condition of everyone left standing. But that was seven years and a war away, and no one at the Rorie dooryard that August could see a foot of it.

Mary Jane could not see it. She finished the wash that afternoon and hung it and went in to help her mother with the supper, a woman of twenty-seven written down at twenty-five, in a prospering house on a green ridge, three dozen doors from the man she would marry and no nearer to him than she was to the war, and she did not know about either of them, and she went to bed that night not knowing, and got up the next morning not knowing, and the summer went on.

She was twenty-seven. Nothing had happened to her yet, in the way the world means the word. But the world was very nearly ready to happen to everyone.
