A Reader's Primer

The Russian Language

The Cyrillic alphabet, pronunciation, names, and just enough grammar to stop stumbling over the histories. Not a course — the minimum to read with confidence.

This is not a language course. It's the minimum needed to know that Хрущёв is "Khrush-CHÓV," why the same man is Trotsky, Trotskii, and Trockij in three different books, and why every Russian in a novel seems to have three names. Stress is marked here with an acute accent (á) for guidance; real Russian text never marks it.

Pronunciation — the five rules that matter

1. Stress is unmarked — and decisive

Russian has no rule for which syllable is stressed, and print gives no clue. Getting it wrong is the most common error: it's Mask-VÁ, Ne-VÁ, Ka-LÝ-ma, Sta-LÍN, Le-NÍN, Pu-TÍN, Do-sto-YÉV-sky, Ras-PÚ-tin.

2. Unstressed "о" becomes "ah" (akanye)

Written Москва is said "Mask-VÁ," not "Mosk-va"; хорошо ("good") is "kha-ra-SHÓ." It's why Russian sounds far less "o"-heavy than it looks.

3. Final consonants devoice

A final b sounds like p, v like f, d like t, g like k. So Смирнов is "Smir-NÓFF" — hence the vodka — and Романов ends in an f.

4. Soft consonants add a "y"

The soft sign (ь) and the vowels е, ё, и, ю, я palatalize the consonant before them. Нет ("no") is closer to "nyet" than "net" — exactly how English spells it.

5. "Г" can sound like "v"

In the genitive/adjective ending -ого / -его, г is pronounced v: его ("his") is "ye-VÓ." A quirk, but it explains some transliteration oddities.

Why "ё" hides

Printed Russian usually drops the dots on ё and writes plain е — which is why Хрущёв is spelled Khrushchev but said "Khrush-CHÓV," and Потёмкин is "Pa-TYOM-kin," not "Po-TEM-kin."

Names — the three-part system

Every Russian has a given name, a patronymic, and a surname: Иван Иванович Иванов — Ivan Ivanovich Ivanov. Three reasons a single character can look like three people:

The patronymic — "son/daughter of"

Built from the father's first name: -ович/-евич for a son, -овна/-евна for a daughter. Lenin was Vladimir Ilyich — son of Ilya; Stalin's daughter was Svetlana Iosifovna — daughter of Iosif.

Given name + patronymic together is the respectful form — the equivalent of "Mr." — which is why Tolstoy's characters are forever addressed by two names. Read it as deference, not fussiness.

Surnames take a gender ending

A man is Karenin, Romanov, Tolstoy; his wife or daughter is Karenina, Romanova, Tolstaya. Anna Karenina's husband is Alexei Karenin — not a different family. Likewise Akhmatova, Tsvetaeva, Krupskaya.

Diminutives run wild

One name yields many: Ivan → Vanya, Vanechka; Alexander → Sasha, Sanya, Shura; Maria → Masha, Manya, Mashenka; Dmitri → Mitya. When a name suddenly changes in a nineteenth-century novel, suspect a diminutive before a new character.

Why one name is spelled five ways

Three systems compete and books mix them. Popular (trade histories): Trotsky, Gorky, Yeltsin. Library of Congress (academic): Trotskii, Gor'kii, El'tsin. Scientific (linguistics): Trockij. And Ukrainian vs. Russian forms now carry weight: Kyiv vs. Kiev, Kharkiv vs. Kharkov, Lviv vs. Lvov.

Just enough grammar

No articles

Russian has no a or the; context supplies them. It's why translated Russian can feel bare — and why native speakers drop articles in English.

Six cases — so place names shift shape

Nouns change endings by role. Москва becomes Москвы ("of Moscow"), в Москве ("in Moscow"); Россия → России. The variant endings in quoted titles aren't inconsistency.

Three genders

Masculine, feminine, neuter — governing adjective and past-tense endings. Hence Правда ("Truth," feminine) and the gendered surnames above.

Aspect, not tense, is the hard part

Verbs come in pairs — imperfective (ongoing/repeated) and perfective (completed). English uses tense; Russian uses two different verbs. Largely invisible in translation, and the biggest hurdle for English speakers.

Word order is free

Because the case endings carry the grammar, order is free to carry emphasis instead — one reason Russian poetry resists translation, and why Eugene Onegin fights every English rendering.
