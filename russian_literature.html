Russian Culture · the achievement

The Literature

How a poor, censored, half-literate empire produced, in a single century, some of the greatest literature ever written — the canon, by era.

It is one of the strange facts of world culture. In 1800 Russia had barely a literature of its own; by 1880 it had given the world the greatest novels ever written, and it never stopped. What Russian writers brought was a particular moral seriousness — a refusal to look away from the "accursed questions" of God, death, guilt, and how to live — and a conviction that the writer is the conscience of the nation, a role that made literature dangerous and its authors, again and again, martyrs. This is a survey of the achievement, era by era. For the annotated list of what's on my own shelf, see The Reading.

The Golden Age — poetry & the founding

c. 1820–1840 · a national literature is born

Alexander Pushkin

1799–1837 · the fountainhead

The founder of modern Russian literature, who forged the literary language itself. Eugene Onegin (a novel in verse), The Bronze Horseman, Boris Godunov. Killed in a duel at 37 — the first of the martyrs.

Mikhail Lermontov

1814–1841 · the Romantic

A Hero of Our Time gave Russia its first great psychological novel and the "superfluous man." Also killed in a duel, at 26.

Nikolai Gogol

1809–1852 · the satirist

Dead Souls and The Overcoat — comedy, the grotesque, and pity for the little man. "We all came out of Gogol's Overcoat," runs the line often given to Dostoevsky.

The Age of the Great Novel

c. 1850–1890 · the realist novel redefines the form for the world

Leo Tolstoy

1828–1910 · the epic conscience

War and Peace and Anna Karenina — arguably the two greatest novels ever written — and a late turn to radical Christian moralism that made him a global prophet.

Fyodor Dostoevsky

1821–1881 · the psychologist of the soul

Crime and Punishment, The Brothers Karamazov (the "Grand Inquisitor"), The Idiot, Demons — faith, doubt, and free will, written from the far edge of human extremity.

Ivan Turgenev

1818–1883 · the European

Fathers and Sons gave "nihilism" its name and Russia its cleanest portrait of the generational rupture. The most Western-facing of the giants.

Anton Chekhov

1860–1904 · the modern

Reinvented two forms at once — the modern short story and the modern theatre (The Cherry Orchard, Three Sisters, Uncle Vanya) — trading plot for mood, subtext, and unbearable ordinariness.

The Silver Age — poetry returns

c. 1890–1925 · symbolism, modernism, and a doomed brilliance

Alexander Blok

1880–1921 · the Symbolist

The leading poet of the age; The Twelve set the Revolution to verse and left him disillusioned and broken by it.

Anna Akhmatova

1889–1966 · the witness

Requiem, composed in her head during the Terror because it was too dangerous to write down, is the enduring poem of the purge queues.

Osip Mandelstam

1891–1938 · the martyr

Arrested for a sixteen-line epigram mocking Stalin ("the Kremlin mountaineer"); died in a transit camp. Poetry as a capital offense.

Marina Tsvetaeva · Mayakovsky

1892–1941 · 1893–1930

Tsvetaeva, of ferocious lyric intensity, hanged herself after returning from exile; Mayakovsky, the megaphone of the Revolution, shot himself at 36. The age consumed its own.

The Soviet century & its dissenters

c. 1920–1990 · writing under, around, and against the state

Mikhail Bulgakov

1891–1940

The Master and Margarita — the devil visits atheist Moscow — written in secret and unpublished for decades. Now one of the most beloved novels in the language.

Yevgeny Zamyatin

1884–1937

We (1921), the first great dystopia — banned at home, and the seed from which Orwell's 1984 grew.

Boris Pasternak

1890–1960

Doctor Zhivago, smuggled abroad and awarded the Nobel — which the state forced him to refuse.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn

1918–2008

One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich and The Gulag Archipelago — the books that broke the silence and, arguably, the regime's moral standing.

Vladimir Nabokov

1899–1977 · the émigré

Carried Russian literature into English and wrote Lolita, Pale Fire, and Speak, Memory — the exile as master of two tongues.

Joseph Brodsky

1940–1996 · the last laureate

Tried for "social parasitism," exiled, and made poet laureate of the United States — the arc of the Russian poet in one life.

The five Nobel laureates in literature

  1. Ivan Bunin (1933) — the first, an émigré of the old school.
  2. Boris Pasternak (1958) — forced by the state to decline.
  3. Mikhail Sholokhov (1965) — And Quiet Flows the Don; the one the regime approved.
  4. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (1970) — could not travel to accept for fear of not being let back in.
  5. Joseph Brodsky (1987) — by then an exile in America.

Why it endures: Russian literature gave the world the novel of ideas at its deepest, and a model of the writer as moral witness that cost many of these names their freedom or their lives.
