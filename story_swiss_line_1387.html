Stories from the Record

The Swiss Line to 1387

Tracing the de Graffenried line to Peter, treasurer of the city of Bern — the deepest documented root of the whole family tree — and knowing exactly where the record stops.

Status of this finding: the root is Documented to 1387  ·  one generation deeper is Probable  ·  the medieval origin is held as Legend. Where the line stops is part of the finding.

The finding, in brief

The Needham line reaches back into Tudor England. The other old-world line in this family reaches further — and into a different world entirely. The de Graffenried were one of the patrician houses of Bern, seated in the city's governing council since 1352, and across four centuries they gave Bern four chief magistrates, eleven bannerets, and five city treasurers. Because Bern kept its civic records, that line can be followed, name by name, back to a man who held one of those five treasurerships: Peter von Graffenried, Seckelmeister — treasurer — of the city of Bern in 1387. He is the deepest documented root of the entire family tree, roughly twenty generations above the present. What follows is how far the record honestly reaches, and where — deliberately — it is made to stop.

Peter, treasurer of Bern, 1387

Peter von Graffenried sat on Bern's Great Council by 1376 and its inner council by 1385, and in 1387 he became the city's treasurer — the officer who kept Bern's money. He is not a name recovered from a family tradition; he is in the city's own books. Bern's tax and property registers of 1389 — the Udelbuch and Tellbuch, held today in the Bern State Archives — record him by name, and the family's earliest coat of arms survives on his seal from the early 1380s. From Peter the patriline is continuous, generation by generation, down through the Bernese centuries to the man who would cross the Atlantic.

One step deeper — and then legend

The honest work of a family history is not only how far back you can go, but knowing the tier of each step. Below Peter the ground changes three times:

Burkhard, the probable progenitor Probable

The modern scholarly history of the family reaches one generation below Peter to Burkhard von Grafenried, first visible in 1356 lending money at Fribourg to buy sheepskins, and again in the 1370s, who endowed soul-masses for himself, his wife, and his son Peter in a Bern anniversary book. He is well attested as a man — but the link from Burkhard down to our Peter is carried by the scholarship as "probably," and so the family carries it as Probable, not Documented.

A name in a 1272 charter Not lineage

A charter of 1272 names men called "von Grafenried," and the family's Bernese citizenship reaches back to around 1300. But a place-name attached to a person is not a proven ancestor. These are held as context, not as rungs on the ladder.

Descent "from 1191" Legend

An old family tradition traces the house to followers of the Dukes of Zähringen at the founding of Bern in 1191. There is no record for it. It is kept, openly, as legend — the Swiss counterpart of the invented English pedigrees this project has had to set aside elsewhere. The documented ceiling stays at the earliest charter-attested individual, and the story is told at that line, not beyond it.

The rise into the Bernese patriciate

Between Peter the treasurer and the man who sailed for Carolina, the line climbs into the front rank of Bern. The family's wealth was salt: Niklaus von Graffenried (c. 1468–1554), remembered as "the Monk," opened the salt springs of Panex, sat on Bern's councils, and served as a banneret — and his own hand survives in a household book he kept. After him the line runs through Peter (1507–1563); Anton (1545–1612); Abraham (1580–1620), who became co-lord of the estate of Worb by marriage and was killed at Tirano in the Valtellina in 1620; and Christoph (1603–1687), who bought out the other heirs and consolidated the lordship of Worb into the family seat it would remain. His son Anton (1639–1730) was the family's own genealogist.

New Bern — where the two lines meet

Anton's eldest son, Christoph von Graffenried (1661–1743), was schooled at Heidelberg, Leiden, and Cambridge, served as governor at Yverdon, and in October 1710 led a company of Swiss and Palatine settlers to found New Bern, North Carolina. By his own account the Carolina proprietors honored him as Landgrave of Carolina and Baron of Bernberg; he survived the Tuscarora war that devastated the colony in 1711, and he is buried at Worb. His eldest son, Christopher (1691–1742), had crossed to London with him in 1709, stayed in America, and married an Englishwoman — Barbara Tempest Needham, at Charleston in 1714. Their only son was named Tscharner, after his Bernese grandmother, and founded the American branch of the house.

The Bernese Worb line died out in Switzerland in 1869 — but it did not end. It continues in the United States. The American DeGraffenrieds are that line.

How the line can stand on 1387

The spine of this descent rests on the authoritative modern history of the family and on the two-thousand-page Bernese genealogy behind it, both drawing on the family's own archive in Bern — and, at the load-bearing points, on reproduced primary documents: the 1389 city tax-books that name Peter, the medieval seals that carry the first arms, the Monk's own household book, the anniversary book that names Burkhard. It rests, too, on something more physical. In 1983, when the choir floor of the church at Worb was lifted for restoration, the lordship burials beneath were found intact — among them the graves of the grandfather Christoph (1603–1687), the genealogist Anton (1639–1730), and Christoph the founder of New Bern himself, studied by a Bernese anthropologist. The line to Bern is not a hopeful chart. It is a paper trail with the bodies still at the bottom of it.

Key sources

  • Hans Braun, Notabeln – Patrizier – Bürger: Geschichte der Familie von Graffenried (Stämpfli Verlag, Bern, 2012) — the authoritative modern pedigree, drawing on the Bernese archive and reproducing the primary documents below.
  • Genealogie der Familie von / de Graffenried (Familienkiste, Bern, 2002) — the consolidated Bernese genealogy and its numbering.
  • Bern State Archives (Staatsarchiv Bern): the 1389 Udelbuch (B XIII 28) and Tellbuch naming Peter von Graffenried, treasurer 1387; the family's early seals (1380s); the St. Vincent anniversary book recording Burkhard. Family Archive von Graffenried, Burgerbibliothek Bern.
  • Worb parish church, 1983 excavation (Archäologischer Dienst des Kantons Bern; S. Ulrich-Bochsler) — recovered burials of Christoph (1603–1687), Anton (1639–1730), and Christoph the founder (1661–1743).
  • Christoph von Graffenried's own Relation of My American Project (c. 1716), in Todd & Goebel, eds., Christoph von Graffenried's Account of the Founding of New Bern (Raleigh, 1920) — the founder's titles and the Carolina venture in his own words.
