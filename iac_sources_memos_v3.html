Chapter 1 — The Man With the Book

The Man With the Book

Mary Jane Rorie, the census of 1850, and the household on the ridge — Tishomingo County, Mississippi — 31 October 1850

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Records the documentary source for every load-bearing factual claim in the finished Chapter 1, and names every passage of authorial reconstruction (scene, dialogue, interior, sensory detail) that goes beyond the documented record. The reader-facing Note on Sources will be drawn from this at assembly stage. This is the first of the Volume III chapter memos; it sets the format the rest of the volume follows.

Source-credibility key. P = primary source (a document of the period, contemporary or near-contemporary: census schedule, deed, will, vital record, the family Bible register). S = secondary source (compilation or analysis at one remove). R = reference work (period, place, or practice). T = family tradition, oral or written. AR = authorial reconstruction (invented scene, dialogue, interior state, or sensory detail placed on a documented frame). The governing discipline: genealogy first; do not invent to fill a silence; where the record stops, the prose says so.

The whole chapter stands on a single, strong documentary spine — the 1850 U.S. Census, Tishomingo County, 4th District, Northern Division, assistant marshal Wm. Scruggs, enumerated 31 October 1850 (P) — and everything domestic, interior, and dramatic is reconstruction built on that spine and on the family Bible record. The chapter's controlling idea (that this is the one census that recorded Mary Jane's age truthfully) is documented; the morning it is set on is reconstructed.

§Seventeen — the loom and the three women

Documented.

  • Mary Jane Rorie was born 21 November 1832, in Stewart County, Tennessee, daughter of Reuben Rorie and Mary Polly Hubbard Rorie — P (Rorie family Bible register; corroborated by the Bible/POA line-confirmation memo, 21 July 2026). The Bible enters her under the unsettled name "Milbry or Mity Jane."

  • She was the seventh of Mary Polly's children — P/T (Bible register; workbook reconciliation).

  • In 1850 she was seventeen, three weeks short of eighteen, and the census recorded her age as 17 — P (1850 Census, dwelling 751). This is the chapter's load-bearing fact and it is exact.

  • Her mother, Mary Polly Hubbard Rorie, was about forty-six that autumn but was recorded by the marshal at forty, six years short — P (1850 Census, dwelling 751; the age-46 reconstruction rests on Mary Polly's own documented birth, Hubbard Bible, ~1804, making her ~46 in 1850, against the recorded 40).

  • A daughter Nancy, fifteen, was in the household — P (1850 Census).

AR — authorial reconstruction.

  • The entire domestic tableau — the loom in the front room, the shuttle thrown and caught, Mary Polly at the churn, the specific "last day of October" morning — is reconstruction. The census establishes who was in dwelling 751; it does not describe a room, a task, or a morning. The date "last day of October" borrows the census's own enumeration date (31 October 1850) and stages the scene on it; the enumeration date is documented, the morning-scene is invented.

  • Mary Jane's interior — that she liked the loom, liked "the count of it," had "her mother's way of doing a thing all the way through" — is AR, grounded in nothing documentary. It is characterization, and it is invented.

  • The grandmother, "Sarah," in the corner bed is the chapter's most important reconstruction to flag. Sarah as a person is documented: she is Sarah Johnson Hubbard, Mary Polly's mother, daughter of Gideon Johnson and Mary Baker deGraffenreid, b. ~1780 — P/S (Hubbard family Bible; Chadwell Bible page images; Bible/POA confirmation memo). Her presence in Reuben Rorie's household on this morning is NOT documented: the 1850 Census does not enumerate a woman of her age in dwelling 751. Her being resident in the corner of the front room in 1850, her age given as "sixty-eight," and her having "come down from Tennessee three years before, in 1847" are AR. (Her three marriages and early Hubbard widowhood are documented in outline — William Hubbard d. 1813 — but her living in this house at this date is reconstruction. See Open Items: this should be verified against the full 1850 census image before it hardens, and it has a direct knock-on to the Volume III Bridge, which places the same Sarah in the same corner.)

  • All dialogue in the section — the exchange about keeping the loom-count ("You keep the count better than your mother did at your age" / "Threw the pattern out three rows running one winter at Stewart") — is AR. No words of any of these women survive.

  • The closing turn ("the record was coming up the road… was going to get her right") is authorial framing — true in its facts (the census did record her correctly) but a narrator's construction.

§The house on the ridge — the rising family

Documented.

  • Reuben Rorie was 51, a millwright, born in North Carolina — P (1850 Census, dwelling 751).

  • The value of the place was $2,000 in real estate — P (1850 Census). The chapter's claim that this was "a great deal in that country / a front-of-the-meeting-house figure" is R/AR (period context plus authorial gloss), reasonable but interpretive.

  • Louisa Adeline (second daughter) married Alexander Houston (1838) and was enumerated in the adjacent dwelling (752) — P (Rorie Bible marriage entry; 1850 Census, next-door dwelling).

  • Wm. H. Rorie, 21, recorded as "Student," was in dwelling 754 — P (1850 Census).

  • The Rorie family Bible was a real object, kept by Mary Polly, its register in her hand, carrying the births and the two elder daughters' marriages (Elizabeth 1834, Louisa Adeline 1838) — P (Rorie Bible; transcriptions in workbook). Photographic inspection of the surviving leaves is an open item.

AR / tradition.

  • Reuben's migration path — "off the Yadkin country in North Carolina… up into Stewart County in Tennessee, and down at last into Mississippi in the removal of the middle forties" — is T/AR. The NC birth is documented (census); Stewart Co. TN residence is documented (the family is there through the 1820s–30s, per the Bible and the Hubbard/Rorie POA record); the specific "Yadkin country" origin and the characterization of him as a self-made "standing man" who built "deliberately, all the way through" are tradition and authorial characterization.

  • The removal "in the middle forties" / Mary Jane coming "down into Mississippi as a girl of fourteen" (~1846) is AR by inference — sound (TN birth 1832 + MS residence by 1850), but the specific year and age-at-removal are reconstructed, not documented.

  • The mill "turning and the money coming" as of 1850 is AR built on the documented facts of the trade (millwright) and the estate value ($2,000).

  • The account of the household's shifting composition (grown children gone, the loft "not yet emptied," the table "still long with children") is AR on the documented census roster.

  • The Bible-keeping passage — Mary Polly taking the book down "on the mornings of consequential days," having "practiced [the hand] for a year before she had had to use it, so that when the day came the wrist would not shake," the keeping running "back up a line of women that ran out of sight" — is AR on a documented object. The book, the hand, and the "not getting anyone's name quite plainly" (the "Milbry or Mity Jane" entry) are documented; the practicing-for-a-year detail and the lineage-of-women framing are invented.

  • The controlling claim that "everyone in that house believed her consequential day was near" and that the book "spends seventy years disproving" it is authorial thesis — AR in the strict sense (no one recorded anyone's expectations), though it is the true shape of the documented life that follows.

§Scruggs — the census-taker at the table

Documented.

  • The assistant marshal was Wm. Scruggs, for the 4th District, Northern Division, Tishomingo County; the household was enumerated 31 October 1850 — P (1850 Census schedule, marshal's certification).

  • The surname was recorded as a spelling of "Rora," not "Rorie" — P (1850 Census; the documented name-drift).

  • Reuben's trade (millwright), the estate value ($2,000), and the household's ages, including Mary Jane at 17, went into the schedule that day — P (1850 Census).

  • The thread that later censuses would get her progressively wrong is documented: 1860 records her as 25 against her true 27 (marshal Porter Walker), and the pattern continues — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1542, and later schedules). The chapter deploys these as foreshadowing; the underlying facts are documented.

AR.

  • The staging of the census visit — Scruggs arriving "a little before noon," Reuben coming "in from the mill-lot," the two men sitting at the front-room table, Mary Jane standing "a little off, by the cold loom," half-listening, Mary Polly giving the marshal "a cup of something warm at the door" — is AR. The census fixes the fact and date of enumeration and the data recorded; the scene of it is reconstructed.

  • Reuben "not troubling to correct" the misspelled name, and the reasoning attributed to him ("you did not spend the marshal's time… teaching a government man to spell a name he would never say again") is AR.

  • The entire meditation on the record telling the truth "the one clean true thing the record would ever set down about her that she would live to see" is authorial framing on documented data (the 1850 accuracy vs. the later errors are real; the meaning laid over them is the narrator's).

§Nine doors — Freeman Searcy on the same page

Documented.

  • Nine dwellings on from the Rories (dwelling 760), Scruggs enumerated the household of Aaron Searcy, 53, farmer, born North Carolina; his wife Martha; and among the children a son Freeman, 20; real estate read at $400 — P (1850 Census; the $400 figure author-certified at the image). The "nine doors" is literal: dwelling 751 to dwelling 760.

  • Mary Jane (Rorie) and Freeman Searcy married seventeen years later, on 10 January 1867, she 34 and he ~36 — P (Mississippi marriage record, F. J. Searcy and "Miss M. J. Rorie"). The chapter's forward-glance to that marriage is documented.

AR.

  • That the two households "did not know the other was on the page," that Mary Jane and Freeman "had very likely seen each other at the meeting-house or the crossroads store… by sight and by name and by nothing more," and that "there was nothing between them" — is AR by inference. Reasonable (they were near neighbors of an age) but undocumented as to any specific acquaintance or its absence.

  • The refrain "Nothing happened for seventeen years" and the framing of the 1850 page as "the pole this book opens on" are authorial structure — the seventeen-year gap between the 1850 record and the 1867 marriage is documented; the meaning is the narrator's.

Gaps and open items

1. Sarah in the 1850 household (load-bearing AR). The grandmother's presence in dwelling 751 in 1850 is reconstruction, not census fact; the 1850 schedule does not enumerate a woman of her age there. Verify against the full 1850 census image (all household lines) whether any elderly female was recorded in the Rorie household. This directly governs the Volume III Bridge, which places the same Sarah, by name, in the same corner bed — the two renderings must carry the same documented/reconstructed status. If the census places her there, both harden to documented; if not, both remain AR and should read as such.

2. Rorie Bible photographic inspection (open). The register readings (including the "Milbry or Mity Jane" birth entry and the elder daughters' marriages) rest on transcriptions held in the workbook; a photographic inspection of the surviving leaves is outstanding and would raise several §Seventeen / §house-on-the-ridge items from P (transcribed) to P (image-verified).

3. Reuben's Yadkin/North Carolina origin (T). Documented as NC-born (census); the "Yadkin country" specificity is tradition and would benefit from a NC record tie.

4. Removal year (~1846) and Mary Jane's age at removal (14). Inference from the documented TN-birth / MS-1850-residence bracket; not independently documented.

5. Nancy's later life. Nancy (15 in 1850) — whether the second unmarried daughter ever married is an open workbook question, noted here because she is a named presence in the chapter.

End of Chapter 1 Sources Memo v1. The load-bearing distinction this chapter establishes for the whole volume: the census schedules are the documented spine; the domestic scenes, interiors, and all dialogue are reconstruction on that spine and are marked as such.

Chapter 2 — The Town That Came Out of the Woods

The Town That Came Out of the Woods

Corinth rising, slavery arriving on the Rorie place, the decade of marriages and given-back children, and the death of Grandmother Sarah — Tishomingo County, Mississippi — c. 1852 to 1860

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Documented facts first, section by section; every reconstructed passage flagged AR; open items at the end. Tier key as established in the Chapter 1 memo: P primary · S secondary · R reference/place · T family tradition · AR authorial reconstruction. This chapter carries the volume's first morally load-bearing material — the arrival of three enslaved people on the Rorie place — and the memo gives that section (§The house in the yard) the closest reading.

The chapter's documentary spine is the two federal enumerations of 1850 and 1860 read side by side — the free schedules and, crucially, the two slave schedules (1850 certified negative; 1860 three persons and one slave house). Everything the chapter builds — the passage of the decade, the marriages, the laying-out — hangs on that bracket. The controlling factual claim (that slavery arrived on the place across the 1850s, in front of a grown Mary Jane) is documented by the two schedules; the domestic and interior life around it is reconstruction.

§The wheel that went nowhere — Corinth rises, the decade opens

Documented.

  • A town rose in the Tishomingo pinewoods, roughly thirteen miles north of the Rorie ridge, where two railroads crossed — the north–south line laid "the length of the state" (Mobile & Ohio) and the east–west line "clean across the top of it" (Memphis & Charleston) — in the middle 1850s; it was first called Cross City, then renamed Corinth for the crossing (after the Greek isthmus-city) — R (standard Alcorn/Tishomingo County place-history; railroad and town-founding record). The rough chronology (surveys c. 1852, town rising across Mary Jane's early-to-mid twenties) is consistent with Corinth's documented founding (incorporated 1856).

  • Mary Jane went from twenty to twenty-four across these years — P (her documented 1832 birth; 1852–1856).

AR.

  • The entire "wheel that went nowhere" meditation — the mill wheel as the emblem of a daughter "in motion… and going nowhere," the thought she "never put into words and was never asked for" — is authorial reconstruction. The mill itself is documented (Reuben's trade, the 1850 estate); the metaphor and the interior are invented. Flagged as the chapter's governing image and wholly the narrator's.

  • Mary Jane hearing the town's news "late, and secondhand, and at the supper table," "with half an ear," is AR characterization.

  • The Corinth/Mary-Jane naming parallel (a town "named twice before it was even finished" beside "a girl down the road who could not reliably get one") is authorial framing; the Corinth naming is documented (R), the "Milbry or Mity Jane" Bible entry is documented (P, Rorie Bible), the likeness drawn between them is AR.

§The house in the yard — slavery arrives (the load-bearing section)

Documented — and this is the section's spine, held tightly.

  • 1850: Reuben Rorie held no one. The 1850 Slave Schedule for Tishomingo County carries no entry against Reuben Rorie (or any Rorie/Searcy) — a certified negative, both divisions read at image level — P (1850 Slave Schedule, FamilySearch IGN 004200553; certified by the same marshal, Scruggs, who wrote Reuben into the free schedule). This is the strongest class of negative: the pages were read, not merely the index.

  • 1860: three enslaved people and one slave house. The 1860 Slave Schedule, enumerated 22 September 1860 by Porter Walker, enters against "Reubin Roria" (page 42) a woman aged 65, a man aged 36, and a girl aged 7, and in the house-count column, one slave house — P (1860 Slave Schedule, FamilySearch IGN 005170183, image 386; author-certified at zoom, the man's age corrected from a first-pass 30 to 36).

  • The consequence, which is the chapter's claim: the three were not there in 1850 and were there in 1860, so they arrived across the 1850s — the same decade Mary Jane passed from seventeen to twenty-seven in that household. This is documented inference from the two schedules, and it is sound.

  • The three's approximate birth years — the woman c. 1795 ("around the turn of the old century"), the man c. 1824, the girl c. 1853 — are arithmetic from the 1860 ages — P.

AR — and the discipline is held, which the memo affirms.

  • That Mary Jane "watched it built… watched the three come onto the place," "was there for the whole of it, grown, and watching," is AR by documented inference — she was demonstrably resident across the decade (free schedules), and the slave house and the three demonstrably appeared within it; her witnessing is reconstruction laid on that documented bracket. It does not claim a feeling.

  • The prose explicitly refuses to invent past the record, and does not. It names only what the schedules give — three persons, their ages and sex, one house — and states the refusal outright: "the record does not say, and this book will not put into her a feeling she left no trace of"; the three's names, origins, and manner of acquisition are named as unknown and left unguessed ("guessing is the one thing the discipline of this book forbids most where these three are concerned"). This is the "not her metaphor" discipline operating correctly at the point of highest risk. No AR violation here: the section adds no invented fact about the three and assigns Mary Jane no documented-absent feeling.

  • Light AR characterization on documented ages — "old already by the time she came," "in his prime" — is interpretive gloss on the schedule's 65 and 36; harmless, and flagged.

  • The deferral of the three's full accounting to a later chapter (Ch 5) is a structural choice, correct: the 1870 trace and the emancipation-era record belong there.

§Everyone's beginnings — the marriages and the given-back children

Documented.

  • Ezekiah married across the decade and built toward a farm of roughly $2,000 with a young family — P/inference (1860 Census household; the ~$2,000 farm is documented in his 1865 estate appraisement, Vol III bibliography §VI).

  • William H. Rorie (the "Student" of 1850) married and became a farmer with his own household — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1543).

  • Louisa Adeline had married Alexander Houston at fifteen (1838) and lived one door up — P (Rorie Bible; census).

  • Nancy Ann Rorie, about four years younger than Mary Jane, remained unmarried — P (census ages).

  • The line's fast marriage clock — mother married at ~fifteen/sixteen, Louisa at fifteen — is P/inference (Mary Polly b. ~1804, m. Reuben 1820; Louisa 1838).

AR.

  • The whole "given-back children" motif — Mary Jane holding the nieces and nephews, "the particular ache in the forearm," feeling it "most… in the giving-back" — is authorial reconstruction. That she was the unmarried daughter present through a decade of family births is a fair inference from the documented household; the ache, the interior, and the scenes are invented.

  • The reading of "two daughters kept at home" as "a household arranged around something" — a father who liked his girls near, a place that wanted their hands — is offered by the prose as explicitly unknown ("Mary Jane never knew which, and it was likely no single one") and is AR framing, correctly hedged.

  • The "not-saying settling over the house" and the thickening silence around Mary Jane's unmarried state are AR — true to the documented outline (she did not marry until 1867) but undocumented as household mood.

§What she learned over the body — the death of Grandmother Sarah

Documented.

  • Sarah (Johnson Hubbard), Mary Polly's mother, was a real person — P/S (Hubbard Bible; Chadwell Bible; Bible/POA memo), born c. 1780, widowed of William Hubbard (d. 1813) and twice more.

  • Aaron Searcy died within the decade (present 1850, gone by 1860) — P by absence (1850 Census, dwelling 760; absent 1860, when son Freeman is boarding in his brother William B. Searcy's house, dwelling 1505). The prose correctly declines to fix the day.

  • Freeman Searcy after his father's death: landless, a hired hand, boarding in his brother's house, 30, "day-labourer, no estate" — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1505).

AR / tradition / open.

  • Grandmother Sarah's death "in the fall of 1857," in the corner bed of the Rorie house, washed and dressed by Mary Jane is AR, and it inherits the Chapter 1 open item: Sarah's presence in the Rorie household is not established by the 1850 census, so her dying there in 1857 and the specific season/year are reconstruction, not documented. No documented death date for Sarah is in hand; the 1857 dating is authorial. (See Open Items — this must be reconciled with the Ch 1 Sarah flag and the Bridge; all three renderings of Sarah's residence stand or fall together.)

  • The laying-out scene — Mary Jane washing and dressing the body, "the first apprenticeship of a long calling" — is AR, and it is explicit foreshadow of the documented Chapter 9 close (Mary Jane's own 1920 death, the family laying-out, the homemade coffin). The office itself (women laying out their own dead before an undertaker's trade existed) is R period practice; Mary Jane's performing it here is reconstruction.

  • Aaron Searcy as a soldier "in a war fought before Mississippi was a place" (i.e., the War of 1812) is T, and contested — this is the project's open "Aaron #32 / War of 1812" question. The prose hedges it correctly and heavily ("they said," "she could not have told you the war or the year," "a rumor of a life"), assigning it to vague neighborhood tradition rather than asserting it. Correct handling of an unresolved item.

Gaps and open items

1. Sarah's residence and death (carries from Ch 1; governs the Bridge). Sarah's presence in the Rorie household — and therefore her death there in "the fall of 1857" — is reconstruction, not documented. No death date for Sarah is in hand. Resolve against the full 1850 census image and any Hubbard/Johnson death record; whatever is found must be applied consistently to Ch 1 §Seventeen, this chapter, and the Volume III Bridge.

2. The three enslaved people (deferred, not gap). Their names, origins, and manner of acquisition are unknown and deliberately unguessed; the full documented trace (1862 close of the holding, the Corinth contraband camp, the 1870 search) is carried to Chapter 5, where the accounting is owed. Noted here so the deferral is on the record, not an omission.

3. Aaron Searcy's War-of-1812 service (T, contested). The "Aaron #32" identity/service question is open; the chapter treats it as tradition only. A NARA War-of-1812 service/pension tie would resolve it.

4. Ezekiah's and William's exact marriage dates. Their marriages across the decade rest on the 1860 census households and the estate record rather than on located marriage records; marriage records would raise these from inference to P.

5. Corinth founding specifics. The town chronology is R-level place-history; exact survey/incorporation dates (and the precise year the Rorie household would first have heard of it) are not pinned to a cited instrument here.

Chapter 3 — Twenty-Five

Twenty-Five

The census of 1860, the shaved age, the money that had people in it, and the neighbor she would marry — Tishomingo County, Mississippi — summer 1860

Sources memo v1 — audit trail, not for publication. Documented first, section by section; AR flagged; open items at the end. Tier key per the Chapter 1 memo (P / S / R / T / AR).

The chapter's documentary spine is the 1860 U.S. Census and the 1860 Slave Schedule, Tishomingo County, marshal Porter Walker (P), read together, plus the 1860 free-schedule neighbor cluster. The single documented datum the whole chapter turns on is small and exact: the census recorded Mary Jane's age as 25 when she was 27. Everything else in the chapter — the shame, the kindness, the night in the dark, the reading of the number as a loving softening — is reconstruction built on that one recorded figure. The memo's chief task is to hold that line: the number is documented; its meaning is the book's.

§The daughter who stayed — the prospering house and its one trouble

Documented.

  • By 1860 Mary Jane was 27 (b. 21 Nov 1832) and unmarried, in Reuben Rorie's household — P (Rorie Bible; 1860 Census).

  • Reuben was about 60 and worth roughly $5,000 — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1542; the ~$5,000 personal/real worth).

  • Part of that $5,000 was the appraised value of three enslaved people — a woman of 65, a man of 36, a girl of 7, held on the place with one slave house — P (1860 Slave Schedule against "Reubin Roria," enumerated 22 Sept 1860 by Walker). The chapter names the figure and states that it "has people in it," refusing to let "$5,000" read as mill money alone. The discipline is held: the three are named only as the schedule gives them (age, sex, one house), their accounting explicitly deferred to their own chapters, and explicitly "not Mary Jane's to carry."

  • Nancy Ann, unmarried, about four years younger — P (1860 Census).

  • William H. Rorie married with his own household nearby — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1543).

  • Louisa Adeline (m. Alexander Houston, 1838) settled a little off with children — P (Rorie Bible; 1860 Census).

AR.

  • The controlling image — the waiting that "had stopped feeling like waiting and started feeling like the weather," the number as "a stone under a field" — is authorial reconstruction. The documented fact is only her unmarried state at 27; the interior is invented.

  • Reuben's "gray at the temples and slow at the one knee a horse had ruined in his forties" — the age (60) is P; the ruined knee and the physical detail are AR.

  • The full analysis of "past the age" — why a woman of 27 "stood a little to the side of where the looking happened" — is AR/R: sound period-marriage-market context (R) rendered as her worked-out private understanding (AR).

§The man with the book — the census-taker and the wrong number

Documented.

  • The 1860 assistant marshal was Porter Walker, out of the Jacinto post office, enumerating that district in the late summer — P (1860 Census schedule).

  • He recorded the household head as "William Roria," 60 — wrong in both name-halves for Reuben Rorie — P (1860 Census, dwelling 1542; the documented mis-recording).

  • He recorded Mary Polly at 55 (correct) and Reuben at 60 (correct), and Mary Jane at 25 against her true 27, and Nancy at 22 against ~24 — both unmarried daughters shaved two years while both parents stand true — P (1860 Census).

  • Porter Walker was himself a slaveholder — he held two enslaved people in 1850 (Southern Division) — and across the same 1860 round he enumerated the slave schedule, including the three against Reuben Rorie's name — P (1850 Slave Schedule, image 31; 1860 Slave Schedule). The chapter sets this beside the "softer thing he did at the dooryard" and asks the reader to "hold both." This is documented fact deployed for honesty, correctly.

AR — the chapter's central reconstruction.

  • That the "twenty-five" was a deliberate, loving softening by the household to spare her is the chapter's thesis and it is AR. What is documented is the number 25 against the true 27. That it was said to spare her, that it carried "the tenderest thing anyone could do for her," that it was a kindness that "stung" — all of this is reconstruction of motive from a bare recorded figure. The prose is scrupulous about the mechanism's uncertainty — "She did not hear which of them said it. It might have been her father… It might even… have been that no one decided it aloud at all" — which correctly marks the interpretation as unprovable. Legitimate reconstruction on a documented datum; flagged here as such.

  • The dooryard staging (Walker at the door, Mary Jane at the wash "not thirty feet away," the pause, the "whole small storm") is AR. The enumeration is documented; the scene of it is invented.

  • The parallel drawn to Chapter 1 — that 1850 got her right because "the truth cost nothing" and 1860 softened her because "the truth cost something" — is authorial framing on two documented data points (17-true in 1850; 25-for-27 in 1860). The data are P; the meaning is AR.

§What the record could not soften — the night after

Documented.

  • Nothing in this section is independently documented beyond the already-established facts (her age, the shaved number, her mother's and father's ages). It is the chapter's interior movement.

AR.

  • The entire section is authorial reconstruction — the night in the shared bed, the analysis of her mother having "stopped asking… somewhere back around Mary Jane's twenty-fifth year," the meditation on the record learning "to soften" the truth once it "cost something," the sum "she was always doing." None of it is documented; all of it is the book's interior of a woman about whom the record says only that she was recorded at 25. It is the chapter's emotional heart and it is invention on a documented frame. Flagged wholly AR. (It contains no invented fact — no event, date, or person the record denies — which is the line that keeps it disciplined: it reconstructs an inner life, not an outer record.)

  • The forward glances — that the shaved number "was only the first of the wrong ones," that "another side" was "seven years off" — are AR framing on documented facts (the later censuses do continue to misrecord her; the 1867 marriage is documented).

§A door from her sister — Freeman in the same neighborhood

Documented.

  • In 1860 Freeman Searcy, 30, day-labourer, no property, was boarding in his elder brother William B. Searcy's house (dwelling 1505) — P (1860 Census).

  • The immediately preceding dwelling (1504) was Alexander Houston and Louisa Adeline Rorie, Mary Jane's married sister — so Freeman was enumerated one door past Mary Jane's own sister, on the same continuous page — P (1860 Census).

  • The families formed one long neighbor cluster (Houstons, Rineharts, Vanderfords, Searcys, Cheeks) set down the same roads — P (1860 Census sequence; the project's Neighbor Cluster Ledger).

  • Mary Jane (Rorie) and Freeman Searcy married 10 January 1867, she 34 and he ~36 — P (Mississippi marriage record, "Miss M. J. Rorie").

AR.

  • The chapter's reading of why they did not marry in 1860 — "the same arithmetic that had shaved two years off her age," she too old and he too poor by the neighborhood's reckoning — is AR/R: a sound period-social inference (R) rendered as the operative cause (AR). Documented is the proximity and the fact that they did not marry until 1867; the reason is reconstruction.
