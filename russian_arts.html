Russian Culture · the achievement

The Arts

Ballet, canvas, and screen — how Russia reshaped how the world dances, paints, and makes films.

Literature and music are the famous glories, but Russia's reach ran further — into three arts it did not just excel at but reinvented. It gave modern ballet its greatest company and then handed it to the West; it produced, in a single generation, some of the boldest abstract painting ever made; and it laid down the grammar of film itself. Each of these, too, ran headlong into the state — and each left the country in the luggage of exiles who then remade art abroad.

Ballet — and the Ballets Russes

from the Imperial stage to the conquest of Paris

The Imperial tradition

St. Petersburg · 19th century

At the Mariinsky, the French-born ballet master Marius Petipa built the classical repertoire — The Sleeping Beauty, The Nutcracker, the definitive Swan Lake — the foundation everything later stood on.

Diaghilev's Ballets Russes

Paris · 1909–1929

Sergei Diaghilev's company took Paris by storm and became the crucible of modern art — fusing dance, music (Stravinsky), and design into one shock after another. The dancers Nijinsky and Anna Pavlova became the first global stars of ballet.

Balanchine & the exiles

the 20th century abroad

George Balanchine carried the Russian school to America and founded New York City Ballet, shaping ballet worldwide. Later, the defections of Nureyev (1961) and Baryshnikov (1974) became Cold-War sensations.

Visual art — from the icon to the Black Square

the sacred, the social, and the radically abstract

Andrei Rublev & the icon

c. 1360–1430 · the sacred image

The icon was Russia's first great visual art — and Rublev its supreme master. His Trinity is the summit of a tradition of luminous, otherworldly religious painting stretching back to Byzantium.

The Wanderers (Peredvizhniki)

1870s–90s · realist conscience

Painters who left the academy to show Russia as it was. Ilya Repin is the giant — Barge Haulers on the Volga, the harrowing Ivan the Terrible and His Son — social realism a generation before the Soviets claimed the phrase.

The avant-garde

c. 1910–1930 · the explosion

One of the boldest moments in all of art. Wassily Kandinsky helped invent abstract painting; Kazimir Malevich pushed it to the edge with Suprematism and the Black Square; Vladimir Tatlin's Constructivism and his unbuilt Tower proposed art as engineering. Marc Chagall carried the dream-world of the shtetl into modernism.

The clampdown

1932 onward · Socialist Realism

The avant-garde's freedom died fast. From 1932 the state imposed Socialist Realism — heroic workers and beaming leaders — as the only permitted style, and the radicals were silenced, exiled, or erased.

Film — the grammar of cinema

the 1920s and after · inventing the language

Sergei Eisenstein

1898–1948 · the theorist

The father of film montage. Battleship Potemkin (1925) and its Odessa Steps sequence are studied in every film school on earth; later came Alexander Nevsky and Ivan the Terrible, scored by Prokofiev — the second part banned by Stalin.

Dziga Vertov

1896–1954 · the experimenter

Man with a Movie Camera (1929) is a dazzling manifesto of what the camera alone can do — regularly voted among the greatest films ever made.

Andrei Tarkovsky

1932–1986 · the poet

The master of the art film — Andrei Rublev, Solaris, Mirror, Stalker — slow, luminous, and spiritual. He left the USSR in his final years, one more artist the state could not hold.

The pattern worth noticing

In all three arts the same shape repeats: a burst of Russian genius, a collision with the state, and then a diaspora that seeds the art abroad — Diaghilev and Balanchine to Paris and New York, Kandinsky and Chagall to the West, Tarkovsky into exile. Russia's cultural gift to the twentieth century was delivered as much by those who fled it as by those who stayed.

Why it endures: modern ballet, abstract art, and the language of film all carry a Russian signature.
