Stalin and Ivan the Terrible

The mirror the dictator chose.

Most historical comparisons are imposed on the dead by the living. This one is different. Joseph Stalin reached back four centuries and chose Ivan the Terrible as his own reflection — deliberately, publicly, and in revealing detail. If you want to understand how Stalin understood his own terror, the most useful document isn't a confession or a diary. It's what he admired in a sixteenth-century tsar.

The tell: Stalin, Eisenstein, and the film he banned

In the 1940s Stalin personally sponsored a rehabilitation of Ivan IV, recasting Russia's most notorious tsar as a farsighted, progressive state-builder who grasped a hard necessity: that a great nation cannot be unified without crushing its internal enemies. The centerpiece of that campaign was Sergei Eisenstein's two-part film Ivan the Terrible.

Part I (1944) pleased Stalin and won a Stalin Prize. Part II did not. Eisenstein had made the mistake of showing an Ivan who doubted, who was tormented by conscience, whose personal guard — the oprichnina — looked less like a heroic vanguard and more like a fanatical death cult. Stalin banned it. It would not be seen until 1958, five years after his death.

Then came the part that historians keep returning to. In a 1947 meeting with Eisenstein and the lead actor Nikolai Cherkasov, Stalin explained what the director had gotten wrong. Ivan, he said, was a great and necessary ruler — but he had made mistakes. His error was not that he killed, but that he stopped killing: he failed to wipe out the handful of great boyar families completely, and had he finished them, there would have been no Time of Troubles. Worse, Ivan wasted his energy in repentance — praying over his victims when he should have been eliminating the next ones. In Stalin's telling, God "got in Ivan's way."

Read that again. The most powerful man in the world, at the height of his own terror, critiqued a long-dead tsar for insufficient ruthlessness and excessive remorse. What Stalin denied about himself is a matter of endless argument. What he admired in Ivan is on the record — and it tells you more.

Where the mirror is true

The strongest parallel is institutional. The oprichnina and the NKVD are the same idea four centuries apart: a personal instrument of terror deliberately placed outside the ordinary machinery of state, with its own rituals and its own license to kill, aimed not at the peasantry below but at the elite. Both men, strikingly, turned their terror inward — against the very class that ran the country. Ivan broke the boyars; Stalin shot the Old Bolsheviks who had made the Revolution and the marshals who would soon have to fight Hitler. Both fused terror with centralization and expansion. Both cultivated paranoia as a method of rule. And both left the country gutted — Ivan's reign bled directly into the anarchy of the Troubles.

Where it breaks

A responsible comparison has to carry the differences, and they are not small. The scale is incommensurable: Ivan's worst atrocity killed thousands over days; Stalin's system killed millions over years. So is the instrument — the sword and the impaling stake versus the card index and the freight train.

The psychology diverges just as sharply. Ivan killed in passion and then agonized over it, commissioning monasteries to pray for the souls of the people he had destroyed. Stalin killed on paper, at a desk, and left behind no evidence of a troubled night. Ivan's was a sacral, personal, medieval monarchy. Stalin's was a modern party-state armed with an ideology that claimed science and history itself were on its side — a claim no tsar could have made, and one that arguably made his terror more total, not less. Ivan had God to answer to, and it slowed him down. Stalin had History, which only urged him on.

The frame

That last contrast is the whole point. When Stalin looked at Ivan, he did not see a cautionary tale. He saw a kindred spirit who had lacked his advantages — who had been hobbled by conscience and religion where Stalin had a doctrine that dissolved both. His complaint that Ivan "killed too few and repented too much" is not an aside. It is a dictator telling you, in the third person, exactly how he had resolved the one problem that had defeated his chosen ancestor.

He saw himself in Ivan. What he admired tells you more than what he denied.
