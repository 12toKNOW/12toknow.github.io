Accounting Opened All This to Me

From $500 and a library card to Corporate Controller & Treasurer of a company sold for $4.1B, to founder of a practitioner-reference publishing house.

Phil Baratelli, CPA, MBAFounder, The Baratelli Institute
LinkedIn ↗

I started with $500 in 1989. I was a recently-graduated finance major, and I'd quickly realized school was drastically short on the tactical business skills the real desk requires. So I threw myself into the public library's investment and business-management section — every Buffett letter, every Graham chapter, every accounting primer I could find. At the same time, I went back to school at night to earn my CPA.

Before public accounting, I went through formal commercial-credit and commercial-real-estate credit training at two regional banks, First Union and Barnett Bank. That's not the standard CPA path — most accountants go straight into a firm, learn audit and tax, then move to industry. I went the other direction, through the lender's seat first. I think it made me a better public accountant than the standard sequence does: when a senior on an audit hands you a loan agreement and asks you to walk the debt-covenant schedule, you read it differently once you've sat on the other side of that table.

Auditing the company I'd later run

I spent three years at PricewaterhouseCoopers as a Senior Associate, coordinating and supervising financial-statement audits — among the clients, Armor Holdings, whose audit I worked years before I would sit in its Controller and Treasurer's chair. The CPA license came out of those years, and they were life-changing. They put me on the track that became Corporate Controller and Treasurer at Armor, then a string of public-company CFO seats, then Family Office CFO, then cofounder of a company of my own.

One desk, every function

Armor Holdings was a diversified armor-and-security-products business that grew through constant acquisition and sold to BAE Systems in 2007 for approximately $4.1 billion. As Corporate Controller and Treasurer, I rang the NYSE Closing Bell on November 4, 2003. At a company that size — big enough to require public-company finance discipline, not big enough to spread it across a dozen departments — the Controller was the Treasurer was the capital-markets contact was the M&A accounting lead was the insurance-program administrator.

I used to joke that other companies had floors of people to do what I handled.

You see the seams between functions firsthand when you're the one responsible for all of them. That's where the acquisition accounting, the SEC reporting, the treasury and capital-markets work, and eventually the practitioner instincts behind everything I do now actually came from.

More public-company CFO and Treasurer seats followed — Clarus Corporation, Highlands Acquisition Corp, Kanders & Company. Then a different kind of seat entirely: Family Office CFO, sitting at the center of a family's complete advisor team — the estate attorney, the CPA, the wealth advisor, the trustee, the insurance broker. The job wasn't to know more law than the attorney or more tax than the CPA. It was to run the play: call the meeting, frame the decision, make sure the specialists who needed to be in the same room actually got there.

Building something of my own

In 2007 I cofounded ReliaQuest, a cybersecurity firm — my active involvement there concluded in 2016. Since 2019 I've run Baratelli Properties, acquiring and renovating property across thirteen states on what I call an "Extreme Affordable Housing" thesis. I'm also a board member and former interim CFO at Urban Mining, an e-waste recycler, and I've spent time advising small-business buyers through SBA-financed acquisitions.

Today I run the Baratelli Institute, publishing practitioner-grade reference material built from thirty-five years across those seats — banking credit, public accounting, public-company finance, family-office work, company-building, real estate. Different rooms, same discipline every time: read the balance sheet honestly, understand the business model end to end, and don't assert what you can't tie to evidence.

Accounting opened all this to me.

— Phil Baratelli, CPA, MBAFounder, The Baratelli Institute
