Stories from the Record

The Brother's War

The Revolution reached the family in the body of a fourteen-year-old. Vincent DeGraffenreid's own pension file names the four battles he walked through.

Status of this finding: Documented  ·  on Vincent DeGraffenreid's Revolutionary War pension file (R.2845), which names his enlistment and four engagements. Vincent is a collateral of the direct line — a brother of it.

The finding, in brief

The war did not arrive to this family as an idea. It arrived as a boy leaving home. Vincent DeGraffenreid — a great-grandson of Barbara Tempest Needham, and the brother of Mary Baker, the woman at the center of the family's Revolutionary generation — enlisted as a substitute at Lunenburg, Virginia, in January 1777, at the age of fourteen. He served on the Continental Line for the length of the war, and his own pension file, filed decades later to claim what that service had earned, names the four battles he passed through. It is the rare thing in a family history: not a soldier remembered in tradition, but a soldier fixed to specific fields on specific days by a primary record in his own cause.

Who he was

Vincent was a son of Baker DeGraffenreid and a great-grandson of Christopher de Graffenried and Barbara Tempest Needham. His sister Mary Baker is the direct-line ancestor; Vincent stands just beside that line, its brother. He was born about 1763, which makes the arithmetic of his enlistment stark — fourteen years old when he took another man's place in the ranks in the first winter of 1777, and a veteran of the war's last great action before he was twenty.

The four engagements his file names

The pension file references roughly seven years of Continental service and names four actions — a span that runs from the middle of the war to its end:

Brandywine — 11 September 1777

Washington's largest single-day battle, fought to hold the road to Philadelphia. Vincent was fourteen.

Stony Point — 15–16 July 1779

Anthony Wayne's night assault on the Hudson fort, taken at bayonet point with muskets deliberately unloaded to keep the advance silent.

Guilford Court House — 15 March 1781

Nathanael Greene's three-line stand in the North Carolina back-country — the battle that broke the strength of the British southern army even as it lost the field.

Yorktown — 19 October 1781

The siege and surrender that effectively ended the war. A boy who had gone in at Brandywine was on the field when it closed.

From Brandywine to the Yorktown surrender — four of the Revolution's signal actions, in the service of one boy from Lunenburg.

Home, and what remains open

After Yorktown Vincent returned to Lunenburg County, to a household his war had been fought from the far side of — his sister married and a mother, his family's men gone or dead. What the record fixes is his presence at the four named actions and the roughly seven years of service the file references. What it does not yet yield is the full muster-roll trail between those four points, and the exact date of his discharge — named here, in the family's discipline, so that a documented soldier is not quietly rounded up into a documented campaign. The four battles are his. The connective tissue is still being pulled.

Key sources

  • Vincent DeGraffenreid — Revolutionary War pension file R.2845, National Archives — substitute enlistment at Lunenburg, January 1777; roughly seven years' Continental service; the four named engagements (Brandywine, Stony Point, Guilford Court House, Yorktown).
  • Revolutionary War campaign histories — Brandywine (Sept 1777); Wayne's assault on Stony Point (July 1779); Greene at Guilford Court House (March 1781); the Yorktown surrender (Oct 1781) — used as the war as it reached the soldier, not as campaign narrative.
