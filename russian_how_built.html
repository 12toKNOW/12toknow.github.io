How This Was Built

Where the question came from, the order the reading actually happened in, which centuries are deep here and which are thin, how the essays get made — and where you should be careful with me.

In February 2022, Vladimir Putin justified the invasion of Ukraine with a history lesson. Ukraine, he said in substance, has always been Russia — not a country at all, but a piece of one, temporarily mislaid. I remember thinking: really? Not as a debating point. As an actual question I could not answer. I knew roughly as much Russian history as any reasonably well-read American, which is to say I knew Stalin was terrible and the Romanovs came to a bad end, and that was about the whole of it. So I started reading. Four years later this guide is what that reading turned into.

It was never meant to be a website. It was meant to be me finding out. The site happened the way a lot of things happen — the notes got long enough to need organizing, the organizing got good enough to be worth keeping, and at some point a private filing system turned into something a stranger might use. That history matters, because it explains the shape of what you are looking at. This page is the honest account of that shape: what I read, in what order, what I therefore know reasonably well, and what I have barely touched.

The order it actually happened in

Nobody designed this curriculum. It went where the interest went, which is the only reason it survived four years.

It started with Figes. One survey, the whole thousand years in a single sweep, and that was the hook. It gave me the shape of the thing — and, more usefully, it gave me a thesis to hang the rest on: that Russia is a country unusually governed by the stories it tells about its own origins. That idea has run underneath everything I have read since, including the sentence Putin said in February 2022.

Then the Romanovs took over. This is the part I will not pretend was a scholarly decision. Three centuries of one family, and the whole cast is there — Peter dragging a country westward by the beard, Catherine, the long twilight, Rasputin, and the cellar at Ekaterinburg. I read Massie on Peter, Massie on Catherine, Massie on the last chapter, Montefiore on the dynasty. It was the best story I had ever been told and I chased it hard. The dynasty is still the part of this site with the most affection in it.

Then, unexpectedly, backwards. Having gotten interested in where the Romanovs came from, I went further back than I meant to — into Muscovy, and then into the Primary Chronicle itself, which is where Russia first writes down its own beginning. That turn out to be the single most useful detour I took. When somebody tells you a nation has always been one thing, the honest place to check is the founding document. It does not say what the sentence needs it to say.

And then 1917, which I did not want. I will admit this plainly: I was not interested in the revolution and everything after. It felt like a different subject — ideology and committees where I had been reading about tsars and rivers. But you cannot read your way to 1917 and stop. So I read it, and it took over for two years: the revolution, the civil war, Stalin, the Terror, the Gulag. That is where the guide is now heaviest, and it got that way against my original intentions.

The reading was never a plan. It was one question that would not close, followed by a decade's worth of curiosity in whatever order the curiosity arrived. The site is the fossil record of that, not a syllabus.

Where this guide is deep, and where it is thin

Here is the part most sites like this one leave out. Because the reading was unplanned, the coverage is uneven — genuinely, structurally uneven — and you should know which pages are standing on forty books and which are standing on one.

Kievan Rus, to 1240Thin. The Primary Chronicle in the original, and the survey chapters. Almost no monographic reading. Treat these pages as orientation.

Imperial Russia, 1613–1917Deep on the dynasty, thin on the country. Strong on rulers, courts, and wars; much weaker on serfdom, the Great Reforms, the revolutionary movement, and 1905. And the First World War — which is what actually destroyed the monarchy — is the biggest single hole in the timeline.
Revolution & Civil WarSolid. Several full histories plus the participants in their own words.
Stalin, the Terror & the GulagThe strongest part of the guide. The most reading, the most reference pages, and most of the essays. If you trust one section here, trust this one.
The Second World WarNot yet read. Twenty-seven million Soviet dead and the founding myth of the modern Russian state, and I have a timeline card. This is the gap that embarrasses me most.
Cold War to collapse, 1953–1991Not yet read. Khrushchev, the Thaw, the dissidents, stagnation, Gorbachev — a card and a sentence apiece.
Post-Soviet Russia, 1991–Not yet read. Which is the strangest gap of all, given how this started.
Ukraine as its own subjectMissing. See below. It is next.
The literary canonDeep, and the reason for a lot of the rest. Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Gogol, Turgenev, Pushkin, Bulgakov, Pasternak, Gorky. The novels taught me more about the texture of Russian life than the histories did.

Two whole subjects are underweighted, and I want them named rather than discovered.

Women. The who's-who is heavy with tsars and commissars and light on everyone else, and there is currently not a single Soviet-era woman in it. That is a defect of my reading list, not of Russian history, and it is on the list to fix.

Russia's Jews. The Pale of Settlement, the pogroms, the revolutionary generation, the postwar campaigns — a thread running through four centuries of this story that this guide has so far reduced to one entry.

The question I started with, and still owe

The most honest thing on this page is this: the guide does not yet answer the question that caused it. There is no page here on Ukraine. There is no page on the Rus succession — on how Kiev's inheritance came to be claimed by three modern nations, on Pereiaslav and the four different things it has been made to mean, on Mazepa, on the Ukrainian republic of 1917, on the Holodomor, on 1954, on 1991, on 2014, on 2022.

The honest reason is the one this whole page is about: I went where the reading pulled me, and it pulled me toward the Romanovs and then into Stalin, and four years went by. The real answer to has Ukraine always been Russia — which is no, and which is considerably more interesting than a simple no — is the page this site is missing and the page it exists to write. It is what I am working toward next.

How the essays get made

The reference pages here are compiled from the reading. The essays work differently, and since they are the pages most likely to be read on their own, the method should be stated plainly.

Each one began as a question I could not settle while reading — something a book asserted that I wanted pressed on, a story that seemed too tidy, a comparison I wanted made carefully. I put those questions to Claude, an AI assistant, and worked the answer back and forth until it held up. So these essays are collaborative: my questions and my judgment about what is worth arguing, drafted and researched with a machine that has read more than I have and is confidently wrong more often than I am.

That method has one real hazard, and I would rather name it than have you find it. An AI will render a scholarly consensus smoothly and persuasively whether or not the consensus is quite what it says. The safeguards I use are the ones you can see on the page: every essay ends with a note on certainty that separates what is documented from what is being argued, and every essay carries a further-reading list so you can go check. Those further-reading lists are the literature of the field — not, in every case, books I have personally finished. They are pointers, not receipts.

The standing rule

Say which part is the argument

The single rule this guide tries to keep is that a reader should never have to guess which sentences are established and which are mine. Documented is documented; contested is flagged as contested; an interpretation is labeled an interpretation and offered as such. When I get this wrong it is a real error, and I would like to hear about it.

Who I lean on — and where to be careful

The narrative here rests on a small number of authors, mostly writers of large, well-made popular histories rather than academic specialists: Figes, Massie, Montefiore, Kotkin, Beevor, Zamoyski, Rappaport, Douglas Smith, Applebaum. The thematic bibliography is far wider than that and is a map of the scholarly field — but it is a map of where I am going, not a list of where I have been.

Two cautions I owe you specifically:

On Orlando Figes. He is the most-used author on this site and the one you should hold most loosely. In 2010 he was found to have posted anonymous reviews praising his own books and disparaging rival historians, which he denied before admitting. In 2011 the Russian edition of The Whisperers was cancelled after researchers at Memorial — the organization whose oral-history archive the book is built on — documented substantial errors in its case histories. His large syntheses remain widely assigned and I still think The Story of Russia is the best single entry point there is. But where this guide leans on Figes for a specific fact, that fact deserves a second source, and I am working through them.

On the numbers. Death tolls in this period are contested, sometimes bitterly. Where I give figures — for the camps, the deportations, the famine — I give ranges and say where they come from. Cold War estimates ran far higher than the post-1991 archives support; the archives themselves undercount in ways historians are still arguing about. Anyone quoting a single confident number for any of this, including me, should be asked where they got it.

What this is for

It is a reader's guide, built in the open by someone who is not a historian, for anyone else who got curious and wanted somewhere to start. It is not a scholarly publication and it does not want to be mistaken for one. If you are a student, use it the way you would use a good, opinionated friend who has done a lot of reading: for orientation, for the shape of a period, for what to read next — and then go to the books in the bibliography for anything you intend to rely on.

It is also, still, unfinished, which is the point. The reading happens on walks and in the car, an hour or two a day, and the site grows behind it. There is no deadline. There is just the next book, and then the next thing I did not know I wanted to understand.

Where the reading stands: currently working through the second volume of Gorky's autobiographical trilogy, In the World. The finished shelf, with notes on each title, is at The Reading; the full visual shelf including what is queued is at the Bookshelf.

A note on this page: everything above is a statement about my own reading and method rather than about Russian history, and it is the one page here where you should simply take my word for it. The claims about Figes are matters of public record and are easily checked. The gaps are self-reported, which means the real list is probably slightly longer than this one.
