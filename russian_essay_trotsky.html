What If Trotsky Had Won?

The great counterfactual of the Revolution — and why the gentle version of it is a myth.

It is the most tempting "what if" in Soviet history. Lenin dies; the succession is open; and instead of the grey, patient party bureaucrat, the prize goes to the dazzling orator who built the Red Army and expected to inherit by right of talent. Would a Soviet Union under Leon Trotsky have turned outward toward world revolution instead of inward toward Stalin's fortress state — and would it have been, in the bargain, less murderous? The first half of that question is real and answerable. The second half hides a trap, and the trap is the point.

The real distinction

Start with the doctrine, because it is genuine. Trotsky's permanent revolution held that a backward, peasant Russia could not build socialism alone — the revolution had to spread to the advanced industrial countries, Germany above all, or it would suffocate. Stalin's socialism in one country (1924) said the opposite: build it here first, behind fortified borders, and let the world wait. Trotsky faced outward; Stalin faced in. So far your instinct is exactly right.

But one correction sharpens it. Stalin did not really give up on spreading communism — he subordinated it to the Soviet state. The Communist International became an instrument of Moscow's foreign policy rather than an engine of genuine world revolution, and Stalin eventually dissolved it outright in 1943 as a gesture to his wartime allies. Then, after 1945, he exported communism anyway — imposing it across Eastern Europe at the point of the Red Army's bayonets. So the truest contrast is not "spread it" against "don't." It is Trotsky's communism as an international workers' movement against Stalin's communism as an extension of Russian state power — revolution against empire.

The myth of the gentle Trotsky

Now the trap. The notion that Trotsky-instead-of-Stalin would have meant a kinder Soviet Union is largely a comforting fiction — a story the anti-Stalinist left needed, and one Trotsky's own magnificent prose did much to author from exile. It does not survive contact with what he actually did. Trotsky built the Red Army and ran the Red Terror through the Civil War; he ordered the bloody suppression of the Kronstadt sailors' rising in 1921; he championed the militarization of labor, conscripting workers into disciplined armies of toil; and he defended the one-party dictatorship as ferociously as any Bolshevik alive. His line to a party congress in 1924 — that one "cannot be right against the Party" — is not the creed of a man who would have tolerated dissent once in power.

Whatever a Soviet Union under Trotsky would have been, "gentle" is not on the menu. The serious biographers — Robert Service, Stephen Kotkin — expect an authoritarian and bloody regime; even his sympathetic chronicler, Isaac Deutscher, does not pretend otherwise.

Where it really would have diverged

Which does not mean it would have been the same. Strip away the romance and there are genuine, consequential differences to weigh.

The rise of Hitler. This is the strongest point in Trotsky's favor, and it is not hypothetical. Stalin's Comintern in the early 1930s branded the German Social Democrats "social fascists" and forbade the Communists from allying with them — a catastrophe that split the German left and helped Hitler walk into power in 1933. Trotsky, watching from exile, saw the Nazi danger with piercing clarity and pleaded for a united working-class front to stop it. A Trotsky directing the Comintern might well have fought Hitler's rise far more effectively — a single divergence that could have bent the whole century.

Foreign policy. More broadly, Trotsky would likely have gambled harder on revolution abroad and struck fewer bargains with capitalist states — a more confrontational, less cynical posture toward the West, for better or worse.

The economy. Here is the great irony: rapid industrialization and central planning were Trotsky's program first — the platform of the Left Opposition that Stalin denounced in the 1920s while allied with the party's moderate right, then seized and executed with even greater brutality once he had crushed both wings. So the forced march of the Five-Year Plans, and perhaps collectivization with it, might well have come under Trotsky too — possibly with less catastrophic cruelty, possibly not.

The Great Terror. The self-cannibalizing purge of 1937–38, which devoured the Party itself and rested on signed quotas and a cult of the leader, was very much Stalin's personal signature — the product of his paranoia and his method of rule. Trotsky was a ruthless intellectual, but not that particular kind of bureaucratic paranoiac. A Trotsky regime would have been violent; it might not have turned that violence inward upon its own founders in quite that way.

Sidebar · a companion caution

Did Stalin Help Hitler?

The kernel of truth. The "social fascism" line was real, and it mattered: in the November 1932 election the German Communists and Social Democrats together outpolled the Nazis, and a united front might have been a genuine obstacle to Hitler. Historians fault the policy heavily, and Trotsky warned against it at the time.

Why "responsible" overreaches. Hitler's causes were deeper and mostly German — the humiliation of Versailles, the Great Depression, the conservative elites who handed him the chancellorship in January 1933, an antisemitism centuries older than the USSR, and a Communist–Social Democrat hatred that ran both ways. Stalin's blunder was one thread in a very thick rope, not the rope itself.

The tighter link. The clearer case is the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939, which freed Hitler to invade Poland without a two-front war and start the Second World War — and the Holocaust unfolded in the very lands that war placed under Nazi control. Even there, though, the honest word is enabled, not caused.

The verdict. Stalin bears a real share of responsibility for weakening the resistance to Hitler in 1933 and for enabling the war in 1939 — but not for the Holocaust, whose intent and machinery were the Nazis' alone. A contributing factor and the primary guilt are two different things, and honest history holds them in separate hands.

The man, or the machine?

Underneath every line of this sits the deepest question, the same one that runs through the argument over whether dictatorship was Russia's only option: how much of the horror was the man, and how much was the machine? One school holds that the Bolshevik system itself — a one-party dictatorship holding down a vast peasant country, encircled by enemies, armed with an ideology that sanctified any means — would have produced terror and tyranny whoever held the pen; change Stalin for Trotsky and you change the accent, not the ending. The other holds that Stalin's specific character shaped the specific catastrophe — the scale of the killing, the cult, the paranoia — and that a different man writes a materially different history.

The honest answer is probably both: a Trotsky Russia would still have been a dictatorship built on violence — but it might have looked outward instead of in, fought fascism harder, and been spared the particular madness of 1937. A different shape of catastrophe, not its absence.

That is the sober verdict the counterfactual yields. It refuses the fairy tale — the good Bolshevik who would have made it all come right — and it refuses the fatalism that says nothing depended on the men at all. Trotsky losing to Stalin mattered enormously; it simply did not matter in the direction his admirers wish it had. The tragedy is not that the wrong man won. It is that, by 1924, the machine the whole generation had built may already have narrowed the possible endings to a choice among tyrants.

Further reading: Isaac Deutscher, The Prophet trilogy (the sympathetic case); Robert Service, Trotsky: A Biography and Stalin: A Biography; Stephen Kotkin, Stalin (2 vols.); Robert V. Daniels, The Conscience of the Revolution; and Trotsky's own The Revolution Betrayed and My Life, read with a skeptical eye.

A note on certainty: this is a counterfactual, and speculative by nature — no archive can confirm a road not taken. What can be documented is what the men actually did, wrote, and argued, and the essay is built on that: the doctrines are firm, Trotsky's Civil-War record is firm, the "social fascism" blunder is firm; the projections drawn from them are argument, offered as such. Where the piece guesses, it means to guess out loud.
