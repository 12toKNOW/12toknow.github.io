The Surveyor’s Priory (James Nedeham, ca. 1490– 1544)

Working audit-trail file, not for publication — v3.2 chapter draft, 14 May 2026 v2 extends v1. The v1 audit trail is intact below; new entries are marked [v3.2 addition] in the relevant section, and v1 open items that have been resolved or refined by v3.2 are marked [v3.2 update] in the Gaps and Open Items section near the end.

This memo records the documentary source for every factual claim in Chapter 1 v3.1, and identifies passages of authorial reconstruction (scenes, dialogue, sensory detail) that go beyond the documented record. It is for the project’s audit trail; the reader-facing Notes section will be extracted from it at manuscript-assembly stage and styled separately.

Source-credibility key: P = primary source (manuscript, archival document, contemporary record). S = secondary scholarly source. R = reference / aggregator.

T = tradition / family record. AR = authorial reconstruction.

Opening scene (the barn-raising, summer 1541)

VIII [1537].

§1.5 — The Grant of Wymondley

  • December 1537 lease of the priory site from the Court of Augmentations — S — Doggett 1997, Wymondley case-study section, p. ~100 ff. Doggett establishes the lease as preceding the formal grant.

  • 12 April 1538 grant of Wymondley Priory by letters patent under the Great Seal — P — Letters and Papers, Foreign and Domestic, of the Reign of Henry VIII, vol. XIII, item 887, sub-grant 13. Standard published calendar of Henry VIII’s state papers.

[v3.2 addition] • The grant was held “of the king in chief by the service of a tenth part of a knight’s fee” — P/S — Victoria County History of Hertfordshire, Vol.

III (1912), p. 190. The phrase preserves the formal feudal-tenure form of the grant: military tenure (knight-service), not free socage. By 1538 the service was customarily commuted to a money payment; the dignity of the form, however, was the point. A Crown grant by knight-service was the gentleman’s holding; a grant in free socage would have been the lesser thing. Chapter 1 v3.1 did not name this tenure form.

  • The grant carried priory lands in Little Wymondley, outlying parcels in the parish, the rectory and tithes of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire, and the conventual buildings — P/S — Letters and Papers vol. XIII item 887(13); supplementary detail from Doggett 1997.

  • Chancery testimony of John Nedeham confirming Wymondley as a Crown grant — P — The National Archives, Kew: C 2/Eliz/N5/46 (Court of Chancery, Six Clerks Office, Pleadings, Series I, Elizabeth I to Charles I). Short title: Nedeham v Clarke. Bill of complaint contains the phrase: “Wymondley, Hertfordshire, formerly belonging to the priory of Little Wymondley, and since the dissolution of the said priory granted by King Henry 8 to James Nedeham esq, plaintiff’s father.”

  • Priory founded ca. 1200 by Richard de Argenton as a house of Augustinian canons regular — R — Victoria County History of Hertfordshire, vol. III; standard medieval-priory reference.

  • 1537 Bishop of Lincoln’s visitation: buildings “**greatly ruined,” Prior John Atow had spent £5 on repairs with £15 4s 8d further set aside — **S — Doggett 1997, Wymondley case-study section, drawing on the published Lincoln diocesan records and the 1537 inventory.

  • 1537 inventory of priory possessions lists hall, kitchen, brewhouse, bakehouse, buttery, pantry, prior’s chamber; the chancel and bell tower are not listed — S — Doggett 1997 (citing the 1537 inventory in the Lincoln diocesan records and the suppression papers).

[v3.2 update] • Wife: Alice, daughter of a Merry of Hatfield, Hertfordshire — P — 1572 Heralds’ Visitation of Hertfordshire, Harleian Society vol. xxii (1886), p. 16, “Needham of Wymondley” pedigree, which records the wife of James Nedam of Chislest [Chiselhurst] in Kent as “Alice, da. of …. Merry of Hatfield.” The pedigree is signed in autograph by John Nedham of Wymondley, James and Alice’s son, in the summer of 1572.   • Conversion of the western nave of the priory church into the family hall — S — Doggett 1997, Wymondley case-study section, drawing on the 1731 estate map (Hertfordshire Archives and Local Studies, HRO 32,349) showing the cruciform footprint, the surviving lancet windows in the south wall, and architectural analysis of the existing fabric.

It is not directly recorded that any specific meal took place; the architectural inference is that the converted nave functioned as the hall.

Chapter 4 (Barbara’s baptism scene) will require a separate decision on venue.

§1.8 — What James Means

  • Family seat as Crown reward rather than inherited estate — Interpretive synthesis based on the documentary record (TNA C 2/Eliz/N5/46, John Nedeham’s Chancery testimony) and standard scholarship on Reformation gentry origins.

  • Family retention of the priory through eight monarchs and one republic (Mary, Elizabeth, James I, Charles I, Commonwealth, Cromwell, Charles II, James II, William and Mary) — S — 1962 Journal of the Society of Archivists article p. 268; family tradition; Doggett 1997 (mentions descent until 1725/1733).

  • Eustace Nedham survived the Civil War period at Wymondley — T/S — Family tradition; project genealogical research files; consistent with the 1643–44 tax memoranda by George Senior recorded in MS Rawlinson D. 778.

  • The pay-books passed to Richard Rawlinson (1690–1755) by some time before his death and went with his collection to the Bodleian Library at his death in 1755 — S — 1962 Journal of the Society of Archivists article p. 267: “At his [Rawlinson’s] death in 1755 they passed with his collections to the Bodleian Library.”

  • Hampton Court Great Hall hammer-beam roof still in situ, four hundred and ninety-one years after 1535 — Direct present-day observation; Historic Royal Palaces visitor materials.

  • 1973–74 restoration of the priory house destroyed most surviving sixteenth-century datable features — S — Doggett 1997, Wymondley case-study section. Doggett describes the restoration as “insensitive” and identifies the destruction of “all the 16th-century datable windows and many other potentially datable features.”

  • Wymondley priory passed out of the male line in 1725 (death of George Needham, two daughters) and was sold to Samuel Vanderplank in 1733 — S — 1962 Journal of the Society of Archivists article p. 267: “the property descended in the male line until 1725 in which year died George Needham who left two daughters, Dorothy and Martha. About this memo This is the sources memo for Chapter 5 of I Am Coming, Book One Volume I. It records, for every substantive factual claim in the chapter, what tier of evidence supports the claim and what the source is. The chapter is structurally a love story; structurally a love story is also a reconstruction. The reconstruction is disciplined throughout — every external fact in the chapter is documented, and every internal scene is anchored in what the documented facts require. But the chapter has more authorial reconstruction (AR) in it than any of the preceding chapters of the book, and the audit trail has to be honest about that.

This memo follows the v1 / v2 tier convention of the Chapter 1 sources memos:

  • P — primary-source evidence (a parish register, a Chancery file, a contemporaneous letter, a probate document).

  • S — secondary-published source (a printed county history, a published genealogy, a scholarly reference work).

  • T — tradition or family-history claim, supported by the 1925 History or by long-standing family report but not yet anchored at primary-source level.

  • AR — authorial reconstruction. The fact is not documented but is consistent with what the documented facts require, or is plausible within the period’s conventions, and is flagged here so a future reader can see exactly where the chapter has imagined.

What changed v1.1 → v1.2 This memo updates v1.1 in two places only. All other entries are unchanged from v1.1 and are reproduced here for completeness.

The v1.1 → v1.2 revision pass made the following substantive changes to the chapter:

  • §5.9 letter text. v1.1 rendered Barbara’s August 1713 letter as “I am coming. Do not write back to tell me not to. By the time you read this I will already be at sea.” — three sentences, expanded across three short paragraphs. v1.2 strips the letter to three words only: “I am coming.” — no salutation, no date, no signature. The earlier sentences about not writing back and already being at sea are preserved as Barbara’s spoken intention in §5.8, but the letter itself, on the page, is now bare. The v1.2 §5.9 prose renders Barbara at the writing-desk recognizing, in real time, that the three pages she has composed in her head are not the letter — that the letter she owes Christopher is not an explanation but an arrival.

  • §5.10 closing image. v1.1 closed §5.10 on Barbara in the bunk listening to the Atlantic begin under her, with the chapter’s final image being her commonplace-book entry off the Lizard. v1.2 extends the closing by adding a forward-looking paragraph that tracks the August letter ahead of her across the Atlantic — riding the gulf stream south-by-southwest in the hold of the Mary Anne, reaching Charleston in mid-November, carried inland by a runner named Wilkes, arriving in Christopher’s hand on the seventeenth of November at New Bern, in the late afternoon, with the cold light already going out of the river behind his half-built house. The chapter then closes on four cascading lines: He would open it. / He would unfold the sheet. / He would read what she had written. / I am coming. The chapter is now hinged structurally to Chapter 6 §6.1, which opens on the same Tuesday afternoon at New Bern with the letter arriving in the leather pouch.

Both changes deepen the chapter’s commitment to the three words as the chapter’s title-bearing moment. Chapter 6 — Sources Memo

I Am Coming A Searcy Family History Volume I — England, 1490 to 1714 Chapter 6 Sources Memo, v1 — 14 May 2026 Parallel audit trail to Chapter 6 v1 draft DeWayne Lee Searcy Mountain Brook, Alabama — May 2026

About this memo This is the sources memo for Chapter 6 of I Am Coming, Book One Volume I. It records, for every substantive factual claim in the chapter, what tier of evidence supports the claim and what the source is. Chapter 6 is the convergence chapter — the chapter in which Barbara’s three-word letter reaches Christopher at New Bern, Christopher rides south to Charleston, Barbara crosses the Atlantic on the Hopewell, and the two meet on the Cooper Street pier on the morning of 11 February 1714. The chapter closes on the documented marriage at the Cooper Street church on 22 February 1714 and on the documented Bible inscription Christopher wrote on the fly-leaf that night.

Chapter 6 contains substantially more authorial reconstruction (AR) than Chapter 5. Chapter 5 was about a woman at a writing-desk in Hertfordshire making a decision; the documentary anchors were dense (the 1685 baptism; the family proposals; the Wingate cousinage; Maurice’s death). Chapter 6 is about two people on opposite sides of an ocean converging on a single dock in a single February morning; the documentary anchors are sparse (the 22 February 1714 marriage; the Bible inscription; the Reverend Gideon Johnston as officiant). Between those anchors the chapter reconstructs a fourteen-week Atlantic crossing, a seventeen-day Carolina road, thirty-five days on a bench at Charleston, and a dock-side recognition scene at a quarter-mile. The reconstruction is bounded by the documented outcome (they met; they married; they signed the church register)

and by the period’s documented practical logistics (transatlantic packet schedules; the Cooper Street pier as the principal Charleston landing-stage; the colonial commissary at Charleston as the natural marrying clergyman for a Bernese Reformed Protestant). Within those bounds the chapter is, more than any other chapter in the book, a love story.

This memo follows the v1 / v2 tier convention of the Chapter 1 sources memos:

  • P — primary-source evidence (a parish register, a Chancery file, a contemporaneous letter, a probate document, the church register entry, the Bible fly-leaf).

  • S — secondary-published source (a printed county history, a published genealogy, a scholarly reference work).

  • T — tradition or family-history claim, supported by the 1925 History or by long-standing family report but not yet anchored at primary-source level.

  • AR — authorial reconstruction. The fact is not documented but is consistent with what the documented facts require, or is plausible within the period’s conventions, and is flagged here so a future reader can see exactly where the chapter has imagined.

The chapter contains, in addition to the routine documentary claims, four large AR categories that need their own framing notes before the per-claim list.

The four big AR categories (1) The Christopher VI interior The chapter commits Christopher VI’s voice for the first time in the book. Chapter 5 rendered him almost entirely from the outside — his arrival at Wymondley, his letters home, his absence as the engagement’s slowly-receding endpoint. Chapter 6 brings him into close-third interior in §6.1, §6.3, §6.5, and §6.7.

His thoughts, his calculations, his decisions, his physical small actions in the half-built house at New Bern and on the bench at Charleston, are reconstruction.

What is documented. Christopher VI de Graffenried was born at Worb, Switzerland, on 8 December 1690 (1925 History, p. 145; standard de Graffenried genealogy). He was English-educated for two years at a Reformed academy in Switzerland (S). He came to Carolina in 1710 in connection with his father’s New Bern venture (S). He was in Williamsburg in early 1712 after the Tuscarora War (S). He was at Charleston in February 1714 for his marriage to Barbara Tempest Needham (P — the Cooper Street church register; the de Graffenried family Bible fly-leaf inscription). He was at Williamsburg by 1717 when he bought Lot 47 / the Pitt-Dixon House (S). He died at Williamsburg in 1742 (S).

What is not documented. No letters of Christopher VI’s from the period 1710- 1714 survive in the project record. No journal survives. No record of his daily life at New Bern in the half-life of the colony after the Tuscarora War survives in the project record. No record of his thoughts on receiving Barbara’s August 1713 letter survives — the letter itself is invention (see Chapter 5 §5.9). No record of his ride south from New Bern to Charleston in November-December 1713 survives. No record of his thirty-five days on the Cooper Street bench survives. The Bible inscription is the only contemporary first-person Christopher document in the chapter, and it is short.

The chapter’s discipline. Christopher’s interior is reconstructed from what the documented outcome requires — he was at the dock; he married Barbara within eleven days of her arrival; he signed the fly-leaf entry the same night — and from what is plausible for a 27-year-old Swiss-Bernese aristocrat in his circumstances. The trilingual register (English with Hans and the harbor master and Mrs. Brewton; French with his father; German in childhood-memory and to the cat) is consistent with his documented background (Bernese, English-educated, French-correspondent with the Baron). The Pascal in his coat is consistent with a Reformed Protestant gentleman of his class and period; the specific text (Pensées) is invented within the documented French Reformed reading milieu of the early eighteenth century. The bench at Cooper Street, the harbor master with the tea, the brown coat with the hat in his hand, the child’s hand-above-the-head gesture, the careful French calculation about the Mary Anne and the Hopewell — all reconstruction.

The chapter’s most extensive Christopher-interior passage is §6.1, the seventeenth-of-November scene at New Bern. The pacing (the laying-out of the four letters in the Yverdon-taught order; the cold across the body when he sees the letter is thin; the joy that comes up from a place in his chest he had not known about; the practical calculation in French to the empty room; the German sie kommt sie kommt sie kommt into the cat’s fur; the half-written letter to his father that begins Père — and is blurred at the edge) is invention. It is bounded by what the documented outcome requires: he received the letter, he left New Bern within days, he was at Charleston by early December, he was at the pier on the morning of 11 February.

(2) The Hopewell — vessel, captain, passengers, storm The chapter renders the Hopewell as a 170-ton brigantine, eight years old, with a regular Charleston run and a Mr. Eldridge of fifty-some as captain. Barbara is one of six passengers: Mr. Pelletier of Bristol; the widow Mrs. Mary Houghton of Norwich; the two Quaker brothers Christopher and Jonathan Bryce, returning from a year of family business in Yorkshire; Miss Susannah Fenwick of Bristol, twenty-six, going out as lady’s companion to a Mrs. Bellinger of Charleston; and Barbara herself. The crossing is fourteen weeks and four days, with one nine-hour storm on the night of 3 January 1714 that snaps the foremast partly down and leaves the brigantine at half-rig for the rest of the crossing. The ship makes the Charleston roads at quarter past nine on the morning of 11 February 1714.

What is documented. The transatlantic packet trade between London/Gravesend and Charleston in 1713-1714 is well documented in general terms (S, standard colonial maritime history). Brigantines of approximately 100- 200 tons were the standard vessel class for the Charleston run; the typical crossing was 10-12 weeks for autumn or 8-10 weeks for spring; the typical passenger complement was four to twelve, mostly merchants, factors, indentured servants, and women crossing to colonial marriages. The Cooper Street pier at Charleston was the principal landing-stage for transatlantic vessels in this period (S). The general weather pattern for the open Atlantic in January at latitude 30°N — long swells from the southwest, strong storms with troughs of 10-15 feet — is documented in period maritime records.

What is not documented. No specific brigantine named Hopewell sailing from Gravesend on 28 October 1713 and arriving at Charleston on 11 February 1714 has been identified at primary-source level. No captain named Eldridge has been identified for that run. The passenger list is invented. Mr. Pelletier, Mrs. Houghton, the Bryce brothers, Susannah Fenwick, and Mrs. Bellinger of Charleston are all invented characters. The 3 January 1714 storm is invented within the documented general weather patterns of January in mid-Atlantic at latitude 30°N. The foremast damage is invented.

The chapter’s discipline. The Hopewell is the chapter’s largest single AR construction. It is necessary to the chapter’s structure: a vessel, a captain, a crossing of approximately fourteen weeks, with the specific incidents that allow Barbara’s voice to develop at sea. A Customs House Gravesend departures search (flagged in the Chapter 5 v1.1 / v1.2 Sources Memos and reiterated here) might identify the actual vessel that carried Barbara, in which case the chapter’s Hopewell should be replaced with the documented name. If no specific vessel can be identified — and after three centuries this is the more likely outcome — the Hopewell and Mr. Eldridge stand as the chapter’s plausible reconstruction.

The passenger list is structurally important — Susannah is the woman whose hand Barbara holds through the storm in §6.4, and whose name is the chapter’s quietest grace-note (the Quaker brother named Christopher whom Barbara realizes only at the end of §6.4 was one of those praying for her in the night). If a future Charleston-arrival manifest for early February 1714 is found, the passenger list can be revised to match the documented arrivals. The structural function — a young woman whose hand Barbara holds, a Quaker prayer through the bulkhead, a widow who weeps at the first gull — is robust against any specific identification.

The storm on the night of 3 January 1714 is invention. A nine-hour storm in mid-Atlantic at latitude 30°N in early January is consistent with the period’s documented weather patterns but is not specifically attested. The foremast damage and the half-rig for the rest of the crossing are calibrated to a slightly delayed arrival at Charleston (11 February rather than late January or the first week of February per Mr. Eldridge’s pre-storm estimate). The delay is internally consistent with the chapter’s other timing decisions and with the 22 February 1714 marriage date.

(3) The Carolina road south, November-December 1713 The chapter renders Christopher’s ride from New Bern to Charleston as a seventeen-day journey by the inland route, approximately 350 miles, between 20 November and 7 December 1713. He rides with Hans the manservant, two horses, and three trunks. The principal incidents on the road are: a Sunday day-and-a-half delay at Bath’s Mill on 22 November when Christopher’s off-fore horse comes up lame with a stone bruise pared out by a slow careful farrier named Peebles; a Wednesday night at the planter Mr. Burrington’s house on the Cape Fear with the Dutch winter scene over the mantel; a Friday night at a Quaker meeting-house at Cape Fear Bridge with bread, apples, and a pallet on the floor.

What is documented. The inland route from New Bern to Charleston in the early eighteenth century is documented in general terms — it ran south through the Carolina backcountry along the chain of river-settlements (S, standard colonial Carolina history). The typical pace for a gentleman with a manservant and pack-horses was 30-40 miles a day in good weather, 20-25 in bad. The total distance of approximately 350 miles is consistent with period travel-time estimates. Inns, planter houses, and Quaker meeting-houses provided overnight stops along the route (S). George Burrington — later Governor Burrington — was an early Carolina planter with documented ties to the de Graffenried-era colony; a November 1713 hospitality scene between him and Christopher VI is consistent with the documented social pattern but is not specifically attested. The Quaker presence in early-eighteenth-century North Carolina is well documented (S).

What is not documented. Christopher VI’s specific route, dates, and overnight stops are not in the project record. The stone bruise, the farrier Peebles, the Burrington dinner, the Dutch winter scene over the mantel, the Quaker meeting-house at Cape Fear Bridge, and the bread-and-apples supper are all invented. The seventeen-day duration and the 7 December arrival at Charleston are calibrated to the chapter’s overall convergence timeline (Christopher arriving at Charleston about sixteen days ahead of the Hopewell’s likely landing window). The Mrs. Brewton lodgings on Tradd Street and the Reverend Gideon Johnston as officiating clergyman are documented in the broad sense (Charleston had a documented lodging-house culture in this period; Johnston was the documented colonial commissary at Charleston) — see §6.3 and §6.7 per-claim entries below.

The chapter’s discipline. The road south is bounded reconstruction. Christopher had to get to Charleston before the Hopewell could plausibly arrive; the chapter renders him doing so. The specific incidents on the road serve the chapter’s pacing — the stone bruise is the chapter’s first chafe moment for Christopher; the Burrington dinner is one of the road’s two small kindnesses; the Quaker meeting-house is the other. None of the three is documented. All are consistent with the period’s documented road culture. The Quaker meeting-house at Cape Fear Bridge is a particularly small AR — the specific settlement name “Cape Fear Bridge” is a placeholder; a future colonial-Carolina-Quaker-meeting-locations search might identify the documented meeting-house Christopher would have stopped at, in which case the chapter’s invented “Cape Fear Bridge” should be replaced.

(4) The dock scene and the marriage The chapter’s resolution is the dock at Cooper Street on the morning of 11 February 1714 (§6.7) and the marriage at the Cooper Street church on the morning of Monday 22 February 1714, the Reverend Gideon Johnston officiating (§6.7 closing).

What is documented. The marriage of Christopher VI de Graffenried and Barbara Tempest Needham at Charleston on 22 February 1714 is documented at primary-source level — it appears in the surviving Cooper Street church register entry of that date and in the fly-leaf inscription Christopher wrote on the de Graffenried family Bible the same night (1925 History, p. 147, citing the family Bible). The formal inscription reads, in French, in Christopher’s hand: Christophe de Graffenried, fils du Baron Christophe V de Bernberg, époux. Barbara Tempest née Needham, de Wymondley en Hertfordshire, épouse. Mariés en l’église du commissaire à Charleston, en Caroline du Sud, le vingt-deuxième jour de février, 1714. This is the chapter’s primary documentary anchor and the only substantial first-person Christopher document for the period.

Reverend Gideon Johnston. Johnston was the colonial commissary at Charleston for the Bishop of London from 1707 until his death in 1716. He is documented in the Bishop of London’s papers at Fulham Palace and in the early South Carolina church records (S, standard colonial Anglican church history). His role as the officiating clergyman at the marriage is documented by the church register entry; the chapter’s rendering of him is otherwise restrained — he is named but does not have any dialogue in the chapter.

What is not documented. The text of the church register entry, beyond the fact of the marriage, is not preserved in the project record. The specific witnesses to the marriage — rendered in the chapter as Mrs. Houghton and Mr. Pelletier for the bride, and Hans the manservant and a Charleston merchant named Allston for the groom — are invented; Mr. Eldridge in the second pew is invented; Susannah Fenwick attending on the arm of Mrs. Bellinger is invented. The English-language addition She came, rendered as Christopher’s smaller-hand entry on the Bible fly-leaf underneath the formal French inscription, is AR — it is invented as the chapter’s title-bearing close but is not part of the documented inscription. The single quoted French entry is the documented portion; the smaller-hand English coda is reconstruction.

The chapter’s discipline. The dock recognition (the topsails appearing at the south end of Sullivan’s Island; Christopher’s child’s hand-above-the-head gesture; Barbara’s shoulder-height matching gesture; the four-line exchange Barbara / Christopher / You came / I told you I would; the eleven-day interval to the marriage; the Monday wedding date) is reconstruction. None of it survives in the documentary record. The four lines in particular are invention; they are the chapter’s calibration of what two people who have crossed an ocean to be in front of each other in February 1714 would say in the first ninety seconds. The reconstruction is bounded by the documented outcome (they did meet at the dock; they were married eleven days later; the bridal-side witnesses were drawn from her ship-companions; the groom’s-side witnesses were drawn from his Carolina-side circle). The chapter does not invent the marriage; it invents the manner of the recognition.

The She came closing image is the chapter’s single most authorially-asserted moment. It is the title-bearing close — Barbara’s I am coming in Chapter 5 §5.9 / §5.10 is answered in Chapter 6 §6.7’s smaller-hand English entry on the same Bible fly-leaf — and it is invention. The chapter’s discipline is that the formal French inscription is quoted accurately from the documented Bible entry, and that the smaller-hand English addition is flagged here as reconstruction. A future de Graffenried-family-Bible photographic inspection might confirm or refute the She came coda; until that inspection is undertaken, the coda stands as the chapter’s reconstruction of what Christopher would have written underneath the formal entry if he had written anything more, which the documented record does not establish that he did.

The quoted line — Le silence éternel de ces espaces infinis m’effraie (“The eternal silence of these infinite spaces terrifies me”) — is fragment 206 in the standard Brunschvicg numbering, fragment 201 in Lafuma. It is genuinely Pascal. Christopher’s reading it on the bench is invention but is period-plausible for a Reformed Protestant gentleman of his class and education.

  • The harbor master nodding to Christopher; bringing him tea at eleven o’clock from his own kettle in the shed; the children of the longshoremen recognizing him as the gentleman on the bench who would not move — AR — Invention. Period-plausible.

  • The 24 January false alarm: the Bristol brigantine Endeavour of approximately 120 tons appearing at the south end of Sullivan’s Island; Christopher identifying it as not the Hopewell; the Endeavour carrying English manufactured goods and forty-three indentured servants for the Carolina rice plantations — AR — The Endeavour is invented. The general pattern of Bristol-Charleston indentured-servant brigantines is period-attested (S, standard colonial maritime history).

  • The unsent letter Christopher writes that night and seals and puts in the drawer of the small desk; the letter that would remain in the drawer for twenty-nine years until his son Anton Tscharner de Graffenried burns it unread after his father’s death in 1742 — AR — Invention. The chapter’s longest-shadow image. Christopher VI’s son Anton Tscharner de Graffenried is a documented figure (S, standard de Graffenried genealogy); he did inherit his father’s Williamsburg papers. The unread-and-burned letter is reconstruction within the documented general practice of sons handling fathers’ private papers in the eighteenth century.

§6.6 — Hopewell, off the Carolina coast, early February 1714

  • The watch informed two days earlier that they were within four or five days of land; the sighting of gulls on the 10th of February; the widow Houghton weeping at the first gull — AR — Invention.

  • Barbara up on deck before dawn on 11 February; dressing in the dark; the wool gown her cousin’s wife had given her; the cloak; the shoes with the buckles that had not rusted through — AR — Invention. The wardrobe details are consistent with Chapter 5 §5.10’s reconstruction of the trunk-packing scene.

  • The leadsman’s chant — By the mark, twenty. By the mark, eighteen. By the mark, sixteen. By the mark, twelve. — AR/S — The standard depth-sounding chant of a brigantine in coastal waters is period-attested (S, standard maritime history). The specific soundings are invention.

  • The thin gray-green line appearing at the western horizon at a quarter past six; Carolina coming up out of the water in the grey-gold light — AR — Invention. The chapter’s emotional peak (the section is the chapter’s true peak, with §6.7’s dock scene being the chapter’s resolution).

  • Mr. Eldridge in his good landfall coat standing ten feet behind her without speaking; his quiet We will be in the roads by midday, Miss Needham. We will tie up at the Cooper Street pier in the early afternoon if the harbor pilots are ready — AR — Invention.

  • Mr. Eldridge’s He will be there — AR — Invention. The chapter’s most significant quiet line on the Mr. Eldridge side; the simple plain factual judgment of a man who has been carrying women across the Atlantic for thirty years and who has, on the morning in question, decided that this particular marriage is going to happen.

§6.7 — The Cooper Street pier, Charleston, 11 February 1714

  • The brigantine appearing at the south end of Sullivan’s Island at twenty minutes past nine in the morning; Christopher on the bench since seven — AR — Invention. The specific timings are calibrated to the chapter’s narrative-required sequence (the brigantine identified at a quarter-mile out; the ship tying up at the pier at eight minutes past ten).

  • The harbor master’s Mr. de Graffenried. Brigantine at the south end of the island. — AR — Invention.

  • The identification of the Hopewell — 170 tons, pale brown with a dark waterline, half-rigged at the foremast — and Christopher’s recognition that it was the Hopewell the way he had known in November that the letter on his desk was from Barbara before he had read the direction — AR — Invention. The cross-reference to the §6.1 recognition is the chapter’s internal echo.

  • Barbara at the bow forty minutes; the harbor pilot at the wheel; Mr. Eldridge at the rail beside her; the figure on the pier in a brown coat with his hat in his hand — AR — Invention.

  • Christopher raising the hand without the hat in it all the way above his head — the gesture Barbara had seen him make four times in the spring of 1710 at Wymondley — AR — Invention. The cross-reference to Chapter 5 §5.2 is the chapter’s longest internal echo. The cloister conversation in §5.2 also did not survive in the documentary record; the recurrence of the hand-above-the-head gesture at the dock is the chapter’s reconstruction of what would have been recognized between two people who had been waiting four years for each other.

  • The four-line exchange at the foot of the gangway: Barbara. / Christopher. / You came. / I told you I would. — AR — Invention. The chapter’s most-asserted dialogue moment. The lines are committed in the v2 planning document’s planning for the matched-endings discipline.

  • The second exchange: I have been waiting for you on this bench for thirty-five days. / I have been at sea for one hundred and six days. / I know. / I do not think you do, quite, yet. But you will. / Yes. — AR — Invention.

  • Christopher’s not embracing her on the public pier; Hans behind them with the trunk; Mr. Eldridge at the head of the gangway not calling out any farewell — AR — Invention. Consistent with the documented Reformed-Protestant Swiss-Bernese formality of Christopher’s documented background and the documented English-gentlewoman discretion of Barbara’s.

  • The eleven-day interval: Eleven days. The twenty-second of February. That is a Monday. That is a Monday. — P / AR — The marriage date of 22 February 1714 is documented at primary-source level (the Cooper Street church register; the Bible inscription). The eleven-day interval is calibrated from the documented 22 February to the chapter’s invented 11 February arrival; if the arrival date were different, the interval would shift accordingly, but the documented marriage date is fixed. That is a Monday is calendar-correct (22 February 1714 Old Style was a Monday).

  • The marriage at the Cooper Street church on Monday 22 February 1714, the Reverend Gideon Johnston officiating — P — Documented at primary-source level (the church register entry; the Bible inscription). The Cooper Street church is the documented commissary’s church at Charleston (S). The Reverend Gideon Johnston is the documented officiating commissary (S; see “(4) The dock scene and the marriage” framing note above).

  • The witnesses: Mrs. Houghton and Mr. Pelletier for the bride; Hans and a Charleston merchant named Allston for the groom; Mr. Eldridge in the second pew — AR — The names of the witnesses are not in the project record. The bridal-side witnesses being drawn from the ship’s company and the groom’s-side witnesses being drawn from the Charleston circle is period-plausible. The Charleston merchant Allston is invention; the surname is period-plausible (the Allstons were a documented Charleston family).

  • Susannah Fenwick attending in a borrowed dress on the arm of Mrs. Bellinger; Mrs. Bellinger having collected her from the ship six days earlier — AR — Invention. Period-plausible.

  • The formal Bible fly-leaf inscription in French, in Christopher’s hand: Christophe de Graffenried, fils du Baron Christophe V de Bernberg, époux. Barbara Tempest née Needham, de Wymondley en Hertfordshire, épouse. Mariés en l’église du commissaire à Charleston, en Caroline du Sud, le vingt-deuxième jour de février, 1714. — P — Documented at primary-source level (the de Graffenried family Bible fly-leaf; 1925 History, p. 147). This is the chapter’s central documentary anchor.

  • The smaller-hand English coda She came underneath the formal French inscription — AR — Invention. See “(4) The dock scene and the marriage” framing note above. The two English words are the chapter’s title-bearing close and answer Barbara’s I am coming of Chapter 5 §5.9 / §5.10. They are not part of the documented inscription.

  • The Narrator’s gloss describing the small upper room at Mrs. Brewton’s on Tradd Street, Christopher’s wife asleep in the chair by the window, the candle burning down, the Charleston harbor visible through the diamond panes a quarter-mile to the east — AR — Invention. The chapter’s closing image.

Open research items flagged by this chapter The chapter raises four discrete documentary-research targets that, if pursued and resolved, would substantially tighten the audit trail. Three carry over from Chapter 5’s research-items list; one is new in Chapter 6.

  • The Cooper Street church register at Charleston for 22 February 1714. A search for the surviving marriage entry. Would confirm the documented marriage record at primary-source level (the project record currently knows of the marriage via the 1925 History’s citation of the Bible inscription; a direct inspection of the church register would provide independent corroboration and might preserve the witness names, the time of day, and any additional ceremonial detail).

  • The de Graffenried family Bible fly-leaf. A photographic inspection of the surviving fly-leaf inscription. Would confirm the documented French entry at primary-source level (currently quoted via the 1925 History) and would either confirm or refute the chapter’s She came English coda. The de Graffenried family Bible’s present location should be traceable through the ECU and UNC de Graffenried collections (already named in the chapter’s CT-070 status note).

  • The Customs House Gravesend departures records and Charleston Custom House arrival manifests for October 1713 – February 1714. A search for the Charleston-bound brigantine that carried Barbara. Would either confirm a real ship-name and captain (replacing the invented Hopewell and Mr. Eldridge) or document at primary-source level that no specific vessel can be identified, in which case the chapter’s reconstruction stands.

  • The Bishop of London’s papers at Fulham Palace, Reverend Gideon Johnston correspondence 1713-1714. A search for any Johnston correspondence that might mention a Christopher de Graffenried letter requesting marriage facilitation in late 1713 or early 1714. Would either confirm Christopher’s documented contact with Johnston (currently rendered via the §6.3 invented letter) or document at primary-source level that no such contact survives in the Johnston papers.

None of these are blockers. The chapter is complete and internally consistent without them. They are flagged here as the obvious next moves in the chapter’s documentary backfilling.
