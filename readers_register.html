The family at a glance

The Searcy story is a migration. The deepest lines begin in the English gentry of Hertfordshire — the Needham family of Wymondley, documented in the Heralds' Visitations back to a Derbyshire origin around 1490 — and in the Swiss patriciate of Bern, where the de Graffenried line runs back to Peter, treasurer of the city in 1387. Those two old-world lines meet in colonial America when Christopher de Graffenried, son of the founder of New Bern, North Carolina, married an Englishwoman of the Needham family.

From there the family moves the way so many Southern families did: into colonial Virginia by the early 1700s, down into North Carolina (the Searcys were in Granville County by 1748), and then westward and southward across the frontier — through Tennessee and Georgia and into the raw new counties of Mississippi and Alabama in the middle 1800s. The Searcy, Rorie, and Marlar lines converged in one twenty-mile corner of northeast Mississippi; the Daniel, Lovvorn, Mitchem, and Brown lines came down through Georgia and the Carolinas into Alabama. DeWayne was born in Birmingham in 1968, the point where all of it comes together.

The DNA confirms the paper. The Searcy male line carries a documented Y-DNA patriline match; the maternal line — traced through Bonnie Daniel, Dorothy Mitchem, and the Brown women — carries its own mitochondrial signature; and along the way the research established four ancestors as Patriots of the American Revolution, the basis for four separate DAR lineage paths.

Part III · DNA Cousins & Genetic Matches

Confirmation from an entirely independent direction. Because these matches are living people, they are summarized here rather than listed by name.

What the DNA shows

The paper trail has been confirmed genetically on three fronts. On the Searcy patriline, Y-DNA testing places the male line in a defined haplogroup shared with a cohort of documented Searcy-line cousins — the independent proof that the surname descends as the records say. On the maternal line, mitochondrial testing carries its own signature down through the Daniel, Mitchem, and Brown women. And across the wider tree, dozens of autosomal cousins cluster cleanly onto the expected family lines — Searcy, Daniel, DeGraffenreid, Rorie, and Marlar — each one an independent vote for the same paper trail.

The full match list, with kit identifiers and shared-DNA measurements, lives in the private research binder. It is kept off the public record out of respect for the living people who tested.
