In the spring of 1658, in the priory house at Little Wymondley, a man named George Needham — who would, in the historian’s notation, be George Senior, to distinguish him from his son George Junior and his grandson George III who would in due course succeed to the seat — took up the headship of his family at thirty-nine years old.

He was a known quantity. He had been at the priory all his life. He had been baptized in the parish chapel on the sixteenth of August 1618 by John Marshall, the curate of Wymondley Parva, in the same chapel where his father Eustace had been baptized in 1595 and where his great-grandfather John had been baptized in the 1520s. He had been the sixteen-year-old heir of the 1634 visitation — recorded in Sir Richard St George’s pedigree as George Needham, son and heir, 16 years old, 1634 — the same age his great-grandfather John had been at Robert Cooke’s visitation sixty-two years before. He had been at the priory through the Civil War as a young man in his mid-twenties, paying the Parliament’s assessment-tax tallies on the fly-leaves of his great-great-great-grandfather’s pay-book and learning the working details of an estate’s seasonal management under his father’s hand. He had married, by 1655 or thereabouts, a woman named Barbara — the Barbara of his 1666/67 will, the woman whose maiden surname the project’s working record has not yet recovered, the mother of his seven children. He had a son named George — George Junior, the heir, then about three years old — and possibly already an infant daughter named Barbara after her mother.

He was, like his father Eustace, not a remarkable man. He was the third-generation Wymondley lord in unbroken succession by descent if you counted from James the Surveyor, and the eighth-generation in the line if you counted from John of Needham Grange in Derbyshire. He had no Court connection. He had no Privy Council relation. He had no seat in Parliament. He had never been knighted. He had no public profile. He was a steady working manager of a steady working estate that had survived the Civil War in his father’s hand and would now have to survive the next phase of the English seventeenth century in his.

The next phase, when it came, was the Restoration.

The country returns to itself

George Senior’s first two years as the head of the family were spent in the long pause between Cromwell’s death and Charles II’s return.

Cromwell had died at Whitehall on the third of September 1658, four to six months after Eustace’s burial. His son Richard had been proclaimed Lord Protector the same day. The proclamation had been ceremonial; the office had not. Richard Cromwell was thirty-one years old, a man of moderate ability and no military authority, and the army that had been his father’s working instrument of government broke with him within nine months. The Protectorate was dissolved by the army in April 1659. The Rump Parliament was reassembled in May. A confused interregnum of military rule followed through the summer and autumn. General George Monck — the cautious Scottish commander of the army of occupation in Scotland — marched south from Coldstream with his army in the first week of January 1660 and reached London at the start of February. The Long Parliament was dissolved by its own members in March. The Convention Parliament met in April. Charles II was invited to return. He landed at Dover on the twenty-fifth of May 1660.

George Senior at the priory followed all of this through the same channels his father had used: the printed pamphlets and newsbooks that came up from London by the carter on Mondays and Thursdays, the letters from cousins in Welwyn and Bedfordshire and London, the conversation at the Hitchin market on Tuesdays and Saturdays. He took no part in any of it. The family’s posture of non-engagement that Eustace had calculated in October 1640 and had sustained for the rest of his life remained operative. George Senior had no instinct to break with it. He had been raised inside it. He continued it.

What the Restoration brought to the priory, in the working sense, was the return of the Established Church.

The Book of Common Prayer, suspended since January 1645, was restored by the Act of Uniformity of August 1662. The bishops were reappointed; the parish ministers were required to subscribe to the renewed Anglican discipline; those who refused — about two thousand of the country’s parochial clergy — were ejected at Bartholomew 1662 in the event that became known as the Great Ejection. The Hertfordshire ejections were substantial. Andrew Cater, the minister at Graveley-cum-Chivesfield three-quarters of a mile from the priory, was one of the ejected. Other neighboring parish ministers in the Hitchin–Stevenage circle were ejected with him. The post-1662 Hertfordshire Dissenter community that would, by the 1690s, sustain the substantial Baptist and Independent congregations at Hitchin, Stevenage, and Ware, dates its formal beginnings to the Bartholomew Day of 1662.

For George Senior at Wymondley, the Restoration’s parish-life changes were a re-orientation rather than a disruption. The Wymondley Parva curate of the 1650s, whose Commonwealth-era practice had been Directory-based on Sundays and Prayer Book-based privately in the household, simply moved the Prayer Book practice back into the parish church. The family’s pew at the front of the nave operated as it had before the wars. The chapel within the priory house operated as it had before. The 1595 Dyer bell at Wymondley Parva rang for morning prayer in the same patterns it had rung in under Eustace’s working life. The Wilshamstead tithes came in. The Michaelmas rents were paid. The roof of the chapel was re-leaded again, in 1661 or 1662, with the same kind of money George Senior had been managing for his father across the Commonwealth years.

The country around the priory was not so quietly accommodated. The 1662 Act of Uniformity was followed by the 1664 Conventicle Act and the 1665 Five Mile Act — the so-called Clarendon Code, a working framework of post-Restoration religious penalty designed to keep the Dissenter congregations underground and the ejected ministers away from their old parishes. The Hertfordshire Dissenters around Wymondley — at Hitchin, at Tilehouse Street, at Stevenage, at the smaller villages of Preston and Gosmore and Langley — operated through the 1660s and 1670s under the Code’s working pressure, meeting in private houses, in barns, in remote field locations, with the local justices of the peace fining them when they were caught and the Hertfordshire Sessions records of the period preserving the working penalties in some detail.

George Senior was not a justice of the peace. He was not a county magistrate. He took no part in the Code’s working enforcement, which would have been the standard role of an Anglican gentleman of his standing if he had been inclined to take it up. The Wymondley priory’s quiet posture — the lay impropriator of an Anglican parish without active engagement in the suppression of the surrounding Dissent — was the same posture his father had calculated in the Civil War. The family was Anglican. The family was not aggressively Anglican. The family let the Dissenters do their thing in the neighboring villages and the family did its thing in the chapel.

The Restoration also brought, in the broader political sense, an unsettled rather than a settled return. Charles II reigned for twenty-five years, from 1660 to 1685. His reign was punctuated by the Anglo-Dutch Wars (1665–67 and 1672–74), the Great Plague of 1665, the Great Fire of London in September 1666, the Cabal ministry of the late 1660s, the Test Acts of 1673 and 1678 that excluded Catholics from public office, the Popish Plot crisis of 1678–81 with its mass arrests and executions, the Exclusion Crisis of 1679–81 attempting to bar the king’s Catholic brother James from the succession, the Rye House Plot of 1683, and the king’s death in February 1685 leaving the throne to his Catholic brother James II. James II’s reign — three and a half years — would end in his flight to France in December 1688 and the Glorious Revolution that brought William and Mary to the throne in February 1689. Through all of these national convulsions the country was, at the gentry level, working out what kind of England it was going to be. The settled Stuart constitutional shape of the early seventeenth century was not coming back; what was coming, slowly, was the parliamentary-monarchical balance that would be more or less locked in by the 1689 Bill of Rights.

The Wymondley Nedhams sat through all of this in their priory three miles south of Hitchin. They had no part in any of the high political crises. Their name does not appear on any of the Hertfordshire Sessions Indictments of Catholics, on the Exclusion Crisis loyal addresses, on the Rye House Plot informants’ rolls, on the 1685 Monmouth Rebellion lists. They were not players. They were spectators. They were spectators with a working estate, an armigerous house, and a chapel within the priory walls in which they baptized their children and buried their dead.

George Senior across the 1640s and the long Stuart middle years was building his family.

He had married, very probably in 1640 or 1641 in the months immediately before the Civil War began — when he was twenty-two or twenty-three, of marriageable age, the working heir of the priory under his father Eustace, with the country sliding toward open breach but the gentry of Hertfordshire still able to make routine marital arrangements — a woman named Barbara. Her maiden surname is one of the project’s continuing open research threads; the documentary record preserves her Christian name in the 1666/67 will but not her parentage. The recurrence of Fitch as the Christian name of George Senior’s second son in the same will is suggestive — Tudor and Stuart families often carried maternal surnames forward as boys’ Christian names — and the Fitch family of Essex and Hertfordshire is one candidate, but nothing has yet been documented at primary tier.

She bore him seven children across the next two decades. The 1666/67 will names them in the formal roster: four sons (George Junior the heir, Fitch the second son, James the third, Maurice the fourth) and three daughters (Barbara the eldest, named after her mother; Anne; Elizabeth). The eldest child Barbara was born probably in the early-to-mid 1640s, in the war years, in the same priory bedroom her grandfather Eustace had been born in fifty years before. The youngest of the seven children was Maurice, born in the late 1640s or early 1650s — the working calculation, given that Maurice would father his own daughter Barbara Tempest Needham in 1685, places his own birth somewhere around 1647 to 1655 — and the spacing of the other five children fits in between. George Junior the heir would have been born around 1645, would have been the small boy whose existence steadied Eustace’s calculation of the family’s future across the late 1640s and 1650s, and would in due course be the cousin who took the priory after George Senior’s death.

His wife Barbara — about whom the project’s documentary record preserves a name and not a maiden surname, a parental role and not a lineage — bore the seven children, ran the priory household across the long Stuart twilight, and outlived George Senior’s death in 1679 by some years, dying at the priory in the late 1680s or 1690s and being buried in the chancel with the rest of the family. Her maiden surname was Fitch. The project's long-running research thread on it closed in May of 2026, on the 1671 Chancery bill Nedham v Nedham (TNA C 7/503/16), which names the four sons of George Senior and Barbara and, in doing so, names her.

What can be said about Barbara wife of George Senior is that she was the mother of seven Wymondley children including the fourth son Maurice who would, in 1685, baptize his own daughter at Great Wymondley parish church and name her Barbara Tempest Needham — the Barbara of the granddaughter's name being a name carried forward from Barbara Fitch to her granddaughter through Maurice, in the same pattern by which the Christian name George was carried forward from generation to generation in the senior line. The Barbara of this chapter is the grandmother. The Barbara of this book is her granddaughter. The name was a Wymondley name across two generations.

She is therefore, in the most literal genealogical sense, the namesake of the daughter at the heart of this book.

By 1666, with the priory’s working life settled into the Restoration’s framework, with seven children in residence, with the country having survived the 1665 Plague and (in September of that year) the Great Fire of London, George Senior sat down to write his will.

The fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 776

George Senior’s working management of the priory across the 1660s was, like his father’s before him, almost entirely undocumented at public record level.

He held no Crown office. He served on no royal commission. He sat in no Parliament. He served, when he was called, on the lower-tier local instruments — parish vestry, perhaps the occasional hundred board, certainly the working business of the lay impropriator at Wymondley Parva, paying the curate out of the appropriated tithes and managing the parish church’s working economy. He kept the rectory of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire as the family’s working income property in addition to the priory estate. He held Graveley Hall — the second manor his father had acquired in 1633 or 1637 — as part of the family’s combined holdings. He was Lord of the Manors of Little Wymondley and Graveley Hall, the title that would in due course be inscribed on his tombstone.

What survives of his working life is, again, on the fly-leaves of his great-great-great-grandfather’s pay-books.

In 1655, three years before his father’s death and during the late Cromwellian Protectorate, George Senior had written a set of private household accounts on the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 776 — the second volume of James Nedeham’s pay-books, carrying the Surveyor’s office working accounts for the year 1532–33. The 1655 entries cover the kind of small ordinary household expenditure a thirty-seven-year-old gentleman in residence with his wife and young children would have been making in the working priory: payments to servants, the cost of cloth bought at the Hitchin market, the bailiff’s weekly wages, the small purchases of household provisions that fell outside the working estate’s self-supplied agricultural produce. The Bodleian catalogue, in Macray’s late-Victorian description, preserves the entries as fly-leaf marginalia at the front of the volume.

The 1655 accounts are, like the 1643–44 tax memoranda on the fly-leaves of D. 778, the working scratch-paper of a Hertfordshire country gentleman managing his daily affairs in the book that happened to be at hand. They tell us, in their existence, that George Senior was at the priory in 1655; that he was managing the household himself rather than delegating it entirely to his wife or his housekeeper; that he was the kind of man who kept accounts on whatever blank paper was available rather than maintaining a separate dedicated household-account book; and that the working manuscripts of his great-great-great-grandfather were available to him for personal use rather than being treated as the documentary treasures they would later become.

The same pattern would recur in 1668, when George Senior wrote his Wilshamstead rectorial-tithes accounts on the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 782 — the eighth volume of the run, carrying James’s pay-book entries for 1535–36 and 1541–42 including the lead account from Barking Abbey. The 1668 accounts at the front of D. 782 cover the tithe receipts from Wilshamstead for that year — a working summary of the parsonage income from a Bedfordshire parish twenty-two miles north of the priory, brought up to Wymondley by the carter who had been making the run for thirty years. The pay-books were now serving the family across three explicitly documented working uses by George Senior across the span of a working life: 1643–44 (tax tallies, D. 778), 1655 (household accounts, D. 776), 1668 (rectorial tithes, D. 782). Three points spanning twenty-five years.

What this tells us about George Senior, as a portrait by accumulation, is that he was a settled and orderly man. He kept his books. He wrote in a careful hand. He used good paper when he had it. He recorded his income and his expenditure in the way the working gentleman of his class and generation was supposed to. He did not, like his father Eustace, attempt to navigate political crises by absenting himself from them; he was, on the evidence of the surviving fly-leaves, a man who simply did not encounter political crises at all. The Restoration came; he conformed. The Conventicle Act came; he ignored it. The Five Mile Act came; he ignored that too. He went to the parish church on Sundays. He baptized his children. He raised them. He paid his taxes. He kept his estate.

The country went through the Plague.

In the spring and summer of 1665, beginning in the spring with a small number of cases in the London parishes east of the City and accelerating through the hot months of June, July, and August into a full epidemic with several thousand burials a week at the height of the outbreak in September, the bubonic plague returned to England for what would prove to be its last great visitation. The London death toll across the year was something like seventy or eighty thousand, by the most commonly cited estimates — about one in five of the city’s population. The plague spread out of London along the great roads through the home counties. The country gentry on the great north road and the country gentry on the Cambridge road and the country gentry on the Holyhead road all saw the plague pass through their parishes in the summer of 1665. The Hertfordshire parishes along the Great North Road — Hatfield, Hertford, Welwyn, Stevenage, Hitchin — registered increased burial counts that summer that the parish historians of the eighteenth century would, looking back, attribute to the same epidemic.

Wymondley parish church’s burial register for the summer of 1665 has not been recovered cleanly. Some Hertfordshire parishes were hit harder than others; some were spared altogether. The priory at Little Wymondley, three miles south of Hitchin and twenty-five miles north of London, was at the edge of the epidemic’s reach rather than at its center. What the household’s working response to the plague would have been is the standard response of a country gentry household in the summer of 1665: bring no strangers in, send no servants out except for essential errands, watch the parish-church burial book on Sundays for the names of those who had been buried that week, pray daily that the plague would not turn south from Hitchin and come up the road to Wymondley.

The plague did not, in the event, come up the road. The priory’s working life through the summer of 1665 was the working life of a household at the edge of a calamity that did not quite reach it. The household survived. The seven children survived. The wife Barbara survived. The servants survived. The bailiff — by 1665 a man in his middle forties, the second-generation Hitchin family bailiff who had succeeded his uncle a decade before — survived. The cousin households in Welwyn and Lockleys and Harlington survived. The Wymondley Nedhams were spared the loss of any documented family member in the 1665 plague year.

The fire came next.

On the night of the first of September 1666 — a Sunday — a fire started in a bakery in Pudding Lane in the City of London, spread to the adjacent timber-and-thatch buildings, was driven westward by a strong east wind across the next three days, and consumed approximately three-quarters of the old walled City and substantial areas beyond it before it was contained on the fifth of September. Old St Paul’s Cathedral was destroyed. Eighty-seven parish churches were destroyed. Some thirteen thousand houses were destroyed. Several hundred Londoners died in the fire itself, though the loss of life was strikingly small for the scale of physical destruction. The City’s working economy was destroyed for the better part of a year. The rebuilding would take the better part of a decade and would, under Sir Christopher Wren’s hand, produce the City churches and St Paul’s that still stand today.

The news of the Fire reached Wymondley by Monday afternoon — twenty-five miles up the Great North Road from London, with the smoke visible on the southern horizon from the higher fields of the priory’s estate by Tuesday morning. The carter brought up newsbook reports on Thursday with the standard week’s batch of mail. George Senior would have read the early accounts in his estate room, with his wife Barbara and his eldest children at his side. The reports were terrible. The London cousins of the Wymondley family — including the Pole connections through John’s eldest daughter Anne, who had married into the London Poles a hundred years before and whose descendants were probably still in the City — were unaccounted for. Several days passed before letters came up confirming that the Pole cousins (or whoever the family’s specific London connections of 1666 happened to be — the documentary record is silent on which specific London relatives the priory was in correspondence with by then) were safe but had lost their houses in the burned district.

The Fire was not, like the Plague, an event the priory’s working life sat at the edge of. The Fire was a national event of the kind that the country read and discussed and remembered for the rest of its lives. Samuel Pepys’s diary entries for the first week of September 1666 — among the most vivid first-person accounts in the English language of a major civil catastrophe — circulated then in the form of family letters and conversations among the country gentry, not as printed text; the diary would not be deciphered and published for another hundred and forty years. But the working content of those entries — the burning of London — was the talk of every gentry table in the home counties for the rest of 1666 and into 1667.

Six months after the Fire, on the fourth of March 1666/67 — the double-dated form used in seventeenth-century English documents for any date between the first of January and the twenty-fifth of March, indicating the Old Style year-beginning at Lady Day rather than the New Style at Christmas — George Senior sat down at the oak table in his estate room and wrote his will. — — —

The will and the feoffment

The will is preserved as PCC PROB 11/330/451 in the National Archives at Kew.

It was probated on the tenth of August 1679, on George Senior’s death. It is the central documentary instrument of his life.

It was written on the fourth of March 1666/67, six months after the Great Fire, three years after the Plague, twelve years after Cromwell’s death, seven years after the Restoration, in a country that had spent the previous decade and a half re-establishing itself as a stable polity and that was now, in 1666/67, somewhere in the middle of the long Stuart middle period — neither at the convulsions of the Civil War years behind it nor yet at the constitutional convulsions of the 1680s ahead of it. George Senior was forty-eight years old. He had been the head of the family for nine years. His seven children were all in being, ranging in age from the eldest Barbara at about twenty-three down to the youngest son Maurice at about twelve. His wife Barbara was alive and well. His brother Luke was alive and named in the will as a continuing member of the family circle. The estate was in working order. There was no immediate reason to write a will in March 1666/67 except the standard prudential one — that a man of forty-eight had reached the age at which gentry men of his generation were expected to put their affairs on paper.

The proximity to the Great Fire is suggestive but not necessarily explanatory. The Plague and the Fire together had reminded the country, in the most unmistakable terms, that calamity could come without warning. Many men in their forties and fifties across England wrote or revised their wills in 1666 and 1667 in response to the working sense of mortality that the two events had concentrated. George Senior may have been one of them. He may equally have been writing on a different prompt — the eldest daughter Barbara’s approaching marriage, perhaps, or a working revision of the estate’s settlement instruments — and the post-Fire dating may be coincidental. The documentary record does not specify the cause.

What the will says is straightforward.

It names George Senior’s wife Barbara as the principal beneficiary of his personal estate, with the right to live at the priory for the rest of her life. It names his four sons by their working portions: George Junior, the eldest, to inherit the priory and the lordship of Little Wymondley; Fitch, the second son, to receive a money portion (the amount is variously preserved in the working transcriptions but is on the order of several hundred pounds); James, the third son, to receive a similar money portion; Maurice, the fourth son, to receive five hundred pounds. It names the three daughters — Barbara the eldest, Anne, Elizabeth — each with their own marriage portions or settlements, the amounts again preserved in the working transcriptions in detail. It names brother Luke as a continuing presence in the family, with a small bequest. It names the household servants, in the standard testamentary pattern, with token bequests of a few shillings each. It names the parish church of Wymondley Parva and the parish church of Wilshamstead as recipients of small charitable bequests. It appoints George Junior and brother Luke as the will’s executors. It carries George Senior’s seal in red wax — the 1586 bend-and-bucks of Robert Cooke’s grant, eighty-one years old at the time of the will and still the family’s working visible signature.

The four-sons feoffment of 1667 is the will’s working companion instrument.

The feoffment was a separate property settlement, executed by deed rather than in the will, in which George Senior settled major portions of his estate on his four sons by inter vivos transfer. The instrument has not yet been retrieved at primary tier in the project’s research apparatus; what is documented is its existence and its working consequence. The standard interpretation, on the conventions of seventeenth-century gentry estate practice, is that the feoffment moved the substantive property out of the will’s working orbit and into a settled lifetime arrangement. The estate would not have to wait for probate to take effect on George Senior’s death; the four sons would have their portions already in being.

The will would, in this arrangement, function primarily as a residual instrument covering whatever had not been settled by deed — which would explain the will’s relatively brief and orderly structure.

What this means for Maurice — the fourth son, born about 1648 or 1650, by 1667 a young man of about seventeen to nineteen — is that his £500 portion was, by the four-sons feoffment, his working inheritance from his father. He would receive the money — or the income from invested principal generating the equivalent of the money — across the next twelve years, as the feoffment’s working terms made the portion available to him. The £500 was a substantial sum in 1667 — equivalent to perhaps £100,000 in modern early-twenty-first-century purchasing power, by the rough adjustments standard in early-modern economic history. It was not, however, the kind of money that would buy Maurice his own working country seat. It was the kind of money that would set him up as a younger son of a gentry house — with enough income to maintain himself as a gentleman, to marry, to keep a small household, to send a few children to school — but not enough to give him land in his own right. He would always be, by the working measure of his generation, the fourth son. He would always be at the priory rather than over it.

This is the central economic fact of Maurice’s adult life. He was a younger son with a money portion but no land. He would have to live with that fact for the rest of his working life. He would, as we shall see in the next section, manage it with the standard discretion of a Hertfordshire gentleman whose family had been at the priory for a hundred and thirty years and who was content to be the fourth son rather than the first.

George Senior in March 1666/67, having put his will on paper and having arranged the four-sons feoffment, had the satisfaction of a man whose working affairs were in order. He had twelve more years to live. The estate was settled. The priory was intact. The arms were on the door. The bend-and-bucks were on his seal. The wife at his right hand and the seven children growing through their teens and twenties around the household and the priory’s working life continued as it had continued for a hundred and twenty-eight years.

In 1668, a year after the will and the feoffment, he wrote his Wilshamstead rectorial-tithes accounts on the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 782. The Wilshamstead income that year had been £45 7s 4d — a respectable working sum, brought up from Bedfordshire by the carter in late autumn. He entered the figure in his careful hand at the front of the volume. The Surveyor’s office accounts of 1535–36 sat on the pages immediately following. The family was writing, again, into its own working manuscript. The eight-generation thread had not been broken.

He lived twelve more years. — — —

Maurice the fourth son

Maurice Needham, the fourth son of George Senior and Barbara, was the Wymondley son who would spend his life at the priory rather than over it.

He was, by the working measure of seventeenth-century English gentry inheritance, the standard fourth-son figure. He had a money portion of £500 from the 1666/67 will, an annuity income from the four-sons feoffment of 1667 of perhaps £30 a year on the £500 principal, a working education that had presumably included some Latin tutoring at the priory and possibly a year or two at one of the Inns of Court in his late teens or early twenties. The project's documentary record still does not confirm Inns of Court attendance for him at primary tier, and may never. But it now places him, at thirty or thereabouts, living in the parish of St Bride's — Fleet Street, between Ludgate and the Temple, the parish a man read law in and the parish a man practised it in. Whatever Maurice was doing with his life in the early 1680s, he was doing it in legal London. And he had, besides, the standard fourth-son's life trajectory: marry into another gentry house, set up a working household, raise a family on the income from the portion plus whatever marriage settlement the wife’s family contributed.

He married on Saturday the twenty-first of June 1684, at St. James Duke's Place in the City of London, by licence.

The entry is in the parish register, in the volume covering 1684 to 1690, between a marriage of the fifth of June and a marriage of the second of September, and it reads: Mauris Nedham of S[ain]t Brides London & Letisia Bowyer of Wymondley in Hertford-sheire weare married y[e] 21[s]t of June 1684 by Licence.

Four facts come out of that line and each one is worth holding.

He was living in London. Not at the priory, not on the £30 a year from the feoffment in a subsidiary house in the parish — at St Bride's, Fleet Street, the wedge of ground between Ludgate and the Temple, which is where you lived if your business was law or print or the money that moved between them. Maurice was thirty or thereabouts and he was a London man.

She was living at Wymondley. The clerk wrote of Wymondley in Hertford-sheire, and in a marriage register that phrase means residence and nothing else. It does not mean she was born there. She was not.

Her name was Bowyer — Letisia, the Latinate written form a clerk reached for; Lettice in a room, on a tongue, in a household.

And they married in neither of their own churches. St. James Duke's Place was the City's marriage manufactory. It claimed exemption from the Bishop of London's jurisdiction, and across thirty years it married something on the order of forty thousand couples by licence — couples from every parish in England and none, couples in a hurry, couples with nobody to object, couples who simply had no church that was theirs. Two Hertfordshire gentry children went there, with a licence, and were married by a clerk who had already written out eleven hundred that year and did not look up.

The volume will not pretend to know why.

There is a second document, and for a long time I read it wrong.

The 1684 Chancery case Nedham v Wingate is preserved at the National Archives at Kew under the call number C 10/214/65. It was filed the same year as the marriage. The plaintiffs are Maurice Nedham, Esq., and his wife Lettice, jointly. The defendant is Sir Francis Wingate, knight, of Harlington in the county of Bedford. The subject is a parcel of London-suburban land in the parish of St Martin in the Fields, Middlesex — a small holding that formed part of Lettice's marriage settlement and that Sir Francis was retaining under the Wingate estate's working management. Maurice and Lettice were suing for its conveyance to themselves. Sir Francis was resisting.

Every word of that is true and none of it makes her a Wingate.

But it looks as if it does. A woman named Lettice, joint plaintiff with her husband, suing a Wingate baronet for the land out of her own marriage portion, and a printed family history from 1925 sitting there saying her mother's maiden name being Wingate — and the two of them appearing to shake hands across two hundred and forty years. I promoted the attribution from PROBABLE to DOCUMENTED on the strength of it. I was wrong to, and the register says so.

What the suit actually establishes is narrower and, now, more interesting. Sir Francis Wingate was holding property out of the marriage settlement of a woman who was not a Wingate. Why he was is a live question and the project has not answered it. He may simply have been a trustee of the settlement — a routine arrangement, and the dull answer, and probably the right one; the Nedhams had a real Wingate connection through Eustace's second wife, and a Wingate trustee would surprise nobody. Or the disputed land may have come in from the Nedham side of the settlement rather than the bride's, in which case her family is not in the suit at all.

Or — and this is worth writing down even though it cannot yet be supported — a Wingate holding a Bowyer woman's settlement property may be the thread that explains how a Bowyer woman with no family in Hertfordshire came to be living at Wymondley in the first place. Somebody brought her into that county. Nobody wrote down who.

Where Maurice and Lettice lived after the marriage is the project’s central genealogical open question for this generation. The 1684 Chancery suit places them in the working orbit of London via the Middlesex property; their marriage settlement therefore included an interest in St Martin in the Fields. And the register removes the may: in June 1684 Maurice was resident at St Bride's. The London residence is not an inference any more. It is where he was living on his wedding day. But the 1685 baptism of their daughter Barbara at Great Wymondley parish church places them in residence at Wymondley by August of that year. The most economical reading is that they kept a London property as part of the marriage settlement (with the working dispute over its conveyance being the subject of the 1684 Chancery case) but established their primary residence at Wymondley, either at the priory itself in shared occupancy with the cousin George Junior (the eldest son and heir, who had inherited the priory on George Senior’s death in 1679) or at a working subsidiary property somewhere in the parish or the immediate neighborhood.

The chapter will work with the second reading. Maurice and Lettice were, by 1685, settled at Wymondley — perhaps in a subsidiary house attached to the priory complex, perhaps in a small leased property in the village, perhaps in the kind of arrangement by which a younger son’s household lived adjacent to the family seat with the working understanding that the eldest son’s household had precedence. The boundary between at the priory and near the priory in the 1680s was a working boundary, with overlapping households and shared use of the chapel and the cloister yard. Maurice’s family belonged to the priory’s working orbit even if it did not, strictly speaking, occupy the priory itself.

What they did at Wymondley in the 1680s and 1690s was raise their daughter. They appear to have had no other surviving children, though the project’s continuing research on the parish registers may yet recover others. Barbara was born to them in 1685 — twelve years after their marriage, by the chronology that places the marriage in the early 1680s; or four to six years after the marriage, by the alternative reading that places the marriage in the late 1670s. The dating of the marriage is a continuing project research thread; what is documented is the August 1685 baptism and the 1684 Chancery suit as the working anchors of Maurice and Lettice’s married life.

Maurice’s status at the priory through the late seventeenth century was that of the younger-son uncle — the man who lived in the working orbit of the priory under his elder brother George Junior’s hand, who was the working family figure that the priory’s other generations encountered at family meals and at the parish church on Sundays and at the household’s working gatherings, but who did not himself hold the priory or its tithes. He was the second-tier man within the senior line. He was the man who, on a Sunday morning in 1690 or 1695, would walk his daughter Barbara across the cloister yard to the chapel for the standard family attendance, would sit with her in the family pew at the parish church, would walk her back to whichever household they were living in afterward. He was the man whose working presence at the priory through Barbara’s childhood was the surrounding masculine fact of her early life.

He would live until 19 November 1712 — the date assigned in the working reconstruction of Chapter 5 v1.2, where Maurice’s death is dated to the cold week before Advent of that year, from a fever that came on after he returned from an extended stay at Lockleys. The dating is AR rather than primary; the project’s open research includes a focused search of the Hertfordshire burial registers for the Little Wymondley and Great Wymondley parishes in the window 1700 to 1714. But the dating sits inside the chapter’s working chronology of Barbara’s adult life. Maurice was alive across Barbara’s childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood; he was alive when Christopher de Graffenried first rode up to Wymondley in April 1710 to be presented; he was alive when the three-and-a-half-year engagement began. He died about thirteen months before Barbara wrote the three-word letter that sent her across the Atlantic.

That is in Chapter 5. The Maurice of this chapter is the fourth son, the priory’s living junior uncle, the father of the daughter we are about to meet. — — —

The death of George Senior, 1679

George Senior died at the priory at some point in the summer of 1679.

The exact date is not preserved at primary-source level in the project’s current record. What is documented is that the 1666/67 will was probated by the Prerogative Court of Canterbury on the tenth of August 1679 — meaning George Senior must have been alive at the writing of the will in March 1666/67, must have died at some point before the probate of August 1679, and most probably died in the weeks or months immediately preceding the probate, in the standard probate-court working practice of the period. The probable death is therefore June, July, or early August 1679. He was sixty or sixty-one years old.

The country at the moment of his death was, as it had been at his accession in 1658, in a period of political crisis. The Popish Plot — the alleged Catholic conspiracy uncovered by Titus Oates in the autumn of 1678 that had produced a wave of arrests, trials, and executions of suspected Catholic conspirators across 1678 and into 1679 — had been at its working height through the previous twelve months. Several thousand Catholics across England had been arrested; thirty-five had been executed; the country had been in a state of confessional panic for the better part of a year. The Exclusion Crisis, which would attempt to bar the king’s Catholic brother James from the succession, was just beginning. The first Exclusion Parliament had met in March 1679 and had been dissolved by Charles II in July. Hertfordshire’s gentry had been polarizing through the Plot and the Exclusion Crisis along the same lines they had polarized in the Civil War, with the Anglican-Court loyalist (Tory) faction and the Whig-Country opposition faction taking shape.

George Senior, like his father Eustace at the eve of the Civil War in 1640, took no part in any of this. The family does not appear on any of the Hertfordshire Popish Plot indictments, on the Exclusion Crisis loyal addresses, on the Tory or Whig pamphlet rolls of the period. He died, as he had lived, the quiet lay impropriator of Wymondley Parva and the lord of two Hertfordshire manors, in the same priory where his father had died twenty-one years before.

The funeral procession was the standard one for a Hertfordshire gentleman of his standing. The coffin was carried from the priory house to the chancel of the parish church by six of the senior tenants. The pall carried the bend-and-bucks of the 1586 grant. The household followed: the widow Barbara, the four sons George Junior and Fitch and James and Maurice with their respective wives or fiancées, the three daughters Barbara the eldest (by then in her mid-thirties and presumably married or unmarried in some arrangement the project record has not fully recovered), Anne, Elizabeth, the brother Luke, the Wingate cousins, the Bedfordshire alliances, the parish clergy, the senior tenants. The curate of Wymondley Parva read the standard Book of Common Prayer burial service — the Prayer Book restored, the Established Church operating, the Anglican working framework of the parish operating without disruption for the first time since George Senior’s own father’s funeral in 1658. The chancel was opened. George Senior was lowered into the prepared grave near his father Eustace and his grandfather George I and his great-grandfather John. The flagstones were re-set with a small mason’s mark 1679 cut into the stone. The mourners returned to the priory for the funeral meal.

George Junior — eldest son and heir, born about 1645, by 1679 a man of about thirty-four years, married for some years to a wife whose name the project record has imperfectly preserved (probably Anne, on the standard reading of the Wymondley descent), with at least one young son named George born in 1672 — became the head of the family.

He was, by the working measure of the seventeenth-century English gentry succession, the second of his generation to step into the role of priory lord. His cousin the second John of Welwyn had succeeded John of Welwyn at the cadet seat; he himself was now succeeding his father at the senior seat. The succession was clean. The estate was, as his father had arranged it through the 1666/67 will and the four-sons feoffment, already in working order. George Junior would simply continue what his father had been doing.

The cousin George — the son of George Junior born in 1672, by 1679 a small boy of seven — would in due course be the next generation of the priory’s headship, the man who would in 1700 succeed his own father George Junior to the seat. He is the cousin George in this book’s terminology — the third-named George of the seventeenth century, the George III who would, by Barbara Tempest Needham’s adult years in the early eighteenth century, be the cousin under whose roof she lived. He is the man whose three daughters — and three daughters only, no surviving sons — would in 1725 mark the end of the male Wymondley line. He is, in the project’s working dating, the priory’s last working Needham lord by full inheritance, since the priory after his death in 1725 would pass first to his daughters jointly and then in 1733 (by the marriage of the youngest, Martha, to Thomas Browne the future Garter King of Arms) out of the family altogether.

But that is the family’s far future from 1679. In 1679 the priory passed cleanly from George Senior to George Junior; the working life of the household continued; the next decade unfolded.

It is in that decade, in the household of Maurice the fourth son and Lettice his Wingate wife, that the daughter at the heart of this book was born. — — —

Barbary Nedham, 16 August 1685

On the sixteenth of August 1685 — a Sunday — Maurice and Lettice Needham brought their infant daughter to the parish church at Great Wymondley to be baptized.

The church was Great Wymondley, not Little Wymondley. The reason is geographic and parochial rather than family-political: Great Wymondley was the larger parish, with the substantially larger parish church (St Mary the Virgin, a Norman-foundation building rebuilt and extended through the medieval and early-modern periods); Little Wymondley was the smaller parish, with its chapel-of-ease that had been the priory church before the dissolution. The Needham family had been lay impropriators of the Wymondley Parva (Little Wymondley) rectory for over a century by 1685, but the Great Wymondley parish was where many of the family’s baptisms and marriages were recorded — particularly in cases where the family’s residence was in or near Great Wymondley rather than at the priory itself, which was technically in Little Wymondley parish.

The choice of Great Wymondley for the baptism is therefore a small piece of evidence about where Maurice and Lettice were living in 1685. If they were resident at the priory itself, the baptism would more naturally have been at the chapel-of-ease at Little Wymondley, in the same chapel where George Senior had been baptized in 1618 and where so many of the Wymondley Nedhams had received the sacrament across the previous century and a half. The fact that the baptism was at Great Wymondley instead suggests that Maurice and Lettice’s household was located in Great Wymondley parish — either at a property within the village of Great Wymondley itself or at a property on the boundary of the two parishes whose households conventionally took baptisms to the larger church. The specific Great Wymondley residence has not been identified at primary tier; it is one of the project’s continuing research threads.

The baptismal entry survives, indexed in the Findmypast England Births & Baptisms 1538- 1975 record set and corroborated by the Hertfordshire Baptisms transcripts. The entry reads, in the period’s spelling, Barbary Nedham — the spelling Barbary being the standard seventeenth-century English variant of Barbara, with the y ending rather than the a reflecting the early-modern English convention of feminine personal names. The entry gives the parents as Maurice Nedham and (variously rendered in the surviving transcriptions) Lettice or Lettis. The middle name Tempest, which the family-tradition record carries forward and which appears in Christopher VI de Graffenried’s family Bible entry of February 1714 (the marriage record at Charleston) as Barbara Tempest Needham, does not appear in the baptismal entry. Tempest was either a name carried in the family record but not formally registered at baptism, or a name added later — perhaps at confirmation, perhaps in adult use — to honor a maternal family connection to the Tempest line that the project’s continuing research has not yet fully identified.

She was, then, baptized as Barbary Nedham on the sixteenth of August 1685 at St Mary the Virgin, Great Wymondley, by the parish curate (whose name in the 1685 register would have been preserved but is not yet recovered in the project’s working file). She was the daughter of Maurice and Lettice. She was the niece of George Junior, the lord of the priory three-quarters of a mile away. She was the great-granddaughter of Eustace Needham who had carried the family through the Civil War. She was the great-great-granddaughter of George I who had erected the 1605 monument to James and John. She was the great-great-great-granddaughter of John Nedham who had signed the 1572 visitation. She was the great-great-great-great-great-great-granddaughter of John of Needham Grange in Derbyshire. The line at her baptism ran through six documented generations of recorded gentry residence, three of them at Wymondley, going back a hundred and forty-seven years.

She would, twenty-eight years later, walk out of the priory yard for the last time and ride east toward London and the Atlantic. She would marry Christopher VI de Graffenried at Charleston in February 1714. She would have children in Carolina. She would die in Virginia in the 1740s or 1750s, the exact date pending project research. Her descendants would carry her name forward through eleven generations to the writer of this book in Alabama in May 2026.

On the morning of the sixteenth of August 1685 she was an infant in her mother’s arms.

The baptismal service was the standard one of the post-Restoration Anglican Church — the office in the 1662 Book of Common Prayer, restored after the Commonwealth and operating in the Wymondley parish church for the past twenty-three years. The curate would have asked the parents and the godparents the standard questions: did they renounce the devil and all his works, did they affirm the Christian faith as the Apostles’ Creed expressed it, did they desire the child to be baptized into the faith. The parents and godparents would have given the standard answers. The curate would have taken the child in his arms, would have made the sign of the cross on her forehead, would have poured the small ewer of water over her head from the font, would have spoken the words: Barbary, I baptize thee in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. The water would have run down her face. She would have cried — most newborns did at the cold water on a hot August morning. The curate would have handed her back to her mother. The congregation would have spoken the Lord’s Prayer together. The service would have closed.

The household at Great Wymondley that morning would have included Maurice and Lettice and the infant; the godparents (whose identities the project has not recovered at primary tier, but who would by convention have been two godfathers and one godmother chosen from the family’s working network — perhaps George Junior the priory lord as one godfather, perhaps a Wingate cousin from Harlington or Lockleys as the second, perhaps Maurice’s elder sister Barbara the eldest as the godmother for whom the child was named); the parish curate of Great Wymondley; the small congregation of village householders who had come to Sunday service that morning. After the service the family party would have walked out of the church and along the road back to whichever Wymondley household Maurice and Lettice were living in, where a small family meal would have been laid out by Lettice’s working household women. The afternoon would have been spent in the company of the small immediate circle.

The child slept through most of it. She did not yet know her name. She had been alive for the better part of a month — born in late July 1685, by the standard interval between birth and the post-Restoration English Anglican baptism, which had returned to the more leisurely two- to four-week interval that had prevailed before the Commonwealth’s working pressure for immediate post-birth baptism. She was healthy. She was, by the standard of early-modern infant survival, in the working window of the second month of life when the first dangerous post-birth illnesses had largely passed and the chances of survival to her first birthday were the higher chances of an infant whose constitution had proven robust.

She was the chapter’s pivot moment. The whole book has been moving toward this child.

The seven generations of the Wymondley line before her — James, John, George I, Eustace, George Senior, George Junior at the senior line and the John-of-Welwyn-Eustace-George Senior path at the operating line, Maurice as her father — have all been the documented architecture of how she came to be born at Wymondley in 1685. The decisions that produced her were made before she was. James Nedeham’s bond to Cromwell in 1532 produced his appointment to the Surveyor’s office. The Surveyor’s office’s working life across the 1530s and 1540s produced the priory grant of 1538. The priory’s working life across the 1540s and 1550s produced the family’s Hertfordshire residence. John’s signature on the 1572 visitation produced the family’s documented identity. The Beckenham marriage of 1581 produced the next generation. The 1586 grant of arms produced the family’s visible heraldic standing. The 1605 monument produced the family’s memorial tradition. Eustace’s non-engagement in the Civil War produced the priory’s survival through the Commonwealth. George Senior’s 1666/67 will and 1667 feoffment produced Maurice’s £500 portion. Maurice's marriage, at a City church in June 1684, to a woman whose family we cannot name, produced the household in which Barbara was born.

The eight-generation Wymondley arc closes on this child.

The chapter’s remaining sections are her childhood at the priory through the 1690s, the death of her uncle George Junior in 1700 and her cousin George III’s accession, the slow gathering of her adult life as she enters her teens and twenties under the cousin’s roof, and the closing pages that calibrate the voice forward to the moment she sits at the writing-desk in the east-window of her bedroom and the chapter’s narrator becomes, for the first time, her own. — — —

A childhood at Wymondley

She grew up at the priory.

Not in the priory itself, in any one of its working senses — the great hall, the east range, the chambers above — because her parents’ household was a younger-son’s household, separate from but adjacent to the priory complex. But on its grounds, in its working orbit, with its bells and its cloister yard and its tithe barn and its chapel as the daily geography of her childhood years.

Maurice and Lettice’s household in the 1680s and 1690s was, on the chapter’s working reading, a property in the village of Great Wymondley or a subsidiary house attached to the priory complex itself. The specific building has not been identified at primary tier. What can be said about it is that it was within walking distance of the priory — the cloister yard was a five-minute walk from any property in the village of Great Wymondley, and an even shorter walk from any subsidiary property within the priory grounds — and that Barbara’s daily working life as a child was conducted across that walking distance, between her parents’ household and the priory’s working complex.

The priory in the 1690s was her uncle George Junior’s house. He lived there with his wife Anne — the project’s working name for her, the documentary record not having preserved her maiden surname at primary tier — and his children: the small George born in 1672, by the early 1690s a young man in his early twenties; and other children whose names and dates the project’s continuing research has not fully recovered. The cousin George — cousin George, as Barbara would have known him in family conversation, the young man fourteen years older than she was — would have been the working male presence at the priory across her childhood. He was the heir. He would, after his father’s death, become the priory’s lord.

Barbara would have been at the priory daily across her early years. The cousin household was the larger family circle; her own household with her parents and any siblings (no surviving siblings are documented at primary tier, though the project’s research on the Wymondley parish registers may yet recover them) was the smaller circle within it. She would have walked across to the priory at meals on family days. She would have walked across to the chapel on Sunday mornings for the family attendance. She would have walked across to the cloister yard on summer afternoons to play with her cousins — the cousin George’s younger sisters and any younger brothers, the children of the priory household, the children of her uncle and aunt.

The cloister yard was the children’s working outdoor space. It was an enclosed garden court, perhaps thirty paces by forty paces, on the south side of the priory house, bounded on three sides by the priory walls and on the fourth by the chapel-of-ease that had been the priory church before the dissolution. The yard had been the canons’ cloister walk in the medieval period, with a covered passageway running along the inside of the enclosing walls; the passageway had been removed in the priory’s adaptation to a country seat in the sixteenth century, and the yard by the 1690s was an open courtyard with paved stone walks around the perimeter and a small garden in the center. The garden had a sundial. The garden had rosebushes along the south wall. The garden had a small fishpond in the southeast corner that the children were supposed to stay away from but did not always stay away from. The wall along the south side carried a wall painting that the v9 working notes had identified as a late-sixteenth-century survival — an image of armoured soldiers, perhaps depicting a scene from the priory’s history or from a medieval romance, painted under one of John of Wymondley’s working renovations in the 1570s or 1580s. The painting was faded by the 1690s. The children would have known it as the old painting on the south wall.

The bell at the chapel rang the standard offices of the post-Restoration Anglican Church day. The 1595 bell of John Dyer rang for morning prayer at seven, for noon prayers at twelve, for evening prayer at four in winter and six in summer, for compline at nine on the evenings when the chaplain held it. The bell was audible across the entire priory complex and across the small village of Little Wymondley three hundred paces beyond. Barbara would have grown up to the working sound of the bell — the chapel bell at compline, the chapel bell at morning prayer, the bell that rang for a baptism (a quick rapid peal, twelve strokes), the bell that rang for a wedding (a longer pattern, alternating ringing-and-pause), the bell that rang for a funeral (a deep slow tolling, single strokes spaced by the bellringer’s working interval at thirty seconds). The bell was the working clock and the working sacrament of the priory’s daily life.

The Spanish chestnut tree stood in the orchard on the west side of the priory. It had been planted, by the project’s working reading of the v9 Urwick material, in the early or middle seventeenth century — perhaps by Eustace, perhaps by George Senior, perhaps by an earlier generation; the dating is imprecise. By the 1690s it was a substantial tree, perhaps eighty years old, with a heavily furrowed bark and the broad spreading crown the Castanea sativa develops at that age. The tree produced chestnuts each autumn that the household roasted on the kitchen fire in late October and November. The smell of roasting chestnuts in the priory kitchen in late autumn was one of the seasonal markers of Barbara’s childhood. The taste of roasting chestnuts — sweet, smoky, slightly bitter — was the seasonal taste of October at Wymondley.

The orchard also carried the box-tree hedge that bounded the priory grounds on the south. The box hedge had been planted, again at some date in the seventeenth century, as the standard boundary plantation of an enclosed country-house garden. The box trees by the 1690s were twenty-foot specimens — well-grown, with the dense small-leaved foliage that mature box develops, with the slightly catty intensity of evergreen-foliage smell that became more pronounced on summer evenings. Barbara would have walked the box-hedge path in the late summer afternoons with her mother or with her cousins, with the smell of the box in the warm air and the bees working the small flowers on the trees in June.

The conduit ran along the north side of the priory house. It was a stone-built water channel that brought water from a chalk spring north-east of the priory complex down to the kitchen door, where the spring-water filled a working trough used for cooking, washing, and the daily domestic water needs of the household. The conduit-head building — a small Tudor-arched stone structure over the spring’s source — stood at the high end of the run, perhaps a hundred paces north- east of the priory house. The water in the conduit was cold and metallic-tasting; chalk springs produce water of a particular clarity and mineral quality that the people who grow up drinking it can identify against any other water for the rest of their lives. The conduit-water was, by 1690, the family’s working domestic water for at least the past four generations of the priory’s life.

The tithe barn stood at the eastern edge of the grounds. It was a hundred and fifty years old by the 1690s — having been built by James the Surveyor’s carpenters in the summer of 1541 — and was still in working agricultural use, holding the grain tithes from the family’s lands. The barn was a daily presence on the eastern horizon of the priory yard. Barbara would have seen it from every window of the east range. She would have walked past it on the road to Hitchin. She would have known its working sounds — the workmen loading and unloading the tithes in late summer, the swallows nesting in the rafters from April through August, the slow creak of the great timber frame in a winter wind. The barn was the priory’s anchor structure at the eastern edge, the same way the chapel was the anchor at the south edge and the conduit-head was the anchor at the north-east edge.

This was her geography.

She grew up to it the way every Wymondley Needham child of the previous five generations had grown up to it — to the chapel bell at compline, to the cloister yard’s wall painting, to the box hedge’s summer smell, to the tithe barn at the eastern edge, to the conduit-water at the kitchen door, to the Spanish chestnut in the orchard and its autumn chestnuts on the kitchen fire. She had no reason, as a small child in the early 1690s, to think any of this would not be there for her whole life. The priory had been in the family for a hundred and fifty years by then. The bell had been there for a hundred years. The barn had been there for a hundred and fifty. The chestnut tree had been there for eighty. They were the permanent furniture of the world she had been born into.

She would not, in the event, see most of it after the autumn of 1713.

But she did not yet know that. She was a child. She had her parents nearby. She had her cousins at the priory. She had her uncle and aunt in the great hall. She had her grandmother — the dowager Barbara, George Senior’s widow, by the early 1690s an old woman of perhaps seventy, alive into Barbara’s first years and possibly into her early childhood — somewhere in the household. She had her name from her grandmother. She had her place at the family table. She had her bed in whichever bedroom her parents’ household had given her.

The bed, by the time she was eight or ten or twelve, was a bedroom in the east range of the priory itself.

The arrangement is the chapter’s working reconstruction. Sometime in the late 1690s, with the cousin George Junior in continued occupation of the priory and with Maurice and Lettice’s household in their separate establishment but in close working contact, Barbara — in her late childhood and early adolescence — took up a bedroom at the priory itself. The arrangement would have been the working accommodation of a niece by a uncle, with Barbara spending several nights a week at the priory in the company of her cousins and several nights a week at her parents’ household. The bedroom was in the east range, on the upper floor, with a tall window facing east into the cloister court and looking across to the chapel on the far side. The thirteenth-century arch that the v9 working notes had identified as the survival of the priory church’s south window stood in the wall just above the bedroom door, low enough that a tall adult had to duck slightly to pass under it, soft clunch limestone partially obscured by later wall fabric. Barbara would have ducked under it perhaps ten thousand times across her years at Wymondley. She would have come to think of it as the doorway to her room.

The window faced east.

In the mornings, the light from the cloister yard came in across the window-sill onto her desk — a small oak writing-desk her father had given her, with a writing-slope and a small drawer beneath. In the evenings, with the sun behind the priory house to the west, the cloister yard outside her window went into the soft shadow that summer evenings produce, with the chapel across the yard catching the last of the light on its west-facing wall and the bell at the top of the chapel tower visible against the late sky. She would, across the second decade of her life, spend more hours at the writing-desk in the east window than at any other working space in the world.

This is where the chapter’s voice begins, finally, to slide toward her own. — — —

1700

In the year 1700, George Junior — Barbara’s uncle, the priory’s working lord since George Senior’s death twenty-one years before — died.

The exact date is not preserved at primary tier in the project’s current record; the project’s working chronology places his death sometime in the year 1700, with the priory passing to his son George — the cousin George, born in 1672, then twenty-eight years old — in his place. The funeral was the standard one: the procession from the priory house to the chancel of the parish church, the Book of Common Prayer office, the chancel burial under the same flagstones where George Senior had been laid in 1679 and Eustace in 1658 and George I in 1626 and John in the early 1590s. The fourth generation of the senior line was now buried in the same chancel where its founder John had been buried a hundred and eight years before.

The cousin George III became the lord of the priory.

He was twenty-eight years old. He had been the heir for as long as he could remember, raised at the priory under his father George Junior’s working hand. He was married — to a wife whose name the project’s record has not yet recovered cleanly — and had begun his family. His three daughters by his wife would be the priory’s next generation: Dorothy, Susan, and Martha, born across the 1700s, the youngest of whom would in 1733 marry Thomas Browne the future Garter King of Arms and carry the priory out of the family altogether.

But that is the family’s far future. In the autumn of 1700, with George Junior just buried in the chancel and the cousin George just taking up the priory’s working management, the household was reorganizing itself around the new generation’s working presence.

For Barbara — fifteen years old in the autumn of 1700, by the chronology that places her birth in late July or early August 1685 — the working consequence was simple. Her uncle was dead. Her cousin, fourteen years older than she was, was now the priory’s lord. Her own parents Maurice and Lettice continued in their separate household, still in the working orbit of the priory but operating under the cousin’s headship rather than her uncle’s. Her bedroom in the east range of the priory was still her bedroom; the cousin’s working accommodation of his fifteen-year-old niece across the priory’s working life would, by the convention of the period, continue what her uncle’s working accommodation had been.

She continued at the writing-desk.

In the autumn of 1700 — the last autumn of the seventeenth century — she sat at the writing-desk in the east window of her bedroom and looked out across the cloister yard.

It was a Tuesday afternoon in October. The harvest had been in for a month. The Spanish chestnut in the orchard was beginning to drop its first nuts. The box hedge along the south boundary was settling into the deeper green it took on at the close of the year. The conduit-water was running. The chapel bell had rung for evening prayer at four. The cloister yard outside her window was in the soft shadow of the late afternoon, with the chapel across the way catching the last horizontal light of the day on its west-facing wall.

She had a book open in front of her. It was a copy of The Whole Duty of Man — the anonymous 1658 devotional text that had become, across the second half of the seventeenth century, the standard Protestant devotional reading of the English Anglican gentry household, going through dozens of editions by 1700 and being one of the working books on every gentry house’s shelf. The book had been her uncle’s; she had taken it from the priory library and had been reading it slowly across the autumn. She was on the chapter on Diligence in our Callings. The chapter argued, in the careful Restoration-Anglican register, that every Christian’s working life was a calling from God, that the calling carried with it duties of attention and labor and patience, and that the working virtue of a young woman of fifteen — though the chapter did not say that exactly, but said something general from which a young woman of fifteen could draw the inference — was to attend to the household duties of her own calling, to read her psalter and her Book of Common Prayer, to do her needlework, to be obedient to her parents, to be charitable to the poor, to keep her thoughts on the things of God.

She had been reading. She had stopped reading. She was looking out the window.

She did not know what to want.

She was the only child of Maurice and Lettice, by the working reading of the project’s research — no surviving siblings yet documented at primary tier. Her mother Lettice was alive, was probably in her forties, was in the household across the way. Her father Maurice was alive, fifty-two or fifty-three years old, the working junior uncle of the priory under the cousin’s headship. Her uncle George Junior was just buried, six weeks or two months before. Her cousin George was the new lord. Her grandmother Barbara — George Senior’s widow, the woman she had been named after — was dead, having died sometime in the 1690s when Barbara was a small child. The family she had been born into was a settled gentry household whose working life had not perceptibly changed in fifteen years.

She would, by the working convention of her sex and class, be married off in the next five years to a gentleman of an adjacent Hertfordshire or Bedfordshire house, with a marriage settlement negotiated through her father with the gentleman’s father, with a dowry from her own £500 inheritance from her father’s £500 portion via her birth as the (probably) only surviving child of the marriage. She would move into her husband’s house. She would bear children. She would manage a household of her own. She would die at some age between sixty and eighty in her husband’s house, and she would be buried in her husband’s chancel under her husband’s church’s flagstones, with her own family’s bend-and-bucks quartered alongside her husband’s arms on the funeral hatchment.

This was the working future of a gentry daughter of fifteen in October 1700.

She did not, at the writing-desk that Tuesday afternoon, want any of it.

She did not want any of it the way a young person of fifteen does not want most of the working future their world has arranged for them — not with any clear alternative in mind, not with any concrete plan to refuse, but with the small steady working uncertainty that they want something else and do not yet know what. She knew that her cousin George had three young daughters in the household and no son; she knew that the male line would, on the cousin’s evident contracting demographics, run out within a generation; she knew that the priory she had grown up in would, sometime in the next thirty or forty years, pass out of the family. She knew, in the careful working calculation of any gentry daughter of an armigerous house in its visible decline, that the world she had inherited was not going to last.

She did not yet know that this would matter.

She turned the page of The Whole Duty of Man. She read a sentence. She read it again because she had not really taken it in. She looked back at the cloister yard.

The chapel bell would ring for compline in three hours. The cousin’s youngest daughter — the cousin’s three-year-old, the family’s smallest — would be in the kitchen with the cook by now, helping (in the way a three-year-old helps) to set out the supper. The cook would be giving her a small piece of bread to keep her occupied. The candles would be lit in the great hall in another hour.

She thought about her father. He was at his desk in the household across the way, probably, doing whatever work his afternoons consisted of — household accounts, perhaps, or a letter to a cousin. He was fifty-two or fifty-three. He was healthy. He would, on the project’s working chronology, live another twelve years, dying in the cold week before Advent of 1712 of a fever that came on after he returned from an extended stay at Lockleys. She did not, in the autumn of 1700, know that. She thought of him as her father in the household across the way, a working presence in her daily life, a man whose face she had known longer than she had known anyone else’s face.

She thought about her mother. Lettice was careful — the kind of woman who had been raised somewhere to manage a household and who managed her own household well, and Barbara could not have told you where the somewhere was. She was also, by Barbara's reading at fifteen, a woman whose own life had not gone where she had once expected it to go. Barbara knew that much from the way her mother handled the good linen, which was the way a woman handles a thing she was brought up to expect more of.

Where her mother had been brought up, Barbara did not know, and had never quite thought to ask, in the way that children do not think to ask the enormous things. There were no grandparents on that side. There were no aunts on that side. Nobody came at Christmas from her mother's people. There was a name — Bowyer — and the name did not attach to a house or a county or a face, and Barbara had grown up inside a family that wrote every name it had into the front of a Bible and had never once noticed that half of her own was not in it. Lettice did not complain. Lettice did not, in any conversation Barbara could remember, say that her life had not gone where she had expected. But Barbara at fifteen could see that her mother’s expectations had bent.

She did not want her own expectations to bend that way.

She did not know yet what she wanted instead. She was fifteen. The closest she came, in the autumn of 1700, to wanting anything specific was the small steady pressure of not this, applied to the working future her family’s gentle-daughter machinery had drawn out in front of her.

The bell rang for compline at nine. She closed The Whole Duty of Man. She put it back on the small shelf above the writing-desk. She blew out the candle on the desk. She went to her bed. — — —

The east window

She kept the writing-desk in the east window of her bedroom across the years that followed.

Nothing in particular changed at the priory in the early 1700s. Her cousin George — the cousin she had been calling cousin George her whole life, the one fourteen years older than she was, the one whose family’s three small daughters she watched grow across the cloister yard — settled into his working management of the priory and continued what his father had done. The Wilshamstead tithes came in each autumn. The Michaelmas rents were paid. The harvest was brought in in August. The cousin’s wife managed the household. The cousin’s children played in the cloister yard.

Barbara was sixteen in 1701. She was seventeen in 1702. She was eighteen in 1703. She was nineteen in 1704. She was twenty in 1705. She was twenty-one in 1706. She was twenty-two in 1707. She was twenty-three in 1708. She was twenty-four in the autumn of 1709 — the autumn in which Baron Christoph von Graffenried, a Swiss aristocrat she had never heard of, was in London arranging his Carolina venture and looking for an English wife for his son.

She did not, across those nine years between 1700 and 1709, marry. The convention of her class held that a gentry daughter who had not married by twenty-two or twenty-three was beginning to be remarked on. By twenty-four she was, in the working terminology of the period, a spinster — not yet hopeless, not yet on the shelf, but in the working window in which her marriage prospects were narrowing. Her parents had not arranged a marriage for her. Maurice and Lettice had not, on any reading of the documentary record, made any visible effort to find her a husband through any of the standard Wingate or Bedfordshire or Hertfordshire connections that any other gentry parents of her class would have been working through in their daughter’s twentieth, twenty-first, twenty-second, twenty-third year.

This is the chapter’s small open question. Why she had not been married off.

The project’s working reading is that the family’s contracting demographics — the cousin George without a son, the male line visibly running out, the priory’s eventual passing out of the family becoming a matter of perhaps a generation rather than several — had begun to operate on the parents’ working calculations. Maurice was a younger son of a younger son; his daughter, by 1705 or 1706, was the family’s only direct descendant of George Senior’s line who was both alive and unmarried and at the working age for matrimony. The Wingate aunts at Lockleys, the Bedfordshire cousins at Harlington and Northill, the older relations who had grown up in the priory’s working orbit across the seventeenth century — all of them had watched the family’s male line contract and were beginning to think about Barbara as something other than the standard daughter to be married off. She was, by some readings, the family’s working future in a way that her brothers (had there been brothers) would otherwise have been.

The other reading is simpler: she had not wanted to.

Her parents had presented her, presumably, with one or two or three working candidates across the years between her seventeenth and her twenty-fourth birthdays. The candidates had not taken. She had said no, in the small careful way that a gentry daughter of an armigerous house could say no to a proposed match without making a public fuss — I am not ready, I am not yet sure, I would prefer to wait another year, in the working register of polite gentle refusal that did not foreclose the marriage market but did not commit her to it. The parents had accepted the no’s. The Wingate aunts at Lockleys had clucked. The cousin George’s wife had clucked. Barbara had continued at the priory.

She continued at the writing-desk in the east window.

She read more than her age and class ordinarily would have. She had read across the priory’s working library through her late teens — the books her uncle George Junior had left when he died; the books her cousin George kept; the books her father Maurice had brought from his own connections; the small library of devotional and historical and classical reading that any well-furnished gentry library of the period carried. She had read The Whole Duty of Man in full and most of its 1690s working successors. She had read the Geneva Bible at one period of her life and the Authorized Version at another. She had read Foxe’s Acts and Monuments in an abridged Restoration-era version. She had read most of Paradise Lost — Milton’s epic of the Fall, published in its expanded twelve-book version in 1674, by 1700 a standard piece of advanced reading in any well-stocked Anglican gentry library — though she had not, on her own reading, fully made out the theology of Books Three and Four. She had read the Book of Common Prayer in its 1662 form so often she could recite the daily offices from memory. She had read a small body of poetry — some Donne, some Herbert, some Vaughan, the Sacred Hymns of the late seventeenth century — that her uncle had kept on the shelves above his own writing-desk and that she had taken up over the years.

She wrote letters. Her cousins in Welwyn at Lockleys, her cousins at Harlington in Bedfordshire, her uncle’s old London connections — she kept up the standard correspondence of a young woman of her class, in the careful small clear hand her mother had taught her and that her mother’s mother had taught her mother. She wrote a careful capital letter with a small flourish at the top. She made her descenders cleanly. She sealed her letters with the priory’s working seal of the bend-and-bucks, in the small wax impression that had been the family’s visible signature for a hundred and twenty years.

She walked the priory grounds in the afternoons. She rode to Hitchin with one of the grooms on most Tuesdays for the market day, in the standard gentry-daughter convention of being seen at the market under appropriate male escort. She attended the parish church on Sundays in the family pew with the cousin’s household. She did her needlework in the great hall after supper. She watched the cousin’s three daughters grow — Dorothy, then Susan, then Martha — across her early twenties. She helped her mother with the household management when her mother needed her, which was not often.

She kept her room. She kept her desk. She kept her place at the east window.

In the long summer evenings she would sit at the desk in the bedroom and watch the cloister yard go into the soft shadow of the after-supper hours, with the chapel across the yard catching the last horizontal light on its west-facing wall and the small swallows working their late-evening sky flights over the tithe-barn roof at the eastern edge. The cousin’s youngest daughter Martha — by 1709 a small girl of three or four, the cousin’s third and last child — would sometimes be in the cloister yard with the household maid, playing the small games a three-year-old plays at the close of the day, with the maid working her own needlework on the bench by the chapel wall. Barbara would watch them from the window. The bell at the chapel would ring for compline at nine. The maid would call the small girl in. The cloister yard would empty into the dark.

· · ·

She would, in the autumn of 1709 — a Swiss baron she had not yet heard of in London, arranging a colonial venture she did not yet know about, asking the working London-Carolina circles whether they could find him an English gentry wife for his son — be twenty-four years old.

She would still be at the writing-desk in the east window.

She would have been waiting most of her life for something to happen.
