Was Death Preferable to the Gulag?

The question the prisoners lived inside — and the two great witnesses who came out of the same abyss and answered it in opposite ways.

Anyone who reads deeply into the camps eventually arrives at a question, usually with a flush of guilt at having thought it: given the torture, the starvation, the cold, and the slow unmaking by disease, wasn't a bullet the more merciful fate? It feels almost disrespectful to ask — a comfortable person weighing horrors he will never face. But it is not our question alone. The prisoners lived inside it, turned it over on the bunk at night, and two of the greatest witnesses the Gulag ever produced came out of the same abyss and answered it in flatly opposite ways. Their disagreement is the truest map we have of the problem.

The two witnesses

On one side stands Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn. For all that The Gulag Archipelago is a vast catalogue of cruelty, it arrives at something almost unbearable to modern ears — gratitude. "Bless you, prison, for having been in my life," he writes, because it was there, stripped of everything on a rotting bunk, that he believed he finally understood himself, and found the line for which he is most remembered: that the border between good and evil runs not between nations or classes but straight through every human heart, including his own. For Solzhenitsyn the suffering meant something; survival was not merely luck but the ground on which a truer man was rebuilt. He would answer the question with a hard no — death was not the better path, because the person who walked out had been made more real by the ordeal.

On the other side stands Varlam Shalamov, who served some seventeen years in Kolyma, the deadliest corner of the whole system, and who despised that redemptive reading with everything he had left. His Kolyma Tales insist the camp taught nothing — that it was a school of pure degradation where friendship, dignity, courage, and art all dissolved below a certain temperature and depth of hunger, and that the experience left behind no wisdom, only damage. To Shalamov the dead were, quietly, the ones who had been spared; survival was a matter of blind chance and often of small shames, and there was nothing whatever to bless. The two men corresponded, and then fell out precisely here — Shalamov would not lend his name to any account that made the camps a teacher.

Same inferno, opposite verdicts — and neither can be waved away, because both men paid for the right to speak in the only currency the question accepts.

It was almost never a choice

But the framing hides a crueler truth, and it is the first thing to say plainly: it was hardly ever a choice. Very few prisoners ever got to set the clean bullet against the slow years and pick. Whether you died was decided for you — by a quota drawn up in Moscow, by frostbite, by which brigade you drew that week, by a guard's mood on a bad morning. The horror is not only the suffering; it is the total theft of agency, so that "wouldn't death be better?" became a question a human being had to hold with no power to act on it. And in the rare moments when death was within reach, Solzhenitsyn noticed something strange and stubborn: suicide was less common than you would ever guess. The body keeps voting for life long after the mind has despaired — which is either the deepest proof of the human will, or, as Shalamov would answer coldly, merely biology refusing to quit, and no proof of anything at all.

The drowned and the saved

There is a further shadow that survivors themselves named, most piercingly Primo Levi writing of the Nazi camps in The Drowned and the Saved — and the moral structure carries straight across. It is the suspicion that the best did not survive: that the truly decent — the ones who shared their bread, who would not inform, who could not bring themselves to grab — went under first, and that survival sometimes demanded a hardness the survivor then had to live with for the rest of his life. This is what complicates the tender reading, "they survived to love and have children." It is true and it is beautiful, and it is also haunted, because the ones who lived to tell us often felt they had no clean right to be the ones telling us. Survival was not innocence. It was, frequently, a wound with a witness attached.

Survival as testimony

And yet. Every prisoner the system killed, it also tried to erase — no grave, no marker, no record, the person unmade as though they had never existed, which was the whole point of the machinery (see The Ledger of Terror). The ones who survived and loved and raised children and, above all, told the story did the single thing the entire apparatus was built to prevent: they remained, and they testified. Eugenia Ginzburg gave eighteen years to that machine and lived — and it is only because she lived that we have Journey into the Whirlwind at all. The psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, writing from the Nazi camps, put the mechanism of endurance simply: a person with a why to live can bear almost any how. For some, that why was a wife, a child, a manuscript composed in the head; for Ginzburg and Solzhenitsyn it became the vow to bear witness. That does not make the suffering worth it — no ledger balances that — but it means survival was never only endurance. It was defiance. The memoir on the shelf is a prisoner who outlived the state that tried to delete them.

No verdict from a warm room

So, was death preferable? The honest answer refuses to be tidy. For some prisoners, in some hours, yes — it was a mercy they genuinely longed for, and it would be a lie dressed as reverence to pretend otherwise. For others, the far side of the wire held everything that makes a life worth the name, and they crossed to it and built exactly that. Both are true at the same time, held in the same hand, and it is not the place of anyone reading this in a warm room to rank another human being's agony. What can be said is this: the deepest cruelty of the Gulag was not any single instrument of it, but that it forced ordinary people to become the kind of person who had to know, about themselves, which answer was theirs.

The camp asked each prisoner the one question no one should ever have to answer. That it could be asked at all is the measure of the crime; that some answered it and still chose to live, and love, and testify, is the measure of what it could not take.

Further reading: Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, The Gulag Archipelago (esp. Vol. II, "The Ascent"); Varlam Shalamov, Kolyma Tales; Eugenia Ginzburg, Journey into the Whirlwind and Within the Whirlwind; Primo Levi, The Drowned and the Saved (the Nazi camps, but the same moral country); Viktor Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning; and, for the tsarist prelude, Fyodor Dostoevsky, Notes from a Dead House.

A note on this one: unlike the guide's other essays, this question has no archival answer and never will — it is moral rather than factual, and it is offered in that spirit. The aim here is not to settle it but to hold it honestly, and to let the people who actually lived it — who disagreed with each other — do most of the speaking.
