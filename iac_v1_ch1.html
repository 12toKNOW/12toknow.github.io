In the summer of 1541, in a Hertfordshire field three miles south of the market town of Hitchin, a crew of carpenters was raising the timber frame of a great barn.

It would be a hundred feet long when they finished. Nine bays on either side. The principal posts were oak, brought down out of a wood the master of the works had bought four years earlier in South Mimms across the Middlesex border; the rafters and the wall plates and the tie beams were the standard sizes a working carpenter knew by sight. The frame would carry a tiled roof. The walls below would be timber-framed with brick infill, the brick coming from a kiln nearby. The barn was being built to hold the grain tithes of the manor of Little Wymondley, the lord of which had been, since the spring of 1538, a man named James Nedeham.

James was not at the worksite that summer. He was probably in London, or at one of the half-dozen royal building projects that he ran simultaneously as Surveyor of the King’s Works under Henry VIII. He had hired master carpenters to build his barn the way he had hired master carpenters all his life to build the king’s palaces, and he trusted them to do it. The frame went up. The roof went on. The barn was finished and standing by the end of 1541, or possibly in the spring of 1542. James lived two more years.

The barn is still there.

It is standing today, four hundred and eighty-five years after the carpenters finished it, in the same field three miles south of Hitchin, on the same priory grounds, at the same address — Priory Lane, Little Wymondley, Hertfordshire, SG4 7HD. It is now licensed for civil weddings. Up to two hundred and twenty guests can be seated under its hammer-beam roof. Brides and grooms have exchanged vows in it more than two thousand times since 1992. The current owners are a couple named Michelle and Bill Bacon, who took over the property at the beginning of 2026 and who would, almost certainly, be glad to know that a descendant of the man who commissioned the barn in 1541 is writing about him in Alabama in the spring of the same year.

This is the story of how the man who built that barn got there, and of the eight generations of his family that lived in the buildings he made before, in the autumn of 1713, a young woman named Barbara Tempest Needham walked out of the priory yard for the last time and rode east toward London and the Atlantic.

The London Carpenter’s Son

James Nedeham was born about 1490 in London.

His father was Christopher Nedeham, citizen and carpenter of London — the phrase that appears in the formal Bodleian Library biographical note on his son, and that fixes Christopher’s place in the City of London’s social order with precision. He was a citizen, meaning a freeman of the City, sworn into the liberties of London with the right to trade within the walls; and he was a carpenter, meaning he practiced the woodworking trade. Behind him stood a third detail that the Tudor heralds, sixty years later, would record at the top of his grandson’s pedigree: Christopher’s father — James’s grandfather — was John Needham of High Needham in Derbyshire, an obscure Peak District landholder whose family had been at a place called Needham Grange since at least the late fourteenth century.

One thing to clear before the rest of the story can run. James was the first Nedham at Wymondley. He was not the first Nedham in Hertfordshire. Two and a half centuries before him, a man named Luke Nedham of St Albans witnessed John of Langley’s foundation deed for the St Albans Grammar School — the deed is undated, but the related case in the Court of King’s Bench is dated to 1286 — and was named, twenty-two years later, as one of four citizen-sufferers in Abbot Hugh’s long handmill dispute with the borough, which ran from 1308 to the abbot’s death in 1326. Luke is in the Gesta Abbatum Monasterii Sancti Albani at the back of volume two, on page 154 of Riley’s Rolls Series edition, and in William Urwick’s Nonconformity in Hertfordshire at page 43. Whether he is a forebear of the Derbyshire line that produced James, or a separate Hertfordshire-rooted Nedham family entirely, the documentary record does not say. The surname’s presence in the county, however, runs deeper than the chapter would otherwise suggest.

The three generations form a recognizable Tudor social arc. A Derbyshire gentry family, modest but armigerous, sends a younger son south to London to learn a trade. The son joins the Carpenters’ Company, marries, takes up the freedom of the City, becomes a citizen and a tradesman. He raises his own son to follow him into the craft. The third generation — James, our man — rises out of the trade and into the king’s service, and from the king’s service back into the gentry his grandfather had quietly belonged to. By the end of his life he will style himself, in formal legal instruments, James Nedeham of Wymondley, Esquire.

He will hold a former Augustinian priory by letters patent from Henry VIII. His son will sign a herald’s pedigree to register the family’s arms in the fourteenth year of Elizabeth’s reign.

The arc takes three generations to complete, but it is a single movement. The Nedehams of London were not strangers to the gentry; they were a junior cadet line that had taken thirty years away from land to make money in trade, and that returned to land when the chance came. James was the chance.

He grew up in a house in London — the exact parish is not recorded, but probably one of the carpenters’ parishes north of the Thames, in the cluster of working-trade neighborhoods between St Paul’s and the eastern wall. His father worked with timber. His childhood was spent in the smell of sawdust and pitch and oak. He would have learned to handle a chisel before he could read; he would have known the names of the tools by sight before he was eight. By the time he was fourteen or fifteen he was apprenticed, almost certainly to his father or to one of his father’s colleagues in the trade. The apprenticeship would have run seven years.

In 1514, when he was about twenty-four, he was sworn into the freedom of the Worshipful Company of Carpenters, the London livery company that regulated his craft. The Carpenters’ Company in 1514 was one of the senior twelve City companies, with a hall, a livery, a master and wardens, an annual feast, and the authority to inspect work done within the City walls. To be sworn into the Company was to acquire the right to trade as a carpenter in London. James was now, formally, what his father had been: a citizen and carpenter of London.

Within a year he was a warden of the Company. The wardenship was an annual office, elected by the membership, and a young man elected to it at twenty-five — a year out of his apprenticeship — was a man being recognized by his peers as exceptional. James would be warden again in 1533, and he would serve as Master of the Company three times: in 1534, 1535, and 1542. The Company stayed close to him for the rest of his life. He died still a freeman of it.

The first project recorded under his name is in 1521, when he was about thirty-one years old. The Mercers’ Company — a richer and more politically connected City company than the Carpenters’, representing the import-export merchants in luxury textiles — hired him to design and build a louvre for their hall. A louvre, in Tudor English, was a roof-vent: a small turret built into the roof of a great hall to allow smoke from the central fire to escape, while also providing decorative finish above the gable. Building a louvre required a carpenter who understood roof framing, ventilation, and architectural ornament simultaneously. The Mercers’ commission, given to a young man only seven years out of his apprenticeship, established him.

Between 1522 and 1525 he was abroad as a military carpenter. The exact projects are not entirely clear from the surviving record, but he is documented as having served at Calais — the English-held enclave on the French coast that was the kingdom’s permanent military outpost on the Continent. Calais was a fortress town, fortified continuously by the English from the fourteenth century onward, and its defensive works required constant maintenance and improvement. A military carpenter at Calais worked on gun platforms, ramparts, gates, drawbridges, and the timber components of the curtain walls. James spent three years there, in his early thirties, learning how to build for war.

In 1525 he came home to England and was appointed a gunner at the Tower of London. The position was a Crown salary — modest, but secure, and tied to the central armaments establishment of the realm. The Tower in the 1520s was both a working arsenal and the symbolic heart of the Crown’s military power. A gunner was a senior artilleryman with responsibility for the maintenance and operation of the Tower’s cannon, and within a year James had been promoted to senior gunner. He was, by the late 1520s, a man with a foot in two professions: master carpenter in London, and senior gunner at the Tower.

It was the Tower posting that put him within reach of the men who would change his life.

The Road to Court

Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, in 1529, was the most powerful man in England below the king.

He was Archbishop of York, Lord Chancellor, Cardinal of the Roman Church, and the chief minister of Henry VIII’s government. He held more offices simultaneously than any English subject had ever held. He lived in extraordinary splendor at his great house at Hampton Court on the Thames, which he had built or rebuilt over the previous decade with the resources of his ecclesiastical and political revenues. He also held York Place at Westminster — the London residence of the Archbishops of York — which he had begun to rebuild as a second great house. Both houses required ongoing construction. Wolsey kept a building staff.

Sometime in the late 1520s, James Nedeham was hired by Wolsey to work on York Place.

The exact circumstances are not recorded — we have no contract, no warrant, no payment record from the Wolsey period — but the fact is documented in the working biographical traditions and confirmed by what happened next. Wolsey fell from power in October 1529. He was stripped of the chancellorship, banished from the king’s presence, and ordered to surrender his properties. York Place was taken from him; the king began rebuilding it as his own residence, which would become Whitehall Palace. The building staff that Wolsey had assembled was largely retained by the king. James Nedeham, who had been working for Wolsey, was now working for Henry.

This was the transitional moment. James was about thirty-nine years old. He had spent fifteen years in the City as a working carpenter, three years in Calais as a military carpenter, four years at the Tower as a gunner. He had built a louvre for the Mercers and galleries for the Marquess of Exeter at the great man’s London garden. He had worked at York Place under one of the most demanding patrons in England. He was now working directly for the king.

In March 1531, by royal warrant, he was appointed Master Carpenter of the King’s Works. The office was the senior craftsman position in the royal building establishment, with authority over all carpenters working on Crown projects. It was a major promotion. It came with a fee — a daily wage of twelve pence, which was twice the day-rate of an ordinary working carpenter — and with the right to oversee subordinates across multiple royal sites simultaneously.

In June of the following year, 1532, James executed a sealed bond in favor of Thomas Cromwell.

Cromwell in June 1532 was Master of the King’s Jewels — a Crown office that gave him control of the royal treasury of plate, jewels, and ceremonial objects — and was rising rapidly toward the chief ministership that he would consolidate over the next eighteen months. He was, in the summer of 1532, the man you needed if you wanted Crown advancement. The bond James gave him was for two hundred pounds. The amount is striking. In 1532, two hundred pounds was the annual income of a substantial country gentleman. It was four years’ wages for a master craftsman at the king’s day-rate. The bond was a formal financial obligation — a sealed instrument, conditional on some performance, by which James bound himself to pay Cromwell two hundred pounds if certain unspecified conditions were not met. The endorsement that would have specified the condition does not survive in full.

What survives is the bond itself, in the National Archives at Kew, catalogued as E 40/5944, dated 11 June 1532, and described in the catalog as the obligation of James Nedeham, the king’s master carpenter, to Thomas Cromwell, master or treasurer of the king’s jewels, for two hundred pounds. Whatever the specific condition, the document marks James as a man in Cromwell’s circle in the most consequential summer of Cromwell’s rise. Four months later, on 1 October 1532, James was appointed Surveyor of the King’s Works — the office at the head of the royal building establishment, above the Master Carpenter and the Master Mason, with overall administrative responsibility for every Crown construction project in the realm.

The succession of dates is too tight to be coincidence. June 1532: a £200 bond to Cromwell. October 1532: appointed Surveyor. James owed his promotion to Cromwell, in some part. He had been in Cromwell’s pocket, financially and politically, for at least four months when the appointment came through.

This was the deal he had made. It bought him the office that would define the rest of his life.

The Works in the 1530s

The English royal building program in the 1530s was the largest construction enterprise in northern Europe.

Henry VIII had inherited from his father a half-dozen working royal residences — the Tower of London, Westminster, Greenwich, Richmond, Windsor, Hampton Court. He would add to them, over the course of his reign, more royal residences than any English king before him had owned. He bought Hampton Court from the falling Wolsey in 1529 and made it his own. He took York Place from the fallen Wolsey and rebuilt it as Whitehall. He acquired or built or substantially expanded properties at Bridewell, St James’s, Nonsuch, Oatlands, Hatfield, Hunsdon, Enfield, Eltham, Woodstock, Dover, Leeds Castle, Ampthill, Grafton, the Manor of the More, Woking, and a dozen others. By the end of his reign, the Crown held more than fifty houses scattered across the south and midlands of England, and every one of them required maintenance, expansion, or active rebuilding.

All of this passed through the office of the Surveyor of the King’s Works.

On 1 October 1532, when James took up the post, the Surveyor’s office was a small administrative bureau with a clerk, a few subordinate craftsmen, and a permanent staff of perhaps a dozen people. By the time James died twelve years later, the office had grown to oversee a working roster of hundreds of named artificers and laborers active across two dozen sites simultaneously, with annual budgets in the thousands of pounds. The Victoria County History of Hertfordshire, in its volume on the Hundred of Broadwater, gives his style in its fullest three-fold form — accountant, surveyor-general and clerk of the king’s works. The accountantship was the financial spine of the office, which is what the eleven-volume Rawlinson series itself preserves; the surveyor-generalship was the architectural and engineering oversight; the clerkship was the administrative headship. He was all three at once. The growth was driven by Henry’s appetite. The administration was driven by James.

He kept a clerk named Antony Pontesbury, who described himself in the books as “clerk to the said accomptant.” Pontesbury wrote out the fair copies of every pay tally for the duration of James’s surveyorship. His handwriting fills the surviving pay-books — a careful, looped, slightly inclined hand that you can pick out across eleven volumes at the Bodleian Library at Oxford. Pontesbury was, in effect, James’s working partner. He sat at a desk somewhere in the King’s Works office — probably at Westminster, possibly at Whitehall once the rebuilding allowed it — and produced the documentary backbone of the royal building program for ten consecutive years.

Of the twelve annual pay-books that James and Pontesbury compiled between Michaelmas 1532 and James’s death in September 1544, ten survive. Eleven are at the Bodleian Library at Oxford, in the Rawlinson collection, shelfmarks D. 775 through D. 785; one further volume is at the National Archives at Kew, shelfmark E. 101/504/2; and a twelfth volume was rediscovered as recently as 2008, when it surfaced in the Foljambe family library at Osberton Hall and was acquired by the Bodleian at a Christie’s sale, where it is now MS Don. c. 206. Three additional volumes are held by the University of Nottingham. Across these volumes, the daily working life of the King’s Works in the 1530s and early 1540s is recorded with a level of administrative completeness that almost no comparable Tudor government office can match.

They are extraordinary documents. They record, by the four-week period, the names and wages of every workman — master carpenter, sawyer, bricklayer, scaffold-maker, common labourer, tiler, plumber, ditcher, carter — employed on every project. They record the days each man worked, the days he did not work, the feast days he kept, the half-days he put in. They record the purchases of materials — lime by the cwt, tile by the thousand, brick by the load, lead by the fother, timber by the foot, paper and ink for the clerk by the quire. They record the carriage costs — every cart-load of sand and brick and tile, every horse and wagon and widow with a cart hired by the day to move material from one place to another. They form, taken as a whole, a portrait of how the king’s England was physically built.

The first volume — MS Rawlinson D. 775, covering the year from Michaelmas 1532 to Michaelmas 1533 — opens with the work at Greenwich and the Old Court, runs through Windsor Castle and the arbours in Windsor Park, takes in Woking and Dover Castle and the Palace of Westminster, and culminates in a set of accounts under a heading that is one of the most striking single entries in the entire corpus.

The heading reads: Ageynst the coronacion of the Quene. The Quene is Anne Boleyn. The folios are 173 to 201 of MS Rawlinson D. 775. They record the carpentry and joinery work done at Westminster Hall in May and June of 1533 in preparation for the coronation of Henry VIII’s second wife. Pews. Galleries. Hangings. The whole apparatus of a state ceremony, prepared for a queen whose coronation Henry had moved heaven and earth to bring about, and whose execution he would order three years later.

Anne Boleyn was crowned at Westminster Abbey on 1 June 1533, in Whitsun week, with the realm in a state of barely-suppressed political tension over the king’s break with Rome to enable the marriage that had produced her. James Nedeham, eight months into his surveyorship, ran the works that made the hall ready for her. He would have been at Westminster in those weeks. He would have seen the new queen — perhaps from a distance, perhaps not — as she processed through the hall his men had prepared.

Three months earlier, in the spring of 1533, James had also been at the Tower of London. The Tower in 1533 was the customary point of ceremonial departure for an English coronation procession; the queen would lodge at the Tower the night before the coronation and process the next morning to Westminster. James’s men had been there in the spring to restore the timber framing above the great water-gate that opened onto the Thames — the gate known then and now as the Traitors’ Gate — in preparation for her arrival. The gate’s grim later name comes from its later use to admit Tudor prisoners to the Tower for execution. In 1533 it was the queen’s gate. James’s carpenters made it ready for her.

It was also the gate she would pass through on her way out, three years later, on her way to the scaffold.

The pay-books document, year after year, the buildings James worked on. In 1533-34, the second volume — MS Rawlinson D. 776 — records work at Greenwich, Eltham, the Tower of London, Bridewell, Richmond, Windsor, Woking, the Manor of the More, Chobham, Ampthill, and Bishop’s Hatfield. Twelve royal sites in a single year. In November 1534 one of the entries reads: The Kyngys greatt barge, callyd the Lyon — ayenste the commynge of the Frenche ambassitour beynge the hyghe Admyralle off Fransse. The king’s great barge, called the Lion, refitted against the coming of the French ambassador, who was the High Admiral of France. James’s men were working on the king’s ceremonial water-transport for a diplomatic visit. They worked on everything.

In 1535-36 he completed the new roof of the Great Hall at Hampton Court Palace.

This is the work that survives most visibly. The Great Hall at Hampton Court is the largest and most architecturally significant interior space in any of the surviving Tudor royal palaces. Henry VIII had acquired Hampton Court from Wolsey in 1529 and had begun an aggressive program of expansion almost immediately. The Great Hall, completed in the mid-1530s, was the formal dining and ceremonial chamber at the heart of the palace. It is one hundred and six feet long, forty feet wide, and sixty feet high to the roof apex. The roof is a hammer-beam roof, the most complex and structurally demanding form of medieval and early Tudor English timber roofing, with carved oak beams and pendant decorative work that has been called one of the finest surviving examples of its kind in England.

James designed it. He drew the architectural plans — the platts and molds by which the carpenters would frame the structure. The roof was completed under his supervision in 1535. It has held up the ceiling for four hundred and ninety-one years. It is, today, one of the most visited interiors in any English royal palace: a public space open to the public throughout the year, walked under by tourists and schoolchildren and visiting heads of state. If you have ever stood in the Great Hall at Hampton Court and looked up, you have looked at a thing made by James Nedeham.

Other work in those years had less of a future. The Tower of London received continuous attention through the 1530s — the Jewel House, the rebuilding of which is documented in MS Rawlinson D. 778 from January 1534-35 to April 1536, was a major project; James also restored the framing above the Traitors’ Gate. At Windsor Castle he built a new terrace on the north side, supported on brick vaults that drained the castle gutters, where Henry VIII could practice with his hand-guns — a private royal shooting range, with benches, sixty feet above the river. The terrace is still there, though the king’s hand-guns and his benches are not. At Greenwich he extensively prepared the site for the arrival of Anne of Cleves, the king’s fourth wife, in late 1539 and early 1540 — a project whose joyless aftermath was the king’s immediate aversion to his new bride and his arrangement of her annulment within six months. James also built, at Greenwich, a cockpit for cock-fighting, with custom seating for Catherine of Aragon — a detail that places him chronologically before the king’s first divorce, since Catherine was banished from Court by 1531.

He worked on Westminster Hall, where he managed extensive repairs and reinforcement of the medieval hammer-beam roof, the structural guttering, the King’s fish house, and the office of the Exchequer. He worked at Calais on the English fortifications. He converted dissolved monasteries into royal palaces — sections of St Augustine’s Abbey at Canterbury, the priory at Rochester, the nunnery at Dartford, and others. He was the man who turned, by physical labor, the bones of the old Catholic England into the residential infrastructure of the new Tudor state.

The Surveyor’s Hand

The volumes have come down to us because they stayed in the family.

This is one of the small wonders of the record. The pay-books that James and Pontesbury wrote, or had written, or oversaw the writing of, between 1532 and 1544, were the working papers of the Surveyor’s office. They should, by the ordinary administrative logic of the Tudor state, have stayed with the office — passed to James’s successor at his death, kept in the Works office’s central files at Westminster, eventually transferred to the Crown archives where they would have been catalogued in the Pipe Office or the Exchequer of Receipt. That is what happened to most surviving administrative records of the Tudor government. They went into the central archive, where they survive in the State Paper classes of the National Archives at Kew.

But James’s pay-books did not. They went home with him.

They stayed at Wymondley Priory — his country house, which he had received from the king in 1538 — from the year of their compilation in the 1530s and 1540s until at least the early 1770s, when the priory was finally sold out of the family. That is roughly two hundred and thirty years of continuous private custody. For most of that time the books sat on shelves in the priory house, in the rooms where James himself had lived and where his son John had signed the herald’s pedigree in 1572 and where his grandson George had baptized children in the chapel and where the line continued down through Eustace and George Senior and Maurice and Maurice’s daughter Barbara.

The proof that they were at Wymondley sits inside the books themselves. On the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 776 — the pay-book for the year 1533-34, the volume that opens with the Greenwich works — are two pasted fragments of private accounts in 1655, kept by Mr. Nedham of Wymondley Priory, i.e. George Needham.

This is George Senior, the man baptized at Little Wymondley church on 16 August 1618, the writer of the 1666/67 will, the lord of the priory through the entire Civil War and Restoration period, the great-great-grandson of James, and the man whose great-granddaughter would marry Christopher VI de Graffenried in Charleston in 1714.

On the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 778 — the pay-book for the Tower of London works of 1535-36 — are memoranda by the same George Needham, dated 1643-44, recording taxes paid for his lands at Graveley and Little Wymondley in Hertfordshire and at Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire. He was twenty-five years old when he made those notes. The Civil War was raging. Charles I would be executed in five years.

On the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 782 — the pay-book for the demolition of Barking Abbey in 1541-42 — are still further accounts by the same George, this time from 1668. He was fifty years old by then, and the war had been over for nearly two decades, and the Restoration had brought Charles II back to the throne, and George was still using his great-great-grandfather’s working papers as scratch paper for the running of his estate.

Three volumes — D. 776, D. 778, and D. 782 — physically preserve handwriting from both ends of the family. James Nedeham’s clerk Antony Pontesbury wrote the pay tallies. James authorized the accounts. The books stayed at Wymondley for a hundred and twenty years. George Senior, in the priory house, picked them up across multiple decades of his adult life and wrote his own life on their margins. The first hand is from the 1530s. The second is from the 1640s, 1650s, and 1660s. The same paper carries both.

This is the physical link between Chapter One of this book and Chapter Four. It is the documentary thread between the man who built Wymondley and the great-grandfather of the woman who left it. The Bodleian Library, where the books have rested since the middle of the eighteenth century, preserves a tangible piece of inheritance that no will or pedigree could capture quite this way: a working ledger that two ancestors of the same Alabama descendant, four generations apart, both wrote in.

That fact is part of why this book can be written. The other part is that James’s books are unusually detailed even by the standards of Tudor administrative records. The Bodleian’s published catalog of the Rawlinson manuscripts, compiled by W. D. Macray and printed at the Clarendon Press between 1862 and 1900, gives the volume-by-volume contents of the entire eleven-volume Nedeham run. Volume One: the works of 1532-33, including the coronation preparations for Anne Boleyn. Volume Two: 1533-34, including the King’s barge the Lion. Volume Three: 1534- 35, with substantial accounts for Windsor and Greenwich. Volume Four: 1535-36, the Tower of London. Volume Five: 1535-37 with a summary of the year’s total spending across sixteen royal sites — a single figure preserved at the back of the volume, the total amount for the king’s buildings in 1535-1537: two thousand six hundred and thirty-six pounds, seventeen shillings, ten pence half-penny. That is the annual royal building budget at the height of Henry VIII’s program, in the hand of James Nedeham’s clerk.

Volume Six: 1536-37 and 1540. Volume Seven: 1541-42, including Enfield and the Canterbury works. Volume Eight: 1541-42, the demolition of Barking Abbey. Volume Nine: 1541-42, the manor of Dartford. Volume Ten: 1543-44, Dartford continued. Volume Eleven: 1541-42, the manor of Rochester. The structure of James’s life from 1532 to 1544 — month by month, project by project, name by name — is laid out across these eleven volumes.

Two volumes of the original twelve are missing. Neither has been recovered. The years not covered are 1537-38 and 1540-41. The year of the Wymondley grant is one of the missing years. The pay-book that would have recorded James’s activity in the spring of 1538, when the priory came into his hands, is one of the two volumes that have not survived.

The Grant of Wymondley

He was at Wymondley before the grant.

Sometime in December 1537, four months before the formal Crown grant by letters patent, James obtained a lease of the priory site from the Court of Augmentations. The lease was the mechanism by which the Crown allowed him initial possession; the grant of the freehold came afterward. This is the careful order of Tudor real-estate transactions when the Crown was disposing of dissolved monastic property: lease first, to put a man in occupation and let him begin work, then formal grant of the fee simple. James had been quietly placed at Wymondley before the world knew it was his.

On 12 April 1538, by letters patent issued under the Great Seal of England, King Henry VIII confirmed his title. The grant is recorded in the published calendar of Letters and Papers, Foreign and Domestic, of the Reign of Henry VIII, volume thirteen, item 887, sub-grant thirteen. The 1538 grant carried with it the priory’s lands in the manor of Little Wymondley, a scattering of outlying parcels in the parish, the rectory and tithes of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire — which the priory had held by appropriation since the medieval period — and the buildings themselves: the priory church, the cloister, the chapter house, the prior’s lodging, the refectory, the kitchens, the dormitory, and the outbuildings.

That this was a Crown grant and not a commercial purchase is established at primary-source level by the Elizabethan Chancery testimony of James’s son. Sometime between 1558 and 1603 — the exact year is not recorded — John Nedeham, James’s son and heir, filed a Chancery suit against one William Clarke over some matter involving the Wymondley property. In the bill of complaint, John described the property’s history: “Wymondley, Hertfordshire, formerly belonging to the priory of Little Wymondley, and since the dissolution of the said priory granted by King Henry 8 to James Nedeham, esq., plaintiff’s father.” The phrasing is sworn testimony in the Court of Chancery. The case is catalogued at the National Archives at Kew as C 2/Eliz/N5/46. The Wymondley priory was a grant, by King Henry the Eighth, to James the father. That is John’s own statement.

The grant was a substantial reward. The lordship of a small Hertfordshire manor was worth, in the cash terms of the late 1530s, perhaps thirty to fifty pounds a year in net annual income. The tenure form mattered as much as the income. James held Wymondley of the king in chief by the service of a tenth part of a knight’s fee — a military tenure, not free socage, the formal dignity of which by 1538 was largely symbolic (the service had long since been commuted to a small money payment) but the symbolism of which was the point. A Crown grant by knight-service was the gentleman’s holding. A grant in free socage would have been the lesser thing. The buildings themselves represented a windfall — though not, as it turned out, a pristine windfall. James Nedeham, on 12 April 1538, became overnight one of the new Reformation gentry of England: a Crown servant who had received, as direct reward for his service to the king, a former monastic house and the lordship of the parish around it.

This is the founding act of the Wymondley Nedhams. Every generation of the family that this book is about, down to Barbara Tempest Needham who married Christopher de Graffenried in Charleston in 1714, descended from James and from the priory he received in April 1538. The line could not have existed in Hertfordshire without the dissolution of the monasteries. It is, in the most literal sense, a Reformation family.

It is worth pausing for a moment on the strangeness of that.

The priory at Little Wymondley had been founded around the year 1200, by a Norman lord named Richard de Argenton, as a house of Augustinian canons regular. It was small — never more than twelve or fifteen canons at its medieval height, with the usual establishment of a prior, a sub-prior, a sacristan, a cellarer, an almoner, and lay servants. It served the parish church of Little Wymondley. The canons sang the offices day and night across three and a half centuries. They copied manuscripts. They kept the lamps lit. They said masses for the souls of the founders and the benefactors, of the local Argyll family and the surrounding gentry, of the men and women who had given the priory its land or its money or its candles. They buried their dead in the cloister. They tended the fish in the priory pond. They kept the bells.

By the time James inherited it, the priory was not pristine. The 1537 visitation by the Bishop of Lincoln — conducted only weeks before the suppression — found the priory’s buildings “greatly ruined,” and recorded that the prior, John Atow, had recently spent £5 on repairs with a further £15 4s 8d set aside for ongoing maintenance. The east end of the priory church appears to have collapsed shortly before the suppression. The 1537 inventory of the priory’s possessions, taken when the canons were dispersed, does not list the chancel or the bell tower at all; the rooms listed are the hall, kitchen, brewhouse, bakehouse, buttery, pantry, and the prior’s chamber. James was inheriting a half-ruined complex whose west end — the cloister and the western claustral ranges — had been recently patched and was at least habitable; whose east end was wreckage.

The 1536 Act of Suppression dissolved the smaller monasteries — those with annual incomes under two hundred pounds — and Wymondley fell well below that line. The canons were dispersed in 1537, given small pensions or moved to surviving houses. The priory church and the conventual buildings were taken into the king’s hands by his commissioners. The site sat in Crown ownership for about a year while the Court of Augmentations — the new administrative office set up in 1536 to handle the redistribution of dissolved-monastery property — worked out what to do with it.

In December 1537 it was leased to James. In April 1538, eight years and six months into his surveyorship, the Court of Augmentations passed the priory to Henry VIII to be granted as a Crown reward, and the king gave it to James outright. The man who was building the king’s palaces, restoring his castles, melting the lead from his dissolved abbeys, was given a dissolved abbey of his own — one whose recent damage would require him to do the very kind of repair-and-conversion work that he had been overseeing for the king for half a decade.

He was about forty-eight years old. He was at the peak of his career. He had a wife named Alice, the daughter (her son John would later swear to it, in the Heralds’ Visitation pedigree he signed at Wymondley in the summer of 1572) of a Merry of Hatfield in Hertfordshire — the marriage may have been the connection that brought him to Wymondley in the first place. They had seven children, five sons and two daughters. The eldest was that same John, who would later sign the Visitation and who would later swear in Chancery that the priory had come by Crown grant from Henry. By the spring of 1538 John would have been somewhere between fifteen and twenty years old; he was old enough by April 1544 to be a named joint grantee with his father in a Hertfordshire land purchase.

The family came up to Wymondley. What James did with the buildings is one of the most carefully studied questions in the architectural history of Hertfordshire’s post-Dissolution monastic conversions, and the answer is not what the v1 of this chapter assumed. He did not convert the prior’s lodging into the family hall and keep the church as a private chapel. He did something more striking. He kept the western nave of the priory church itself as the principal hall of his new house. He inserted floors. He cut Tudor mullioned-and-transomed windows into the medieval lancet openings of the south wall. He converted the refectory and the western claustral ranges as ancillary domestic space. The eastern end of the church — the chancel, the crossing, the transepts, the bell tower — he left in the ruined state in which he had found it, or partly demolished it for stone. The cloister became an enclosed inner courtyard. The kitchens stayed in use. The fish pond stayed stocked.

This sounds, perhaps, harder than it was. The Augustinian canons had been gone for less than a year. Their church had been a working stone building when James obtained the lease in December. He kept the parts that were sound. He used the parts that were defensible to a working country gentleman’s domestic life. The Nedeham family ate their dinners in the converted nave of the priory church. They walked, in their cloister-yard courtyard, on the stones the canons had walked. The wall paintings of the late medieval cloister walks were whitewashed over or replaced, but a fragment of late-sixteenth-century work survives in the north-east corner of the former cloister walk — a section of figural painting depicting armoured soldiers, possibly executed by James’s son John or his grandson George later in the century, that was rediscovered during an architectural survey of the building in the 1990s and remains in situ. The chapel that the family used for baptisms and burials — the consecrated space in which Barbara would be baptized in 1685 — was almost certainly a small consecrated portion preserved within the converted hall, possibly the area marked by a traceried recess that survives in the south end of the building and may once have been a piscina or the indication of a small private oratory. (The architectural-historical study of the priory by Nicholas Doggett, completed in 1997, is the authority for all of this, and the source on which this paragraph leans.)

The house became a house. The smell of incense, where it had lingered for three hundred and thirty-eight years, faded into the smell of cooking and woodsmoke and the press of household life.

What He Did at Wymondley

Four months before the priory grant, James had already begun building his Hertfordshire estate.

On 19 December 1537, by a legal instrument called a final concord — the standard Tudor mechanism for conveying real property through a fictitious lawsuit — he acquired from Francis Goodyere, esquire, of Hadley, a wood called Cattall Grove and adjoining land in South Mimms, Middlesex. South Mimms is about fifteen miles south of Little Wymondley, between Wymondley and London, on the Hertfordshire-Middlesex border. The wood was a working asset — standing oak, coppice, underwood for fuel and fencing. The conveyance is preserved at the Somerset Heritage Centre in the Walker-Heneage and Button family papers, catalogued as DD/2628.

This was a carpenter’s purchase. A man of James’s trade and life knew the value of having his own timber. The Cattall Grove wood gave him a private source of building material that he could draw on for his own works at Wymondley, or for projects elsewhere, or simply for the household use of a country gentleman. In December 1537, ten months before he took possession of the priory itself, James Nedeham bought a wood. The order of operations is itself revealing. He bought the materials first, and the seat second. The instinct of the working craftsman remained, even as the office of Surveyor was elevating him into gentry status.

Once the priory was his in April 1538 — or, properly, from December 1537 onward, when the lease put him in possession — he set about making it habitable and productive. The conversion was substantial. As described above, the western nave of the priory church became the hall of the new house; the refectory and the western claustral ranges were converted as ancillary space; the eastern end of the church was left ruined or partly demolished. The cloister became an inner courtyard. The kitchens, brewhouse, and bakehouse — still listed in the 1537 inventory as serviceable — stayed in use. James added at least two further structures outside the moated enclosure of the priory: a conduit to the north-east, bringing piped water from a higher point on the property down to the house, and a dovecote to the north-west, providing pigeons for the household table and as a sign of his new gentry status. (Dovecotes in Tudor England were a manorial privilege; only lords of manors could legally keep them. James’s dovecote was the architectural assertion of his having become one.) Both structures are dated by Doggett to the mid-sixteenth century, almost certainly during James’s tenure or that of his son. The conversion would have taken two or three years. The main work was probably substantially complete by the early 1540s.

In 1541 he built the tithe barn.

This was the most substantial new structure he added to the property. The tithe barn was a separate building, set apart from the priory’s domestic ranges, designed to hold the grain tithes that came in from the parish and the priory’s outlying landholdings. The barn measured one hundred feet long, with nine bays on either side of a central threshing floor. It was timber-framed in oak, the principal posts and the tie beams quite possibly brought down out of the Cattall Grove wood that James had bought four years earlier in South Mimms — although the documentary trail does not survive to confirm the timber’s origin. The frame was brick-infilled, with a tile roof. The interior was lit by small windows under the eaves; the floors were rammed earth or stone-flagged.

It is one of the finest surviving examples of an early Tudor timber-framed agricultural building in England. It is now operated as a working wedding and events venue, hosting up to two hundred and twenty guests for civil ceremonies under James Nedeham’s roof. It is the most authentic surviving piece of his domestic architecture anywhere — his country buildings, made for his own estate, in the year of his own choosing, with timber from his own wood and at his own cost. The tithe barn is the home he made.

He continued to add land. On 22 April 1544 — or, more precisely, sometime during the regnal year that ran from that date to 21 April 1545 — he and his son John, described in the indenture as “James Nedeham of Wymondley, esquire, and John Nedeham, gentleman,” purchased messuages and land in Bygrave next Baldock from one Thomas Myldemaye, esquire, of London. Bygrave is about ten miles east of Little Wymondley, in the same Hertfordshire countryside. The indenture is at the National Archives at Kew, catalogued among the Land Revenue records as LR 14/468. This is the documented moment at which James Nedeham styles himself, in a formal legal instrument, of Wymondley, Esquire. The Esquire status is recognized in a Crown-related land record. The six-year arc from London carpenter’s son to Hertfordshire gentleman has formally completed.

It is also the last property record of his life. The Bygrave indenture covers the year that ran from April 1544 to April 1545. James died on 22 September 1544. The transaction may have been negotiated before his death and completed posthumously by John; or it may have been finished in the spring or early summer of 1544 before James embarked for the Continent. Either way, in the last months of his life he was still adding to the family estate.

Boulogne, and the Lead

The 1540s in James Nedeham’s life have a particular weight.

By the spring of 1540 he was approaching fifty. He had been Surveyor for eight years. His patron Thomas Cromwell was beginning to lose the king’s favor; on 28 July 1540 Cromwell would be executed on Tower Hill, on the same day Henry married his fifth wife Catherine Howard. James kept his office. He continued his work. Catherine Howard would be executed in turn in February 1542, on the same scaffold; James was still Surveyor. The king married his sixth wife Catherine Parr in July 1543; James was still Surveyor.

In these years he was running, simultaneously, the largest set of monastic conversions in the kingdom. The pay-books document, between 1540 and 1544, the demolition of Barking Abbey for stone to rebuild the king’s manor at Dartford, the conversion of Rochester Priory into a Crown college and then into a Crown manor, the demolition of buildings at St Augustine’s Canterbury — where, in January 1542, the great Norman steeple of the abbey came down with such force that it broke a section of the king’s garden wall — and the redistribution, through his office, of the lead stripped from these and other dissolved religious houses to the roofs of the king’s palaces.

The lead account survives, in the State Papers at the National Archives, catalogued as SP 5/4/196. It covers the period from 22 April 1542 to 21 April 1544 — the regnal years 34 and 35 of Henry VIII, James’s last two full working years. The account is described in the catalog as London and Home Counties Monasteries: account of lead by James Nedeham, clerk of the King’s Works, 34-35 Hen. VIII, giving destinations of the lead.

This is an inventory of stripped monastic lead and its redistribution. The lead from the roofs of the dissolved abbeys and priories of London and its surrounding counties — Barking, Canterbury, Dartford, Rochester, and others — was measured in fothers (about a ton each), melted, recast in sheets, and laid down again on the king’s palace roofs at Greenwich and Whitehall and Hampton Court. The man who oversaw this process across the entire London region for the last two years of his life was James Nedeham.

It is the hardest detail to write about. The administrative neutrality of the account — the columns of weights and destinations and prices in pence per pound — cannot quite obscure what the work meant. The lead that had covered the cloisters of the canons regular and the friars and the nuns for hundreds of years was being reweighed and recast and laid down again on the roofs of the king’s residences. James, who in those same years was living in his own former priory at Wymondley, was the executive in charge of physically dismantling other people’s former priories so their metal could go onto the king’s palaces.

The Reformation, in the documentary record of his life, is not a single event of 1536 or 1539. It is the ongoing job of his last decade. It is the demolition of Barking Abbey in the summer of 1541, paid by the four-week period to a crew of named workmen — Robert Carterwryght, William Tompson, Reynold Newcome, Phillip Salamond — whose tallies survive in MS Rawlinson D. 782 at the Bodleian Library. It is the cleanup of the fallen Norman steeple at St Augustine’s Canterbury in early 1542. It is the redistribution of monastic lead across the Home Counties in 1542-44. It is the work of a man who has benefited materially from the dissolution and who is, by the office he holds, an active executor of its physical violence.

This is part of who he was. The book does not flinch from it. It also does not impose modern judgment on it. He was, by the standards of his own time, doing a job that was within his office and within the law as Parliament had framed it. He was not unusual in doing it; thousands of English Crown servants and gentry families benefited materially from the dissolution. But to write his life honestly is to name the work he did, including the work whose moral weight does not soften under any later light.

In July 1544 Henry VIII led an English army across the Channel to besiege the French port of Boulogne, on the Pas-de-Calais coast about a hundred miles south of the English-held enclave of Calais. The campaign was the latest episode in the long Tudor war with France. The English laid siege to Boulogne through the summer; the city surrendered on 14 September 1544. The king entered in triumph. The army began the work of fortifying the captured city as a permanent English possession, which it would remain until 1550.

James Nedeham was with the king’s army on that campaign. He was almost certainly there in his capacity as Surveyor, responsible for fortifications and military construction — the same kind of work he had done as a young military carpenter at Calais twenty years before. He was now about fifty-four years old. He had been at war before, but not for two decades. He had a wife in Hertfordshire and a country house and a tithe barn that was three years old and seven children growing into their own lives.

He died at Boulogne on 22 September 1544 — eight days after the city fell. The cause is not recorded in the surviving documentation. Tudor military camps were lethal places even in victory; dysentery, plague, and gangrene from minor wounds killed more soldiers than the fighting did. He was buried in the church of Our Lady at Boulogne.

The family commemorated him in Hertfordshire sixty-one years later. In 1605, his grandson George Nedeham — the lord of the manor by then, son of John, the same George who would later impropriate the rectory and pay the parish curate out of the tithes — dedicated a monument to his father and grandfather in the chancel of the parish church at Little Wymondley. The inscription, transcribed by Sir Henry Chauncy when he wrote his county history a hundred years afterward and preserved in print at page 362 of The Historical Antiquities of Hertfordshire (1700), reads in his transcription: James Needham, of the ancient Family of the Needhams in Derbyshire, came into this County of Hertford, in the year of our Lord, 1536. He was advanced by King Henry the eight, for his Service in England and France, and lyes buried in our Ladies Church in Boloigne; he had issue John, who had George, who dedicated this Monument to their Memory, Anno 1605. Beneath, a single line of Latin: Satius est mortis quam Natalis dies. Better is the day of death than the day of birth. Ecclesiastes 7:1.

The inscription is the family’s own statement of itself. It names Derbyshire as the place the line came from; it names 1536 as the year James came into Hertfordshire — one year before the lease and two before the patent, which fits the pattern of his service on the dissolution-survey commissions before he was personally rewarded with the property. It names the king he had served. It names the church across the Channel in which his body lay. And it names his grandson as the one who, in the year the King James Bible was a project five years from completion, set the stone to keep all of this in view of the parish.

What James Means

Three things, before this chapter closes.

First, the family seat is not an inheritance. It is a reward. The priory was a gift from the king to a man who had earned it by twelve years of running the king’s building program. This will shape everything that follows. The Wymondley Nedhams will be careful with the Court, because their seat came from the Court. They will avoid open political conflict. They will keep the priory through Mary, Elizabeth, James I, Charles I, the Commonwealth, Cromwell, Charles II, James II, and William and Mary — eight monarchs and one republic — by being unobtrusive, and the unobtrusiveness begins with the founder. James knew what he owed the king. His descendants would know it too. When the Civil War came, ninety-eight years after the grant, his great-grandson Eustace would survive it by keeping his head down. The strategy started with James.

Second, the family’s records survived. The pay-books that James and Antony Pontesbury wrote stayed at Wymondley for two hundred and thirty years. They passed, through the family’s careful keeping of its papers, into the antiquarian collection of Richard Rawlinson in the eighteenth century, and from Rawlinson at his death in 1755 to the Bodleian Library at Oxford, where they have been for two hundred and seventy-one years. The Bodleian preserves James Nedeham’s working life in a level of documentary completeness that almost no comparable Tudor administrator can claim. His life is recoverable because his descendants kept the papers. The chapter you have just read could not have been written without those volumes. The volumes survived because the family stayed at Wymondley long enough to preserve them, and because when Wymondley was finally sold, the books went with the daughter who carried them out.

Third — and this is the one the rest of the book is going to lean on — the buildings James made are still standing.

The Great Hall at Hampton Court Palace is still standing. The hammer-beam roof he designed in the early 1530s and finished in 1535 has held up the ceiling, without major reconstruction, for four hundred and ninety-one years. Anyone can go and look at it. The palace is open to the public throughout the year.

The framing above the Traitors’ Gate at the Tower of London is still standing. The Jewel House he built is still part of the Tower’s standing fabric. The north terrace at Windsor Castle, with its drainage vaults, is still there. The repairs and reinforcements he made to the medieval hammer-beam roof of Westminster Hall are still holding up the roof. The conversions he oversaw at St Augustine’s Canterbury are still in part visible at the site, which is now an English Heritage property open to the public.

This needs a small honest correction, because Wymondley is not quite like Hampton Court.

The priory house at Little Wymondley is still standing. The west range and the converted nave that James turned into his hall are still on the same footprint they have occupied since the late 1530s. But a restoration of the building carried out in 1973 and 1974 — by a private owner, under no architectural-historical supervision worth mentioning — destroyed almost all the surviving sixteenth-century datable features of the interior. The Tudor mullioned-and-transomed windows James cut into the medieval lancets, the original panelling, the early fireplaces, the carpentry detail of his hall: virtually all of it was lost, replaced with twentieth-century approximations of what the work had been. The current priory house is, in physical terms, a post-1974 reconstruction over a medieval shell. The building James lived in survives as a footprint, a set of walls, and a few fragments — the wall-painting in the cloister walk, the traceried recess, the conduit and dovecote outside the moated enclosure. The interior he actually lived in has, for the most part, not survived the twentieth century.

This is painful. It is also why the tithe barn matters as much as it does.

The tithe barn at Little Wymondley — the building he commissioned for his own estate in 1541, with his own money, on his own land, three years after he received the priory and three years before he died at Boulogne — is still standing in essentially the form he commissioned it. The oak frame is the oak frame. The hammer-beam construction is the construction his carpenters set up under his direction in the summer of 1541. The barn was not part of the 1973-74 restoration; it was already operating as an agricultural building outside the residential complex, and its agricultural use into the late twentieth century meant that its working fabric was left largely alone. By the time it was adapted for civil-ceremony use, beginning in the 1990s, the bones of the building had survived essentially intact. The tithe barn is the most authentic surviving piece of James Nedeham’s domestic architecture anywhere on earth. It is the only structure of his own that you can walk into today and touch the timber he commissioned. The current owners use it for weddings. They market the venue on the strength of its 1541 date. The building’s history page reads, in part: At the heart of the estate stands the magnificent tithe barn, built in 1541 by James Needham, a master carpenter to King Henry VIII. Rich in history and craftsmanship, it is widely regarded as one of the finest timber barns in England.

Eight generations later, in late October 1713, a young woman who had grown up in those buildings — the chapel where she was baptized, the cloister yard she had played in as a child, the tithe barn that stood at the eastern edge of the priory grounds and that she had walked past every day of her life — would put a small trunk on a cart and ride out the priory gatehouse onto the Hitchin road for the last time. She would be twenty-eight years old. She would be carrying a letter she had written in August saying I am coming. She would be sailing, before the end of the year, for a country that had just had a war.

James could not have imagined her. He was a fifty-four-year-old Surveyor of the King’s Works dying at Boulogne in September 1544, with a tithe barn finished three years earlier and seven children growing into a new and unprecedented family standing in Hertfordshire and a king who would outlive him by two years and four months. He had no idea that his great-great-great-great-great-granddaughter would carry a small piece of his work and the memory of his house across an ocean. He had no idea that the priory he had been given by the king would pass out of the family in 1733, two hundred and seventy-one years after his death; or that his pay-books would outlast the family’s tenure of the priory; or that a descendant in Alabama in 2026 would be reading them in a digitized image on a laptop screen and writing this paragraph.

He had a barn to finish.

It is, for now, the last thing he made that we know of for certain. The Cattall Grove wood likely gave at least some of the timber. His own carpenters — men whose names are not recorded but who would have come up from London or from one of the king’s work-sites — framed the roof. The tile came from a local kiln. The brick came up from the brickfields north of the priory. The frame went up in the summer of 1541 and the roof went on by the autumn and the barn was substantially complete by the end of the year, or shortly into 1542. James lived less than three years after it was finished.

The barn is still standing in Hertfordshire.

So is the family.

· · ·

On the night of 22 September 1544, in the church of Our Lady at Boulogne, James Nedeham was buried. He was fifty-four. He had been Surveyor of the King’s Works for twelve years all but eight days. He left a wife in Hertfordshire, seven children, two hundred and forty-six pay-books and account-books and working ledgers documenting his administrative life, a country house that had been a priory, a tithe barn three years old, a wood in South Mimms, a recent property in Bygrave, and a legal style of Esquire of Wymondley that he had earned by service to the Crown across the span of the most consequential decade of the Reformation.

He also left a son named John, then about twenty, who would inherit the priory — though not, in the strict legal sense, in 1544. James had taken the precaution, before he sailed for Boulogne, of obtaining a licence to entail the manor on John. The entail had to be executed through the Court of Wards before John could enter the freehold, and the year between James’s death in September 1544 and John’s succession in 1545 is the year that mechanism took to grind through. In 1545 John entered. He would go on to defend the priory in Chancery against William Clarke in the Elizabethan era, marry twice, sign the Heralds’ Visitation pedigree at Wymondley in the summer of 1572 by signing his own name at the bottom of the page, and raise the next generation of the family who would carry the house forward through the wars and revolutions of the next two centuries.

That signature is in the next chapter.
