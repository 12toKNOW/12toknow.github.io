The Bible That Changed Languages

A small book carried from a Bern manor to a Carolina harbor, keeping its record of the family in a French the family itself would lose.

Status of this finding: Documented  ·  the object, its French fly-leaf entries, and its chain of keepers are on record; the entries are preserved in a printed transcription. This page keeps to those, and to nothing invented.

The finding, in brief

What runs unbroken through this whole family is not, in the end, a bloodline — it is a habit. In each generation, someone kept the record: a birth, a death, a marriage, written on the fly-leaf of the family Bible in the hand of whoever held the book that year. One such Bible can be followed for eighty years and across an ocean. It was given at the Bernese manor of Worb in 1710, carried in a sea-chest to Charleston, and its earliest entries were set down in a formal French that, within three generations, no one in the family could read. And still the keeping did not stop.

The book

The object is a small Bible, roughly eight inches by five, bound in dark calf. Baron Christoph de Graffenried gave it to his son Christopher at Worb in 1710, with the fly-leaf still clean — the place kept for the family's record. The habit that would fill it was itself inherited: the practice of entering the family's marriages and deaths in formal French on the fly-leaf came from the Baron's own Bible at Worb, where it can be traced back to a death entry of 1697. When Christopher sailed for the New World, the book went with him — out of Worb, through Bern and London, and across to Carolina.

The first entry, and the language it was written in

The first thing written on that clean fly-leaf was made at Christopher's marriage in Charleston, South Carolina, on 22 February 1714 — in his own hand, and in the formal French his father had instructed him to use. A generation on, the convention still held. When his son Tscharner recorded his own marriage, the entry again went down in French, and the printed family history preserves it:

Tscharner de Graffenried … Marie Baker, fille de feu le colonel Henry Baker … Mariés en Chowan … le cinquième jour de juillet, 1742.

That is the strangeness at the center of this story. The family went on faithfully entering its most important moments in a language it was losing. Within three generations of the first entry, no one in the line could read the French their own record was written in — and they kept writing it anyway, because keeping the record mattered more than understanding it.

The chain of hands

The book passed down the way such books do — from keeper to keeper, sometimes changing countries, always changing hands:

Baron Christoph, at Worb (1710) → Christopher, at Charleston (first entry, 1714) → his son Tscharner DeGraffenreid → Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson (at Tscharner's death, February 1794)

Across those hands the one Bible carried the family's record for some eighty years and four generations, from a Swiss manor house to the American South. And when a Bible like this was finally gone — divided among heirs, or simply worn past use — the keeping did not end; it moved into the forms keeping takes when no one is trained to it anymore: a box of photographs, a story told once at a kitchen table, a chart somebody started and did not finish. The record has never actually stopped. That is the thread the whole history is named for.

What is documented, and what is held open

The fly-leaf entries survive because they were transcribed into the 1925 printed history of the de Graffenried family; related family papers are held today in the DeGraffenreid Association Collection at East Carolina University, and the Bible itself has descended to a family member. In keeping with this project's discipline, one seam is left open rather than smoothed: the exact date of Christopher's death — the moment the book passed to his son — is not fixed by the record, and the family holds it open rather than choose the tidier of two readings. The record is kept honest the same way the Bible was: you set down what you know, in the place kept for it, and you do not invent to fill the silence.

Key sources
