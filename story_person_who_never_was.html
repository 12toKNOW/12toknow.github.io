A Person Who Never Was

The "Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley" who was assembled, in good faith, from the pieces of real people.

Status of this finding: the fabricated ancestor is Falsified  ·  the true parentage is Documented.

The finding, in brief

On page 149 of his 1925 History of the de Graffenried Family, Thomas Paul de Graffenried gives Barbara Needham — the English wife of Christopher de Graffenried, and DeWayne's ancestor #1145 — a father: "the distinguished Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire," and a mother whose maiden name was Wingate. After an exhaustive search of the surviving records, this finding sets out why no such man ever existed. "Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley" is not a lost ancestor but a composite — an honest reconstruction, assembled two centuries after the fact from the attributes of several real people who genuinely belonged to Barbara's Hertfordshire family and its world.

The conclusion is not that the 1925 author invented an ancestor. It is gentler than that. He inherited a blank where a name should have been — a blank the family itself had left open since 1735 — and, without the archives we can now read, he filled it with a plausible name built from pieces that were each real but had never belonged to one man. The documented parentage is Maurice Nedham, of the Nedhams of Little Wymondley, and Letisia Bowyer of Wymondley, married in London on 21 June 1684.

The sentence in question

The 1925 History records Barbara's marriage and parentage in a single sentence:

"On February 22, 1714, at Charleston, South Carolina, he married Barbara Tempest (née Needham), daughter of the distinguished Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire, England. She was born in 1688, her mother's maiden name being Wingate."

The sentence carries no citation, and its central figure cannot be found. There is no knight of that name in the heraldic Visitations of Hertfordshire, none in Sir Henry Chauncy's Historical Antiquities of Hertfordshire, none in the Victoria County History's manorial descents for either Wymondley parish, and none in Shaw's Knights of England. That silence is the first clue. A genuine "Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley" — a knight holding a named Hertfordshire seat in the later 1600s — would be among the most heavily recorded people in the parish. His complete absence from precisely the sources that would have to contain him is, in itself, strong evidence that he is a construction rather than a man.

The blank the author was filling

The 1925 author deserves to be read charitably, because the gap he was closing was real and old. In 1735 the founder of the American line, Baron Christoph de Graffenried, wrote from Worb to his son Christopher — Barbara's husband — and, coming to the son's marriage, named the bride only as an Englishwoman and then, rather than set down a pedigree he did not possess, handed the task to his son:

"Now I must talk of you & your posterity — You have married Madame Needham an English gentlewoman from which you have a son; You may put here the Genealogie of your spouse yourself …"

He left the space blank. And the point is decisive: the founder, meticulous about rank and title everywhere else in the letter, recorded no pedigree for Barbara at all, and the family carried none in 1735. Whatever parentage surfaced later was therefore supplied by descendants afterward — the profile of an embellished attribution, not of inherited knowledge. The 1925 book prints this very letter on its page 147, two pages before the "Sir Arthur" sentence.

So when Thomas P. de Graffenried compiled the History in the 1920s, he was working from these same imperfect papers, at the distance of an ocean and two centuries, and without the Hertfordshire parish registers, the printed Visitations, the Victoria County History, or the National Archives finding aids that make the reconstruction below possible. Faced with a blank, he did what a conscientious compiler of his generation did: he reached for the most distinguished-sounding parentage the family tradition seemed to point toward, and wrote it down. His guess then took on a life of its own — repeated, hedged, by Maude Woodfin in 1942 and again in a 1950 Colonial Williamsburg letter — but both later versions simply cite back to the 1925 book, so they are echoes, not evidence.

Barbara's real family: the Nedhams of Wymondley

It would be easy, and wrong, to treat the whole phrase as fantasy. Almost every element of "of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire" is grounded in fact — which is exactly what made the composite convincing. There truly was a Nedham family seated at Wymondley, and they were substantial. (The period spelling is Nedham; "Needham" is the later form of the same name.)

Barbara's great-grandfather, Eustace Nedham, Esquire, of Little Wymondley, died in 1657/58 leaving a will that styles him plainly "Esquire." His son, George Nedham, Esquire, Barbara's grandfather, was lord of the manors of Little Wymondley and Graveley Hall and held the family seat, "The Priory," at Little Wymondley; his 1666/67 will names his four sons — George, Fitch, James, and Maurice. The family's reach was already transatlantic a generation before Barbara sailed: George's brother James Nedham was settled in Virginia by his death in 1677.

The fourth son, Maurice Nedham — a younger son with a £500 portion, gone to St. Bride's in legal London — is Barbara's father. On 21 June 1684, at St. James Duke's Place in the City of London, he married Letisia Bowyer of Wymondley, the marriage register naming both plainly: "Mauris Nedham of St Brides London & Letisia Bowyer of Wymondley in Hertford-sheire." One year later, in 1685, "Barbary Nedham" was baptized at Great Wymondley — an exact and unforced fit: a first child arriving the year after the wedding, the mother returning to her own Wymondley family for the birth.

Two things stand out from this documented family. First, they were genuinely "of Wymondley" — not by courtesy but as lords of the manor, seated at The Priory. Second, in every record that names them they are Esquires and gentlemen, never knights. The 1925 author's place was right. What went wrong was the decoration.

Anatomy of a composite

The fabricated name resolves cleanly into four parts, each traceable to a real person or family. Two are firmly documented; two are proposed — the likeliest source given the geography and the records, offered as interpretation rather than proof.

"Needham … of Wymondsley" — the true core (firm)

This part is simply correct, and it is the anchor that made the rest believable. Barbara was a Nedham of Wymondley; her father Maurice, grandfather George, and great-grandfather Eustace were the resident family, lords of the manor at Little Wymondley. The surname and the seat in the 1925 sentence are right. Everything that follows is the mis-decoration of a true stem.

"Arthur" — a real forename from a real kinsman (proposed)

There was an Arthur Nedham in this family — a gentleman of London whose 1611 will, proved in 1613, declares him "intendinge shortly to travell into the parts beyonde the seas." He died some seventy years before Barbara was born and cannot be her father; but his was exactly the kind of memorable, seafaring family story that survives in tradition for centuries. The most economical explanation for "Arthur" is that this real kinsman lent his Christian name to the reconstructed father.

"Sir" — a borrowed dignity (erroneous)

This is the one element with no basis anywhere in the record. In every document that names them, the Wymondley Nedhams are "Esquire" or "gentleman" — never "Sir." Two real sources can account for the embellishment: the Needham name carried genuine rank elsewhere (the Viscounts Kilmorrey, and knighted bearers such as Sir Francis Nedham of Derbyshire), and community pedigrees have a long habit of quietly lending a grander branch's honours to a humbler one of the same surname. And a real knighthood sat immediately beside Barbara's own family — Sir Francis Wingate, knight, of Harlington, against whom her father Maurice actually litigated in 1684.

"A Wingate" — the wrong mother, from a real name (corrected)

The 1925 book gives Barbara a Wingate mother. The register gives her Letisia Bowyer. So "Wingate" is an error — but not a random one. The Nedhams and the Wingates were genuinely entangled: a Wingate married into the Nedham household, Sir Francis Wingate was a knight in the family's orbit, and in 1684 Maurice Nedham and his wife were in Chancery against Sir Francis Wingate. Tellingly, even this project's modern research was first drawn to a "Lettice Wingate" reading, and only abandoned it when the 1684 marriage register surfaced and named the bride Bowyer. The 1925 author made the same understandable substitution three centuries earlier.

Why this could be untangled now, and not in 1925

It is worth being clear about why the 1925 author could not have reached this result and we can. The difference is not diligence — Thomas P. de Graffenried was, if anything, unusually careful for his day. The difference is access and cross-reference. Resolving "Sir Arthur Needham" meant holding a dozen scattered records in view at once — four Prerogative Court wills, a Loseley feoffment, a decade of Chancery suits, a London marriage register, two Wymondley parish registers, the printed Visitations of Hertfordshire, and the Victoria County History's manorial descents — and reading them all against a single doubtful sentence. That kind of many-sourced collation is the new tool: the credit for the pedigree belongs to the records; the credit for making them legible all at once belongs to the method.

Conclusion

"Sir Arthur Needham of Wymondsley, Hertfordshire" never existed as a single historical person. He is the honest artifact of a blank the family itself acknowledged in 1735 and never filled — a blank the 1925 compiler closed, in good faith and with the limited resources of his time, by fusing the attributes of real people of Barbara's world: her true father Maurice Nedham; a real Arthur Nedham of the wider family; a knighthood borrowed from titled kin and from a neighbouring Sir Francis Wingate; and a Wingate surname, genuine in the family's history, standing in for Barbara's real mother, Letisia Bowyer.

The correction takes nothing away from the family's English gentility. The Nedhams of Wymondley were exactly that: a landed, armigerous house, lords of their manor, with a pedigree that can be followed name by name through primary record. It simply restores the true names. Barbara Tempest Needham was the daughter of a gentleman, not a knight; her mother was a Bowyer, not a Wingate; and the composite "Sir Arthur" can now be set respectfully aside.
