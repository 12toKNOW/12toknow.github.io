This is the chapter of the not-knowing, and it begins where the chapter before it ended: at the head of the lane, in the cold coming-on of a year, the master of the house gone down it on the place’s business and the house behind him taking up the day.

The house was the same south-side seat this history has kept all along — the seat Anton Tscharner DeGraffenreid had built on the Lunenburg ground in the early 1750s. It was the house of the lane and the great oak that stood at its head, the long oak table in the hall, and the family’s Bible kept on the writing-desk in the grandfather’s room, where it had lain since his father’s death in 1742 and would lie until his own in 1794. In 1775 the keeper of it was still the grandfather. Tscharner DeGraffenreid, born at Williamsburg in November 1722, was a man past fifty that year, and the Bible and the record in it were his. The present master was his son: Baker DeGraffenreid, born on the sixth of August 1744, a man of one-and-thirty, married to Sarah Vass, who was the steady center the household turned on. Two children had grown past the cradle — a son, Vincent, twelve that year, and a daughter, Mary Baker, eleven, born at the seat in the autumn of 1764, the child whose chapter the one before this was.

The keeping-place was the Lunenburg seat; but the household’s world in these years ran past it, south and west, to the Carolina line the master’s business lay across. Baker had affairs over the line, in Guilford County, North Carolina, and the last the record holds of him alive is a deed of trust he put his hand to there, on the twentieth of October 1775. The chapter opens in the season of that going, and the span it covers runs four years from it — to the second week of November 1779, and a wedding.

For the war had come. It had broken over the colonies that spring in the country to the north and would grind on the most of a decade; and what it asked of this house it asked at the house’s own scale and on its own clock. It would take the boy: in the deep of the winter of 1777 Vincent would go down the lane after his father, a substitute of fourteen sent to stand in a man’s place in the Virginia line, and be gone to the war until the war let him go. And the father it does not account for at all. Baker rode out over the line in 1775 and did not ride back, and where the four years between the Guilford deed and the wedding put him the surviving sources do not give back.

But those are the things seen at the line’s distance — the documented shape of four years, named and then left. The chapter is not at that distance. It is inside the house: the table that kept a place open, the lane looked up in the evenings, the desk where a girl learned to write a brother at the war. It is the not-knowing lived from within, a day and a meal and a letter at a time, by a household learning to be a thing it had not been — a household that did not know, and held, and went on. It begins with the overdue man.

The overdue man

The house took up the day behind her the morning he rode out, and took up the day after it, and the day after that, the way it took up every day — the fire fed and the water carried and the round of the place done with the one pair of hands gone out of it. He had ridden down the lane in the cold of the morning, under the oak at the head of it and past, out where the lane gave onto the road, and gone the way he had gone out on the place’s many goings, only farther: over the line into the Carolina country, on the place’s business, a going of more days than his short ones. And in the evenings, the way she always had, she looked for him up the lane.

She knew the shape of his coming. The horse would be a little thing at the head of the lane in the falling light, come on under the oak and up the long pale run of it and grow against the road, and the man up on it would be her father, in from over the line with the cold and the going on him. She had seen him come up the lane that way in the evening her whole life, more times than there was any counting, so that the coming was as plain a thing to her as the table or the fire — a thing that had always been and went on being. A going of more days came back up the lane when its days were done. So she looked up the lane for the thing that would grow and be him, and the evenings the looking found nothing she let go the way she had always let go the evenings he was not yet due — because his days were not done, and then because his days were done and a man was given a few more.

The few more went by. A man over the line on the place’s business could be held past his day by any of the ordinary things that held a man: the papers not ready, the men he had to see gone from home, the roads heavy with the wet of the year, a horse to be doctored, a thing on the land over there to be seen to before he could turn for home. The grown people knew the ordinary things that held a man, and turned them over among themselves, and set them down again; and the place went on at its round with the one pair of hands still out of it, the way it had gone on with him out of it before. What needed a man’s word the grandfather gave, leaned on the more now that the county was tilting and the men were oftener away; what wanted a man’s hand and was not past a boy, the boy was set to, Vincent being near enough now to a man’s size to be used at the edge of a man’s part; and the rest the women carried, the way they carried it any time he was gone. He was late. A late man was a thing the place knew. It had laid by his coming before, on this going and that, and the coming had always come — later than its day, but come, up the lane in the end with the man on it. So his lateness now lay among the ordinary lateness of all his other goings, and was, for a good while, only that.

But the table kept his place.

The long table was laid the way it was always laid, and his place at it was the place it had always been — the head, where the man of the house sat, where he had sat at every meal of her life he had been home to sit at. With him gone, the head of the table was an empty place, the way it was empty any time he was gone; they sat to their meals around it and the day closed over the gap of him the way it closed over any gap. Only it did not close all the way. Laid or not laid, the place stayed his — kept his by not being given to another, by the grown eyes going to it and off it, by the head of the table staying the head of the table with no man at it. The mother ran the meal from where she sat, and the grandfather was at his place, and the boy at his, and the child at hers, and the head of the table was his and empty, and the meal went on around it the way the day went on around the hands that were out of the round. They kept his place by keeping it his. That was the thing the table did now that it had not done before: it held a place open for a man who was late.

It was the mother who held it open. She had always been the still middle the house turned on, the one whose hands the day went through, and with the man out of it she was the more so. She ran the lane and the round and the meals and the meting-out of the work the short hands had to do, and she kept his place at the table the way she kept everything, without a word made of it, by the steady doing of it. If there was a thing in her about the lateness, she did not set it on the house. She looked up the lane in the evenings too — the child saw her at it, going to the door at the hour he would have come and looking up the long pale run of it the way the child looked, and turning back in when the light went without him on it — but she looked the way a woman looks for a man who is late, and no further than that, and the look she carried back into the house was a steady one, and the house took its steadiness from her and went on.

And the child, who was old enough now to catch what was not said, caught a thing in it she could not have put a name to and did not try. It was not in any one thing. It was in the place kept at the table a day longer than a place was kept, and a day longer than that; in the way the grown talk would come round to him and go quiet and come round to something else; in her mother at the door at the coming-on of the evening, looking up the lane and turning back; in the count that nobody kept out loud and that she felt being kept all the same, the days of his lateness going by one on another and the run of them getting longer. She caught it the way she caught the weather coming, or the held thing in the men’s low talk — not as a thing told her but as a thing there to be caught, a change in the house she lived inside of. She had no word for it, and it had not got to be fear. It was only that the lateness had a weight in it now that the ordinary lateness of his other goings had not had, a weight she felt at the edges of the days without knowing what it was the weight of. She looked up the lane in the evenings, and the looking had the weight in it now. She did not know that it did. She only looked, the way she had always looked, and the thing that would grow and be him did not come on under the oak, and the evening went, and the place at the table stayed his.

So the year went on over the house the way it went, down into the cold and the dark of it and toward the turn, and the man who had ridden out on the place’s business in the cold coming-on of the year had not ridden back up the lane by the cold deep of it. The place kept its round with the short hands. The mother kept the lane and the meals and his place at the table. The child looked up the lane in the evenings and did the next thing and the next, and the looking did not find him, and the place stayed his, and his lateness went on being lateness — a long lateness now, longer than any of his other goings had run, but lateness still: a man over the line and overdue, and not yet anything more than overdue, the household keeping his place open up the long pale lane, and the dark coming down over the oak at the head of it, and the road past it that he had gone out along and not yet come back.

Interlude — The Outbreak

While Baker DeGraffenreid was overdue on the Carolina roads, the thing that would come to be called the Revolution had already begun, two and three hundred miles to the north, on a scale no county on the south side could yet measure.

It had opened in the spring of 1775. In April of that year, on the road between Lexington and Concord in Massachusetts, militia and British regulars exchanged the first fire of the war. By June the Continental Congress at Philadelphia had voted an army into being and set a Virginian, George Washington, over it; that same month, on a hill above Boston, the two sides learned what the war would cost. The fighting spread and hardened through the year after — the siege of Boston and the British leaving it, the failed thrust into Canada, the war reaching south toward the Carolinas — until, in July of 1776, the Congress declared the colonies free states, and the thing became a war for a nation.

None of it came up the lane whole. It came the way news came to a farm at the country’s edge with its men away: in pieces and late, carried by a rider or a letter or a man home from the court day, a battle named weeks after it was fought and a hundred miles from where the hearer stood. The house had a war around it now, vast and northern and growing; it had a man overdue on it, and would soon have a boy in it; and it knew almost none of what the Congress had declared. It knew its own.

The boy goes

The deep of the winter was on the place the year the boy went. The man had been gone over the line a year and more by then — gone the length of a year’s round and into the next — and his place at the table was still his and still empty, kept his the way it had been kept from the first: the head of the table with no man at it, the lane looked up in the evenings, the round done with the one pair of hands out of it. The house had got used to the shape of that gap the way a body gets used to a stiffness it wakes with, not eased but carried, a thing the day was built around without the building being spoken of. And into that winter the grown people turned their talk to Vincent.

It was the kind of talk the child caught the edges of and was not in. There was a man owed to the war off the district — a draft to be answered, a place in the line to be filled — and a substitute would answer it, and the substitute, if the household sent one, would be the boy. He was near enough a man’s size now. He had been near it in the fall, used at the edge of a man’s part with the father’s hands gone from the round, and over the going of the one more winter he had come the rest of the way to where a boy of fourteen could stand in a grown man’s place in a muster book and the book would take him. The grandfather turned it over and the mother turned it over, and what passed between them about it passed low and mostly when the children were abed; and the child knew only that the talk was of her brother and the war, and went quiet when she came near, and came round again when she had gone.

What there was to be got by it she half caught and did not weigh — that a place filled by the household’s own boy was a place not filled by the drawing of another name the house would then have to answer; that there was in it something of the man owed and the bounty paid and the books made even, the war’s bookkeeping that reached even up the lane and asked the place for a body. She did not weigh it. It was grown business, arranged the way the grown business of the place was always arranged, over her head and around her, and what reached her of it was the end of it only: that Vincent was going for a soldier, and going soon.

He went in the cold of a morning, the way the father had gone in the cold of a morning. He went down the lane behind a man of the district who was carrying him to the gathering-place — down under the oak at the head of it and along the long pale run of it and out to where the lane gave onto the road: the same lane, the same oak, the same giving-onto, that the father had gone down and not yet come back up. The child stood at the door and watched the way she watched the evenings, only it was morning, and the thing she watched was a going and not a coming. The boy who had been the boy at the edge of a man’s part was the man’s part now, gone down the lane to stand in a man’s place in the line; and the door he went out of had two men’s goings in it, and the lane two, and the road past the oak two.

The mother saw him off the way she did the hard things, by doing them. She had him fed and his bundle made and what there was to send with him sent; and she stood at the door while he went, and lifted her hand to the man driving and not only to the boy, and turned back into the house when the seat had gone out of sight past the oak. If there was in her the thing there had been about the father’s lateness, she did not set it on the house this morning either. She had a second of her men down the lane now and the same steady look to carry back from the door, and she carried it back, and the house took its steadiness from her as it had, and the day took up behind her.

And the place took up the gap the boy left the way it had taken up the gap the father left. The hands were shorter by one again, and the part the boy had been used at — the wood and the water and the beasts and the heavier reach of the round that a near-grown boy could take — came back onto the hands that were left, the grandfather’s that were old for it and the women’s and the child’s that was younger than the part. What the boy had done got done by the ones still there, parceled out and taken up, a little more onto each, because it was there to be done and they were the ones to do it. The grandfather did the part a man past fifty could still do and got a hired hand or a neighbor’s boy for the part he could not, the way the district did its short-handed winters with its men away. The mother ran the round and the meals and the parceling-out the way she ran everything, by the steady doing. And the child was set to the more, being now the next pair of hands the place had — older than she had been, nearer the part, the way the boy had been nearer the part the winter before, the reach of the place coming down onto the next one as the ones above it were taken away.

None of it was made a thing of in words. There was no evening the house sat down and named what it would do now that the boy had gone after the man. The work the boy had done was there in the morning, and in the morning the ones left got up and did it, and did it again the morning after, and the gap the boy left closed over the way the gap the father left had closed over — not all the way, never all the way, but enough that the round went on and the house held and the days came one after another and were got through. The doing was the answer. The house did not say what it would do; it did it.

And one of the things it did, now, was write to the boy.

A soldier could be written to. He was gone, but not gone the way the father was gone. The father had gone into a place with no name they had and no road an answer could come back along; to the father there was no writing. But the boy was in the line, and the line was somewhere, and a letter could go to him by the hands that letters went by — slow and far and few — and another come back. So the house, which had no way to write to the man, wrote to the boy.

And the writing of him fell, in part, to the child. She had the hand for it — the family’s hand, the one the grandfather wrote the record in and had taught down the line; she had sat at the desk under his eye and made her letters, and could make them plain enough now to be read far off by a boy with little time and less light to read by. So when there was writing to Vincent to be done that the mother’s hands were too full for, or that the grandfather left to the child as a way of the hand getting its use, the child did it. She sat at the parlor desk where the writing of the place was done and wrote her brother the things there were to write — the plain news of the place, who was well and what was done and how the winter went — and folded it, and it went; and after a while, slow and far, the boy’s hand came back, the same blood’s hand making the same letters at the other end of the road, and the few things he could say of where he was.

So each was kept in the other’s hand — the brother in the sister’s, written to and waited on; the sister in the brother’s, the far proof that he was yet a hand that could write, yet somewhere, yet himself. It was the plainest thing, a boy gone for a soldier and a sister at the desk; and it was the first of a thing that would go on between them, the letters back and forth, each of them the other’s word — for the while it took the word to come — that the other was still there.

So the house went down into the rest of that winter with two of its men gone down the lane: the one a year and more gone into the place with no name, his seat at the table still his and still empty and still looked for up the lane in the evenings; the other gone that morning into the line, written to and waited on, his place at the table emptied too and kept too, a boy’s place that was a soldier’s now. The child looked up the lane in the evenings the way she always had, for the father — the boy was not a thing the lane would bring now, he was where the letters went and not where the evenings could reach — but she looked all the same, and the looking had the both of them in it. And the place did the next thing and the next, and the round went on with the hands it had, and the winter went over the house toward the turn, and the two places at the table stayed the men’s whose they were, and the lane ran out past the oak to the road that the two of them had gone down.

Interlude — The Northern Campaigns

The winter the boy went down the lane, early in 1777, the war he was going into had a year and a half of hard use behind it and no end in sight.

Through that year the heaviest fighting was in the north, around the rebel capital. In September a British army beat Washington’s on the Brandywine, in Pennsylvania, took Philadelphia, and sent the Congress fleeing its own city. A month later, far up the Hudson country in New York, a second British army that had come down out of Canada was surrounded and made to surrender entire at Saratoga — the turn of the war, as it came afterward to be reckoned, the victory that brought France in on the American side. Then the northern front went quiet and long: two more years of marching and waiting and bitter, inconclusive fighting, the armies wintering and maneuvering, the war grinding on without a decision.

Where in all of it was Vincent? The household could not say. It had a boy in the Virginia line and a war full of named places he might be at, or near, or nowhere close to, and no way to lay the one over the other. What the news carried was armies; what the letters carried, when they carried anything, was a boy. The house could not put the one inside the other. So the not-knowing of the father had a second laid beside it now, lesser and live: a brother somewhere in the great northern noise, written to and waited on, and not to be found in any account that reached the lane.

The long not-knowing & the arrangement

The years went on over the house the way the one winter had, and the war came up the lane the only way it could, which was in pieces and late. It came by the man who had been to the court house and back and had it from a paper, or from a man who had it from a paper; by the paper itself when a paper came, a month or two months behind the thing it told; by the word passed at the meeting-house of a Sunday, or brought by a neighbor who had a son in it the same as they had. A battle to the north, when the word of it reached them, had been fought a season gone; a campaign down through the lower country was a thing they heard the front of while the back of it was still happening, if it was happening, off where no word from them could reach and no word to them came any faster than a horse could carry it over the bad roads in the wet of the year. So the war that the rest of the world had whole, day on day, the house had in fragments, out of their order and out of their season, and made what it could of them and let the rest go by.

And Vincent’s letters came up the lane the same way, slow and far and few, and were a different thing from the war-news and would not lie against it. The war-news told of armies; the letters told of a boy — that he was well, or had been when the hand wrote it; the marching and the food and the few things he could say of where he was, which was never enough to set a pin in. When the news came of a fight to the north the house could not know if he had been in it or a hundred miles off from it; and when his letter came it was a month behind the news and answered none of it, because he wrote the ordinary soldier-things and not the great ones, or because the great ones had not reached him either, off where he was. So the household held two things that would not meet — the war it heard of and the brother it heard from — and could not put the one inside the other, and learned to carry them apart: the news for the world’s account, and the letters for the slow far proof that he was yet a hand that could write.

The writing of him went on falling, in good part, to the child, and over the going of the years it made her hand a surer thing. She had the family’s hand from the grandfather, taught down at the parlor desk under his eye, and she wrote her brother plain and close so a boy with little light could read it; and in the spring she was thirteen the grandfather gave her a book of her own — plain calf, the leather pale, the pages empty — and put it in her hands and said she might write in it the doings of the house through the year of the war, the way he had written the doings of his own in the family’s record all his life. She took it and did not rightly know what she would do with it, and put it by, and came to it slowly, a line and then a line, the clear hand setting down what the place did and how the year went. So there were two writings of the place going from that desk now: the one that went down the road to the boy, and the one that stayed and filled a slow line a day with the doing of the days.

And somewhere in the going of those years the thing in the house about the father changed, with no day to fix it on. There had been the late man, and then the long-late man, and his place kept at the head of the table the way it had been kept from the first; and the looking up the lane in the evenings had gone on, the mother at the door and the child at it, the long pale run looked up for the thing that would grow and be him. But a man over the line a year was late; a man over the line two years was a thing the word late would no longer cover, and three was a thing with no word at all that the household had ever had to find. No body came up the road. No grave was sent word of. No man rode up the lane to tell them a thing they could write down. Nothing came; and out of the nothing coming, year on year, the house came by slow degrees to live a way it had never been told to live and had never once said aloud — as though the head of the table would not be sat in again, as though the lane would not bring him, as though the looking, gone on so long for the man, had quietly turned into a looking that did not any longer look to find.

The child felt it cross in herself before she had a word to put on it. She went to the door at the coming-on of an evening, the way she always had, and looked up the long pale run for the thing that would grow and be him; and she found, standing there, that the looking had gone out of her — that she was at the door from habit and not from waiting, that her body had given up the lane some while before the rest of her had been told to, and that what she did at the door now was not watch for a coming but stand a moment while the dark came down over the oak, and turn back in. She did not say to herself that he was not coming. She could not have said it; there was nothing to say it from, no word off any road, no body, no grave, no day. She only found the waiting gone out of her hands and her eyes, gone the way a thing goes that has had nothing to feed it for too long; and she turned back into the house, and the house was a house that had crossed the same thing the same way, each one of them come to it alone at the door and none of them speaking it at the table. The place at the head was kept his. They went on keeping it. But what they kept it for had changed under them — from a place held for a man who was late to a place held for a man gone wherever the unanswered goings went — and no day had told them so, and they had told no day.

And in the autumn of the third year the one place changed where a change could be set down. A man gone past all his days, with an estate and a wife and children and land that the law would want a head for, could not be left a late man forever in the eyes of the court the way he could in the eyes of his own table. There were the things of the place that wanted a hand the law would own — the land, the debts owed it and the debts it owed, the boy’s bounty, the running of the whole — and a wife who had been running them all along by the steady doing could be given the running of them in the one place that asked a name on a paper. So in the September of that year Sarah Vass went before the court at Lunenburg, by the grandfather’s hand to guide her, who was risen now in the affairs of the county as the war stripped the neighborhood of its men, and was made administratrix of her husband’s estate — which was the court’s plain way of saying the thing the house had come to long before and never said: that there was no man at the head of it to answer for it, and the answering must pass to the wife. The record gave him up. It had been the one place still holding him a living man — the rolls, the form, the law’s long patience that lets a man be absent a great while before it gives his place over — and now even the record let the place go and gave the running to the wife; and the wife who had been a widow in the doing of every day for three years was a widow now in the one book that could say so.

And yet the table did not give him up. That was the double thing of that autumn the house lived inside of and did not name: that in the September the record let his place go, and the head of the table stayed his all the same. The court could give a man over to the running of his affairs, because what the court had held was a name against an estate, and the estate wanted a head, and the wife was made it. But what the table had held was not a name against an estate. It was his place — the head where the man of the house had sat at every meal of the child’s life he had been home to sit at — and that was not a thing a court could be asked to give up or had any way to take. So the two places parted that autumn, the one in the book and the one in the room: the book’s place given over, the running of it passed to the wife the law now owned a widow; the table’s place kept his, the head of it the head of it still, with no man at it. The house had its man given up in law and kept at the board, and went on, and did not find the two a contradiction, because the one was a thing done for the court and the other a thing done for him.

And in the same year the mother took the estate in hand she took in hand the other thing a mother of a girl that age had to see to, which was the girl. For Mary was near grown now — fourteen, and going on the age her mother had been not many years before her own marrying — and a daughter to be provided for was as much the business of a house with its men gone as the land was, and more, the way the times ran. So across that year the grown talk the child caught the edges of, the way she had caught the talk of Vincent, turned by and by to a name: Gideon Johnson, a young man of a household down the Dan, a private of the Carolina line who had been away in it and was now, in the way of the war, home — a name spoken among the older heads with the weight of a thing being weighed, gone quiet when she came near and come round again when she had gone, the same as the Vincent-talk had gone quiet and come round.

And then the name was a man. She met him at a cousin’s house, in the way such meetings were made to fall out and were never called what they were — the families come together of a Sunday, the young man home from the line and at his uncle’s board, the girl brought along and set where she would be seen and would see. He was a man of four-and-twenty, quiet, with the war on him the way it was on the men who had been in it and come back; and he was civil to her and to the house, and she looked at him the way she was given leave to look and found in him nothing to fear and a good deal that was steady. She was asked nothing that day. She was shown a man and let to see him, and he a girl, and the grown business of it went on over her head and around her the way it always had — but she was near enough now to the size of it to know what she was being shown, and to weigh the man she was shown in her own measure; and what she weighed came out steady.

And the rest of it was made the way the times made such things, in good part by the road — the mother corresponding it out with the young man’s father, the terms and prospects of it going back and forth by the same slow post that carried the boy’s letters, a thing arranged across a distance because the distance was what the war left them to arrange it across. And in the spring the young man rode up to the grandfather’s house and asked it — asked the grandfather, and asked the mother, and then, which was not the way the child had been told the asking went, asked the girl herself, apart, the supper done and the candle between them. What passed there was hers; she carried it out of that room her own and would tell it after in her own time. But the end of it, that the house came to know, was that he had asked and she had said yes — not driven to it and not given away to it, a girl inside her mother’s providing and not under it, who had been shown a steady man and had weighed him and had found, when the asking came to her and not over her, that the yes was hers to give. She was not in love with him, the way the songs would have it; she was not against him in any part of her; she was a girl going on fifteen who had looked at the man set before her and had not been afraid, and had said the word, and meant it the more for having been let to say it.

So the house came round to the back end of that year with its two long pressures come at last to their hinges, and neither come to rest. The father was given up in the law and kept at the table — his place let go in the one book and held in the one room — and no word had come up any road to tell them more of him than they had told themselves. The daughter was promised, the day set for the November, the young man to ride up with his people and the grandfather to come down from Lunenburg for it. The boy was away in the line still, written to and waited on, the letters going down the road and the slow ones coming back, the war he was in heard of in fragments the house could not lay him into. And the round went on with the hands it had — the mother running it a widow in law now and a widow in fact these three years, the grandfather risen in the county’s affairs and leaned on the more, the daughter set to the full of a near-grown woman’s part and the desk besides, the letters to her brother and the book filling its slow line a day. The parlor desk was where it all came from now, the writing that went down the road and the writing that stayed, the family’s hand at both. And the year turned on toward the November and the wedding, and the place at the head of the table stayed his, and the lane ran out past the oak to the road no answer had ever come back along.

The writing-evening

The evening of the second week of November came down over the house early and cold, the dark gone into the oak before the supper things were cleared away; and when they were cleared, and the fire banked low for the night, and the mother gone in to her bed, the house drew its quiet in around the one place where a candle still burned, and the girl sat down before the desk to write her brother the things he would want to know.

She was fifteen now, and the writing of him had been hers for some years, and she came to it this evening the way she had come to the whole of the part that had fallen to her across the war — not set to it by anyone, but down to it of herself, the body to the chair and the hand to the pen ahead of any telling herself it was the hour. She drew the candle nearer and squared a sheet and uncapped the ink. The desk was the family’s, the one she had been taught the hand at under the grandfather’s eye when she had been a child wanting the cushion to reach it; and the hand it had taught her was hers now, close and plain and sure, the hand a boy by a poor light in a far camp could read without straining over it. She wrote her brother in it, and the news she had to send was the news the house had, which was a great deal that November and the most of it her own.

She told him the wedding was set for the eighteenth — that the young man would ride up with his people in the forenoon of that day and the thing would be done, and that her brother was to think of them all from wherever the road had him by then. She told him the grandfather would come down from Lunenburg for it and would bring the Bible, to write the marriage into the record where the family’s marriages had been written time out of mind, and that he was to ride down the week before and lie the night in the house. She told him their mother was well. And she told him the place went on as he had left it going — the round kept up with the hands it had, the corn in the loft, the year’s business answered as it came due — that the house held.

She wrote it all by the one candle in the still house, and behind her in the dark of the next room the head of the table was kept his the way it had been kept these four years, and out beyond the wall the lane ran past the oak to the road that had carried no word of him up it in all that time and could carry none of him down it that she might send. She wrote her brother none of that. There was nothing in it to tell him that he did not already carry, the same as she carried it; it was not news; it was the ground the news stood on. So she wrote him the wedding and the grandfather and the mother and the holding of the place, which were the things there were to say, and let the rest stand under them unsaid, the way it stood under the days and went unsaid at the table too.

And she came to the foot of the page. The letter was down — the telling of it done, the news all told — and there was the close of it to make, and her name to put to it, the way the grandfather had taught her a letter was closed: the writer’s name under the last line, so that the one who broke it open a month gone and a hundred miles off would know whose hand had carried the words that far. She dipped the pen for the name. And before the name there wanted something more — not news, for the news was told, and not a wish for him, though she wished him home and whole — but a word for the whole of it, for the thing the letter was beneath all its telling, the thing the house had been doing for four years and had never once said what it was. She understood that now, at the foot of the page, in a way she had not had to before: that the house had never had a word for what it did, that it had only done it, through the lateness and the long not-knowing, and the place given up in the book and kept at the board, and the year come round again to a wedding. But the doing had wanted its word all that while, the way a thing long done and never named comes at the last to want the naming; and sitting there with the pen wet in her hand she found the word the doing had wanted, which was two words; and she wrote them.

She put her name under them — Mary Baker — and let the ink dry, and read the page once over by the candle, the news and the two words and the name, and folded it along its creases. She held the stick of wax to the flame until it ran and let it fall on the folded edge and pressed it under her thumb while it cooled and set. Then she stood the letter up on the corner of the desk against the morning, when the carrier would go down the road to the south with the post and it would start the long slow way to her brother and reach him when it reached him, a fortnight off or more, the road being what the war had made of it and the camp being wherever the camp had moved to by the time the letter came to the road’s end.

The candle had length in it yet. She did not put it out. She drew to her instead the other book, the one that went nowhere — the plain calf the grandfather had set in her hands the spring she was thirteen, bidding her write in it the doings of the house through the year of the war, that she had come to slowly and had filled since a slow line at a time. She turned to where she had last left off, and dipped the pen again. And in it, that same evening, the letter sealed and standing for the morning, she wrote the two words over — not the wedding, which had its plain line down already in its place, and not the day’s round, which would have its line after as the days had had theirs; but the two words by themselves, under the date, in the book that stayed in the house when the letter had gone out of it.

Then she closed the book and laid it by. The wax on the letter had gone hard. The house was still all about her — the mother asleep beyond the wall, the head of the table kept his in the dark of the next room, the lane outside running the two ways it ran, down toward the boy the letter would go to and out toward the road no answer had ever come back along. The wedding was six days off. She sat a moment more before the desk with the two writings of the evening done between her hands — the one to go down the road in the morning and the one to stay in the house, and the same two words signed under her own name in both of them — and then she put out the candle, and the one lit place in the house went dark with the rest, and she went up to her bed.

The candle the girl put out that evening was put out six days short of her wedding; the days between were not still ones.

The morning after she sealed her letter — the thirteenth of November 1779 — a grant went out under the hand of Richard Caswell, governor of North Carolina, of four hundred acres in Guilford County to Sarah DeGraffenreid. The widow who had been administering an absent man’s estate and keeping his place at the head of the table was made, by that document, a freeholder in her own name, in the Carolina country the family had long been drifting toward. Five days later, on the eighteenth, the wedding was done. Mary Baker DeGraffenreid married Gideon Johnson — the younger of his name, born in Amelia County, Virginia, on the seventh of November 1754, a private of the North Carolina line before the war was out. The grandfather had ridden down from Lunenburg with the Bible and wrote the marriage into the record in the French hand he had kept the family’s names in for forty years. What the girl had written under her own name twice that November is not in the Bible; the Bible carries the marriage and nothing else of the evening.

The boy came home. Vincent served in the Virginia line to the autumn of 1781, and came back up a lane to a house, the brother-and-sister letters ended by the plainest means; what he had seen in those years is not this chapter’s to tell.

The father did not come home, and the record never says he died. The last it holds of Baker DeGraffenreid alive is the Guilford deed of October 1775; the next is his wife acting as administratrix of his estate by September 1779, which marks his absence and not its cause. Between them is the silence this chapter was the inside of. Family tradition holds that he was killed at the outbreak of the war; this history carries the tradition, has not found the proof, and leaves the gap a gap. Where he died, and when, and by what, are not given back.

And the line went on. In October of 1780 the first child of the new household was born — a daughter, Sarah Johnson, the ninth of that month — and the descent this history follows runs through her. The Bible stayed on the grandfather’s desk at Lunenburg, his to keep until 1794, and then came to Mary, the names going on in it after the French hand was done. What the house had learned to be across those four years — a household that did not know, and held, and went on — it did not unlearn at the wedding; the girl carried the thing itself out of her mother’s house into her own. The chapter ends not on the evening but on the line: a marriage written, a daughter coming, a father unaccounted and not abandoned, and the keeping moving down the years toward the hand it had not yet reached.
