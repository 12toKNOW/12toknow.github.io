The household on the farm south of the railroad was a household of nine. Robert Edwin Searcy worked the general farm in the Third Beat of Alcorn County and was fifty years old that autumn; his wife, Hattie Guthrie Searcy, was three-and-forty and kept the house and the six children in it — Joseph near grown and turning his hand in the field, Mary behind him, and the little ones down to the twins Lester and Esther, just six with their birthday not a fortnight past, and the youngest still a baby carried on a hip. And the ninth of them, the oldest living thing under that roof, was his mother, Mary Jane Rorie Searcy, who had been the matriarch of every house she had lived in since the year her own mother died, and was the matriarch of this one — though it was her son's house and not her own, and she had not kept a house of her own in a dozen years.

She had given up her own house after her husband died. She had kept it one-and-forty years, on the ground she had come to as a bride at thirty-four, and when Freeman was two years in the earth she had let it go and come to live in her son's, a widow, and had carried the two Bibles over in her own lap and set them on the shelf at the south wall of the front room with her own hands — the Rorie Bible in its worn black calf to the right, the Hubbard people's book brought down out of North Carolina before any of them were born, and Aaron Searcy's working Bible in the smaller leather to the left, in the little satchel the old man had carried his keeping in. They had not come down off that shelf since she set them there. She had been a widow twelve years and eight months. She was, on the second Sunday of October, the oldest living thing on the line she had spent the whole of her life keeping.

It was the shape the house had always made of its women, and she knew it better than anyone, having watched it come round twice before it came round to her. Her grandmother had come down from Tennessee in her sixties and taken the bed in the corner of a daughter's kitchen, and Mary Jane had laid her out at twenty-four. Her mother had given the Rorie place up at the last and gone to live in a daughter's house, and had been set down there, an old woman in a household not her own, under the one plain word the census kept for such women — mother, seventy-five. And now it had come round to Mary Jane. She had given her own house up and come to her son's, and in the January of that last year a census taker had written her into the book of her final months exactly as her mother had been written into hers: mother, in a child's house, on the same dirt none of the three of them had ever left. One fate, the three of them, a generation apart each time. The one thing the record marked differently between her and the two before her was the age: where the marshal had asked it, she had given him eighty-seven, and she was right, and she had been right every time in her life that anyone had asked her — which was more than the house she was counted in could have said for its own. She had watched this thing travel down the family her whole life. She was the third to arrive at it, and she knew she was, and she had come to it the way she came to everything, without complaint and with the housekeeping done.

The deaths page of that Bible ended where her mother had left it — her father in the summer of 1862, her brother killed at Atlanta in the summer of 1864, both set down in her mother's careful round hand — and after that the column held nothing, and it held nothing for her mother either, whose own dying had gone into no page anywhere. She had kept the book more than thirty years and never added a death to that page, not her husband's when his time came nor any other; the keeping of that column had closed with the war, before the book was ever hers. And she had never once stood at the marriages page to find her own line there, because her own line had never been written. She had known that the way she knew her own name. It had stopped troubling her a long time ago.

· · ·

She did not come down to the kitchen at the dawn of that Sunday.

By six Hattie had heard her overhead, the way she had heard her every morning for years — the slow cross of the room, the give of the boards where the old woman went first to the window to look at the weather. Then the boards stayed quiet. The kitchen clock went past the quarter, then the half. Hattie poured the coffee and stood over the stove listening for the step that did not come. Two of the children were already at the table waiting to be fed and she hushed them low, without knowing yet why she did it, only that the quiet overhead was the wrong quiet.

At a quarter past seven she went to the foot of the stair and called up. The answer came back small.

She went up. The door of the south room stood half open, and Mary Jane was sitting on the edge of the bed by the window with her bare feet on the floor and her hands flat on the quilt at her knees. The pitcher on the washstand stood full from the night's draw and the basin was dry. She had not washed.

"Mama, are you sick?" Hattie said.

Mary Jane did not turn from the window. "It's a chill," she said. "I didn't sleep past three. My shoulder's stiff on the right." She drew a breath. "I'll sit here a while."

Hattie went down and came back with the brown shawl off the front-room chair and a cup of coffee. She set the cup on the stand and put the shawl over the old woman's shoulders, and Mary Jane took the cup up in her left hand. The right hand stayed where it was on the quilt. Hattie watched it a moment. It lay open and easy and did not rise.

"It's stiff," Mary Jane said, before she was asked. "It'll come back with the warm."

It did not come back. She knew it was not coming back. She had known since she woke in the dark and reached for the quilt's edge to pull it up, and her own hand had not answered her — had lain there at the end of her arm like a thing set down on the bed, warm, hers, and not hers to lift. She had lain still a long time after that. She had thought, listening to the full house breathe around her, all those children asleep down the hall and under it, that she had come to the morning her own mother had come to, and her mother's mother before that, when the body begins the quiet business of setting the keeper down. She was not afraid of it. She had watched it come for everyone she had ever loved, and she had set every one of them into the page herself, and she knew the shape of it from the outside the way a woman who has sat with the dying knows it, and now she was inside the shape and it was the same shape, only quieter. The thing she felt, lying there in the gray, was not fear. It was the old housekeeping pull, the one that had got her out of every bed for seventy years: there are things to be set in order before the day gets on.

The first of them was her sons. There were three of them living, and only the one under this roof.

She drank a little of the coffee. It was good and hot and her left hand was steady on the cup. Out the south window the field had gone to stubble and the one tree in the fence-corner had turned its October red, and past it the land ran flat and quiet the way it had run flat and quiet the whole of her life, whatever county they said it was in that year. She looked at it a while. Then she set herself to the day.

"Send word to your brothers," she said. "William and Frank. Send it this morning, while the roads are light. Tell them their mother's took poorly and to come if they can come."

"I'll send word, Mama."

"This morning."

"This morning," he said. "I'll set it going now."

· · ·

No doctor came, because none was sent for, and none was sent for because there was nothing a doctor could do that the house could not do as well, and the person under that roof who knew it best was the one in the bed.

Robert Edwin had turned it over, standing in the yard in the early light after Hattie called him up. There was a doctor in town, a long way off over bad October roads, and one could be fetched at a cost the farm could bear if there were a use in the fetching. But he had seen what was in the south room — the hand that would not answer, the corner of the mouth gone soft, the right foot slack under the quilt — and he knew a stroke of paralysis when he saw one, the way everyone raised on a farm in that country knew one when they saw one; it took the old the way the frost took the last of the garden, and there was no medicine against it and never had been. A doctor would ride out and stand in the room and say the word Robert Edwin already had for it, and take his fee, and ride home, and it would change nothing. And his mother, who had sat with more of the dying than any doctor in that county ever had and had made ready more of the dead than she could have counted, would not have thanked him for spending good money to fetch a stranger to tell the family a thing the family already knew.

So he did not send. He went up instead, and pulled the straight chair from the wall over to the window, and sat at his mother's left side and took her left hand, and the hand closed on his. She had a strong grip in that hand still.

"I'll be here, Mama," he said. "Hattie'll bring you broth at noon. The word's gone for William and Frank."

"Good," she said. Her eyes were steady on his face — the left side of it her own, the right side gone a little slack. She let the eyes close a moment, and her mind went where a mind goes when it is tired and the body has begun to let go of it — back, easily, the way a current takes a boat that has stopped being rowed. She thought of her own mother in the small bed off the kitchen at the cabin, talking low in the dark years and years ago, and she half-listened for that low voice now and could nearly hear it, and she knew the cabin was long gone and her mother longer, and she did not mind that the two times had come up close against each other in her head. They had been coming closer for a while. She let them.

"Where'd you go, Mama?" he said.

"I'm here," she said, which was what her mother used to say. She opened her eyes. "Is the word gone?"

"It's going now, Mama."

· · ·

The household kept the kind of Sunday that does not disturb an upper room.

Hattie fed the children and set the older ones to keeping the little ones quiet in the far end of the house, and sent the eldest boy out to do the stock so the yard stayed still. The word for William and Frank had gone off toward the railroad in the morning, the way Robert Edwin had promised — to go on by wire to wherever the two brothers were at their work, a day's travel and more between the sending and any coming. Robert Edwin sat the watch. At noon Hattie carried up a tray with broth and a cup of milk, and Mary Jane took five spoons of the broth from her son's holding of the bowl and would not take the milk, and turned her head to the window and the red in the fence-corner and said nothing through the noon. The boy Lester came to the door of the room and stood in it and did not come in, and Hattie took his hand and brought him away.

Late in the afternoon the old woman's eyes opened on Hattie at the bedside, and held on her.

"Robert sent word to your boys this morning, Mama," Hattie said. "To William, and to Frank."

"That was right of him," Mary Jane said. She did not say that they would come. She had done the arithmetic of roads her whole life, and she knew what a day's travel was, and what the night between might do, and she did not spend herself on the hoping. Her eyes went from Hattie to her son and back, and her left hand worked once at the quilt, and the right hand lay where it had lain all day. "You've been good to me, daughter," she said to Hattie — she had never had a daughter of her own, and had called this one daughter for a dozen years and meant it. "All this while you've heard me on the floor of a morning and never once let on you'd rather not."

"I'd rather, Mama," Hattie said, and could not say more, and put her hand on the old woman's shoulder, and Mary Jane closed her eyes and was quiet.

At dark she was where she had been that morning — the weakness no worse and no better, the speech clear, the breathing even. No doctor rode out to say so; the house said it to itself, in the way a house that has nursed its own knows how a body is doing without being told. Robert Edwin settled it that he would sit up with her, and said as much to Hattie in the front room under the shelf at the south wall — that she should sleep across the hall with the door open, and that if the breathing changed or the speech went he would call her. Outside there were no lights anywhere in the dark but the one lamp burning in the upstairs window, and the fields lay black to the rail and past it, and the road to town ran empty in the cold, because no one was on it and no one was coming.

Before nine he and Hattie moved her to the bed — Hattie at the left and Robert Edwin at the right, the both-hands carry the household had always used for the old at the last — and laid her in the middle of it with the quilt to her shoulders and the brown shawl folded by her side. The right arm did not help in the lifting. The right hand had not opened or closed since the dark. Hattie washed her face with a wet cloth, and Mary Jane took a swallow of water from the cup at her son's hand, and said, with her eyes already closing, "The lamp on the stand."

"Yes, Mama. The lamp'll stand the night."

Hattie took the two youngest to the far room and lay down with them with the door open. Robert Edwin sat the straight chair at the window and held his mother's left hand and did not sleep. The clock struck the eleventh hour, and the twelfth, and the small hours after, and the breathing went on even, and the lamp burned its steady flame on the stand.

· · ·

The second one came near three in the morning.

He felt it before he understood it — the breath going from even to uneven while he was down in the gray half-sleep of the chair — and he was on his feet at the bedside before he was awake. The right hand had drawn into a fist it had not made all day. Her left eye was open. The right eye was open and did not find him.

"Hattie, quick," he said at the far door, low, and she came up out of sleep and stood in the doorway in her shift, and the children did not wake.

He sat on the edge of the bed at his mother's right shoulder and took the closed right hand in one of his and laid his other hand on her forehead, and the forehead was cool. The breathing slowed. Hattie sat down in the straight chair at the window where he had kept the day and did not speak. And then his mother's eyes came back — the left one found his face and held it, the way it had held it every morning and every evening of his life.

"Robert," she said. The word came clear off the left side of her mouth.

"I'm here, Mama," he said.

"The Bible," she said. "At the south wall."

"It's on the shelf, Mama," he said. "The Rorie to the right. The working Bible to the left, in the satchel. It's all where you set it."

"Yes," she said. And then, after a breath: "Yes."

She closed her eyes. The breathing came small and slow. Hattie reached from the chair and laid her hand on the old woman's ankle through the quilt at the foot of the bed, because it was the part of her she could reach, and held it. Robert Edwin held the left hand in both of his. The lamp burned.

The clock struck four below, and he did not hear it, or heard it the way a man in a sickroom hears a thing that is not the breathing he is listening for. She breathed, and he thought, in the quiet of it: she's at the same place. She's not going. She breathed again, smaller. The clock's hand went on toward the half. Then, at half past four in the morning, she did not breathe.

He held the hand. He did not let go of it. He waited for the next breath the way a man waits for a thing he has always been able to count on, and it did not come, and he waited longer. The hand in his stayed warm and did not cool and did not move. He said her name once, and again, and waited through the long minutes a man waits before he will let himself know the thing, and then he laid the hand down on the quilt at her left side and stood up.

"She's gone," he said.

"She is, Robert," Hattie said, from the chair, with her hand over her mouth and the silent crying come up in her shoulders.

He put his right hand to his eyes a moment. Then he did the thing there was to do, because a man does the thing there is to do — he drew the quilt up over the still face the way it is done, and turned the lamp down but did not put it out, and went out himself into the yard, because he could not yet be in the house, and stood at the fence in the October cold with his breath white in front of him and the fields black to the rail and no light anywhere but the one lamp in the window above and behind him, and did not weep, because the standing in the cold was a thing he could do and the weeping was not, and a man holds to what he can do.

No one came at the gray of first light to tell him she was at peace, and no one needed to, because he had been the one at the bedside and he knew it already, the way he knew few things in his life so plainly. When the light came up at the east window he went back in and stood over her a while. She had gone easy at the end, in the dark, the way the old and good go, and she had known him — the left eye had found his face and held it, and she had said his name and asked after the Bible, and that was the last of her, and it was a good last, and that was the thing the son kept. He said it once, low, to Hattie, who had come to stand beside him: "She knew me at the last." And Hattie said, "She did," and put her hand on his arm, and the two of them stood over the old woman in the first light and let it be true.

· · ·

He woke the house at first light and took the boy aside.

He had not slept. He had sat the chair again at his mother's side through the close of the night with Hattie beside him, and when the gray came up at the east window he went down and washed his own face and put the kettle on, and told the older children low, and left Hattie to the little ones, and then he brought Lester out to the porch step and sat him down and sat down at his right.

"I've something to tell you, son," he said.

"Yes, papa."

"Your grandmother went in the night. She wasn't in any pain. She went easy, in the dark, the way the old and good go."

The boy looked at his father a while. He did not cry. He put his fingers along the edge of the step the way he did. "She was in the chair at noon yesterday," he said.

"She was."

"She didn't drink the milk."

"She took the broth," Robert Edwin said. "She didn't want the milk."

The boy thought about that. "Is she upstairs," he said.

"She is, for now. Your mama and the women'll see to her this morning, and I'll see to the box. Your uncles are on the road — the word's gone to William and to Frank. We'll bury her tomorrow at Pine Grove."

"Will I be there," the boy said.

"You'll stand at my right hand and your mama's. You can cry if you've a mind to, son. There's no shame in it at a burying. The Searcys have always cried honest at their burying-days."

"I won't cry," the boy said, deciding it, and Robert Edwin let him have the deciding, because a man lets a boy keep what a boy can keep.

No undertaker came out, in the way no doctor had come out — because the family did the thing itself, the way the family had always done the thing itself, and because to send to town for a stranger to wash and dress and box their own dead would have been a thing none of them would have known how to want. What the undertaker's trade did for money in the town, the women and the men of that country did for love and duty on the place, and had done for as long as anyone could remember, and did now.

The women saw to her. Hattie and a neighbor woman or two carried up the warm water and the good dress and the clean cloths, and they washed the old body and dressed it and folded the hands and set the white hair right — the plain necessary office, done in the south room with the light coming in low, by younger hands. And there was a thing in that the family felt and did not say, because it did not need saying: that Mary Jane Rorie Searcy had done this very office, with these very motions, over more of her own dead than she could have counted — had washed and dressed her grandmother at twenty-four, and her father, and her husband, and a long roll of others across seventy years, had been the one steady pair of hands a family turns to when there is a body to make ready — and that now the hands doing it were not hers. She was the one being made ready. The layer-out was laid out, gently, by the daughter-in-law she had called daughter for a dozen years and had taught the work to without once meaning to teach it; and the office came down to the next pair of hands the way the keeping did, and the way everything in that family came down — quietly, in the doing, from the one who could no longer do it to the one who now could.

And Robert Edwin saw to the box. He and his brothers when they came, or he alone before they did, took good seasoned plank down out of the barn — pine, the wood that country buried its people in — and measured it against the length of her, which was not long, and planed it and squared it and joined it, and made a coffin with their own hands. It was a plain box, made the way a farm makes a thing it needs and cannot buy or will not: square, and true, and honest. There was no bought casket and no bought lining and no undertaker's price. There was a homemade coffin, built on the place by the men of the family for the woman who had held the family together the whole of their lives; and if it was plain, it was plain the way she had been plain, and it was made right, because a Searcy made a thing right, and this was the last thing the family would ever make for her.

The brothers came in the night and the next morning, off the roads, the way the old woman had known they might and had not let herself count on. What passed between the three sons in that house is not the book's to set down; they were grown men at their mother's death and they kept their own counsel. It is enough that by the burying there were three of Reuben Rorie's grandsons standing where one had stood the day before.

· · ·

The State of Mississippi made one paper of her, and the family had carried the truth of her the whole way without it.

In the days after the burying, the rest of the paper was taken down from Robert Edwin's own mouth, because he was the informant of record and there was no one else the state could get her from — no doctor had attended her and none signed her out. Where the certificate asked what physician had seen her, and what test had confirmed the cause, the answers came back in a plain hand: No Doctor, and No. The cause the state carried, paralysis, was the family's own word for the thing that had taken her, set down on no authority but a son's. He gave his mother's name, and the clerk wrote it out: Mary Jane Searcy. The two plain words of her given name, whole and correct, set on a public paper — which was a thing that had not happened once in the whole eighty-seven years the record had been getting her wrong. The Bible had called her Milbrey or Mity Jane on the day she was born. The first census had gotten her exactly right, at seventeen, the one clean true reading it ever gave her — and then never again: the second had shaved her to twenty-five when she was twenty-seven; the marriage return had made her Miss M. J. Rorie, two initials and a courtesy and no name at all; the counts after had set her down Mary J. and M. J., and a later hand, indexing the last of those old sheets, would misread even the initial and make her H. J. Across the whole of a life the one thing the record never once managed was her own whole given name. And here it was at the last, in a son's plain answer to a clerk in the worst week of his life — Mary Jane, written whole — and her husband's Searcy beside it, F. J. Searcy, dead twelve years that February. He gave the day of her death, the eleventh of October, and the hour, half past four in the morning, and the county, Alcorn.

Even then it did not give her the whole of herself. It gave her Searcy, the name she had taken at thirty-four when she stood up in a house on the Rorie land and said her plain yes; her own name, the Rorie she was born to, it left to the line below, where the paper asked for her father and the son wrote Reuben Rorie, and for her mother, Mary Hubbard — Polly, in the family's keeping, though the paper could stand at Mary. Her maiden name survived on the certificate of her death only as her father's, one line under her own. And where the paper asked where she had come into the world he gave the answer she had given him all his life, which was the answer the Bible her mother kept had entered beside her name in 1832: Tennessee. She had been born on the Tennessee side, she had always said, the year before her people crossed down into Mississippi. He signed the informant's line R. E. Searcy, fifty years old. The record came nearer to her at the end than it had ever been, and stopped one name short. But it gave her Mary Jane, plainly, for the first time in her life — and she was dead when it got there.

And the paper kept, without meaning to, the record of how the family had done all of it themselves. Where it asked for the undertaker it got no undertaker; where it asked what he had furnished it got, in the same plain hand, homemade coffin. No doctor had signed the front of it and no undertaker the back. It had been filled from a son's mouth and filed, five days after they had already put her in the ground, by a country registrar who took the family's word because there was no other word to take. The state made one paper of her at the end — and even that paper, read close, is mostly the record of the people who did not wait for the state: who nursed her with no doctor, and washed and dressed her with no undertaker, and built her box and dug her grave and filled it with their own hands, and only afterward rode to town and told the county what they had already done.

The state did not get the whole of her right. At the line for her age it set down eighty-seven years, ten months, and six days — the son's spoken figure — and the figure was fourteen days short of the count the household kept, for she had been born on the twenty-first of November of 1832 and had died on the eleventh of October of 1920, which is eighty-seven years, ten months, and twenty days, to the day, by the subtraction her own people had always been able to make in their sleep. The certificate said six. The household said twenty. Why the son gave the short figure into the state's book, no one can now reach — a man tired past counting, a count made from grief instead of from the page. The day she died did not move in the fourteen days between the two numbers. The state kept its six. The household kept its twenty. The household was right, and had been right since the night her mother wrote her into a Bible carried up out of North Carolina before any of them were born. The state writes a person down once, at the end. The household had been writing her down, and carrying her, the whole way through.

· · ·

The burying was at Pine Grove in a clear October light.

The family came up the lane to the burying-ground, and a small gathering stood at the grave — the Methodist minister with his Bible open, and a few neighbors from the country round who had heard, some of them the same hands that had helped the day before at the house. The minister read from the twenty-third Psalm and from the eighth chapter of John, and read the committal, and named her over the open ground — Mary Jane Searcy, daughter of Reuben and Mary Hubbard Rorie, wife of F. J. Searcy, mother of William, Robert Edwin, and Frank Searcy, grandmother to their children, in the eighty-eighth year of her life — and closed the book.

The men lowered the box on the ropes — his two brothers and two of the neighbors — and drew the ropes up. Then Robert Edwin stepped to the head of the grave and took up the spade himself from the mound of October earth, and lifted a spadeful of the dark earth and held it a moment over the open ground, and thought, standing there, that his mother had stood with a spade at the head of her own mother's grave, and her father's before that, and that the motion came down a family the way the keeping did. He let the earth go. It fell soft on the box. He passed the spade to his brothers, and the two men who had come off the roads took each a spadeful and said nothing and passed it to Hattie, and Hattie took hers and passed it to her children, and the children took theirs down to the least that could hold the handle, and last of all it came to the boy.

Lester took the spade in his two hands. He lifted a small spadeful the careful way his father had, and held it over the ground, and let it fall. He did not cry. He stepped back to his father's side and looked up.

"Papa," he said. "The cross says the dates."

"It does, for now," Robert Edwin said. "The painted cross stays this year. The stone comes at the close of the year, cut clean, with the dates and At Rest."

"Eighteen thirty-two," the boy said, reading the painted board. "Nineteen twenty." He said it again, low, to fix it. "I won't forget the dates, papa."

"You won't forget," his father said, and put his hand on the boy's shoulder and left it there, and did not say — because there was no way to say it to a boy of six at a graveside, and no need — that those two dates and the paper the state had made were nearly the whole of what the record would ever set down plainly of a woman it had spent eighty-seven years calling by half a name, and that the line had come down to this child at the graveside regardless of the record, and would go on past him.

The minister came to Robert Edwin when the men had begun to close the grave, and put his hand on the son's shoulder. "She kept it faithful to the last, Mr. Searcy," he said. "It comes to your hand now. The Bible at the south wall. The page."

"It comes to my hand," Robert Edwin said.

· · ·

It came to his hand the next afternoon, when the brothers had gone back to their own places and the house was quiet.

He sat down at the writing-table under the south-wall shelf where his mother had set the two Bibles, and reached up and took the Rorie Bible down, and laid it on the table, and opened it to the deaths.

The last death in the column was his uncle Ezekiah — killed July 22nd 1864 in Atlanta, Georgia — in his grandmother Mary Polly's careful round hand, and above it her husband, Reubin Rorie departed this life the August of 1862, in the same hand. That was the last thing she ever wrote on the page. Below Ezekiah the book ran on — births, a marriage, a Blake grandchild, a Houston wedding, in her hand and others' — but the deaths stopped there, at the war. There was no line for Mary Polly herself, who had kept the page and died with her own dying entered nowhere. There was no line for his father. The column of the family's deaths had closed in 1864 and stayed closed for fifty-six years, and every dying since — his grandmother's, his father's, and now his mother's — had gone unwritten in it. He was opening the book to set down the first death entered on that page since the war.

The keeping was supposed to pass from a mother's hand into a daughter's, he thought, each keeper writing the one before her, the name handed on down the column. But there had been no daughter in this line to hand it to — his mother had borne three sons and no daughter — and so it did not come down the way it was supposed to. It came to him, a son, the way his mother's own beginning had come down to her: without the entry. His parents' marriage was nowhere in the front of the book. The morning his whole family began had never been written onto the marriages page; his mother had carried her wedding-day only as a date in her head, and the state had not set it down for years, and no hand had ever set it here. The line had come down out of an unwritten morning, across the better part of a century, into his mother's keeping, and was coming now into his — and the book that was supposed to hold it had a silence at its beginning where the founding should have been, and had held the family anyway, the way the family had held the book.

The keeping was never only the entry. He saw that plainly, with the pen not yet in his hand. The entry was the family's way of saying it out loud on paper. But the thing itself was what his mother had done with her hands and her days for eighty-seven years — the deaths she had witnessed and the births she had watched and the long stretches when there was nothing to write and she had kept the line alive across them anyway, in the cabin and the kept house and her son's front room, in the broth carried up a stair and the word sent down a road and the two Bibles set on a shelf with her own hands. The page was the record of it. It was not the whole of it.

He would write her into it in his own hand, on a day soon, when his hand was steadier than it was that afternoon — the mother's death set down by the son, because there was no daughter to set it down, and no one had set a death in that page since a war that ended before he was born. He closed the Rorie Bible and put it back on the shelf beside Aaron Searcy's working Bible in its old satchel, where his mother had set them both, and where she had named them to him in the last clear words of her life. The keeping had passed to Robert Edwin Searcy, at the writing-table under the south wall, on the afternoon after they buried her.

Out the window the one tree in the fence-corner had gone past its red toward the bare, and the field lay flat and quiet the way it had lain the whole of her life, whatever they had called the county that year. The line she had carried out of an unwritten morning in a Tennessee cabin had come the whole long way down and was in his hands now, and would go from his hands to the boy's, and the boy had the dates by heart already.

She held the line.
