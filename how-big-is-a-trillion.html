A scale exercise in thirteen zeros

How big is a trillion, really?

Your intuition handles millions just fine. It quietly fails somewhere around a billion — and a trillion is a thousand of those. Pick an amount and see what it actually means.

August 18, 2026: the US national debt crossed $40 trillion for the first time — $40,047,425,768,420 and change. It took about five months to add the last trillion. The first one took roughly two centuries.

Nobody sent you this bill, nobody asked for a down payment, and there is no closing date. That is the whole trick of a trillion: it is large enough to feel like it belongs to no one.

Assumptions

A US $100 bill is 0.0043 inches thick. A year is 365.25 days. Interest is simple annual yield divided into seconds — no compounding, no taxes, no judgment. Counting assumes one dollar per second with no sleep, which is roughly how month-end close feels anyway.

Per-person figures divide total debt by a US population of about 343 million. Median household income of $83,730 is the Census figure for 2024. Median home price of $410,700 is the Census/HUD median sales price for Q2 2026. Growth of $6 billion per day is the current federal borrowing rate cited by the House Budget Committee.

One more thing this page does not show: the interest. Federal interest costs ran $931 billion in the first ten months of FY2026 — more than defense, more than every budget line except Social Security and Medicare. The share above is principal only.
