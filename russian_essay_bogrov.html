The Ticket to Kiev

Bogrov walked into the Kiev opera on a police ticket and shot the prime minister. What Solzhenitsyn found, what he invented, and what nobody can prove.

On the night of 1 September 1911 (14 September in the West), in the City Theatre at Kiev, the second interval of Rimsky-Korsakov's Tale of Tsar Saltan had just begun. Nicholas II was in the imperial box with two of his daughters; the town was in festival for the unveiling of a monument to Alexander II, the Tsar-Liberator, fifty years after emancipation. Pyotr Stolypin, the prime minister, stood at the orchestra rail talking with other ministers and courtiers. A young man walked down the aisle, drew a pistol, and fired. One bullet passed through Stolypin's hand and wounded a musician in the pit; the other struck the cross of the Order of St. Vladimir on his chest, glanced, and lodged near the liver. He died four days later. The young man, Dmitry Bogrov, was in that theatre on a ticket handed to him by the Kiev security police — the men whose job it was to keep him out. That is the story Solzhenitsyn tells at length in August 1914, and the question a listener is left with is the one that follows: how much of it is true, and how much is the novelist's?

The short answer, layer by layer: the skeleton — who Bogrov was, the lie he told, the ticket, the shooting, the police failures, the buried inquiry — is documented, and unusually well. The interior — what he thought, how the evening felt, what men said in rooms no stenographer entered — is necessarily invented. And the meaning — why he did it, and whether the police wanted it done — is where the argument still runs, and where a novelist's instincts and a historian's caution part ways.

What the record holds

The man. Bogrov was twenty-four, born in Kiev in 1887 to a wealthy Jewish family, a law graduate, a self-described anarchist. He had also been a police informer since 1907, working for the Kiev Okhrana under the cover name "Alensky," with a second stint under another name in St. Petersburg in 1910. Some of the anarchist circle he reported on were arrested or exiled; by 1908 some of his comrades suspected him. The Russian police of the period ran on informers of exactly this kind, and Bogrov had a three-year record and, by one account, a retainer of around a hundred rubles a month.

The lie. In late August, as the Tsar and his ministers converged on Kiev for the celebrations, Bogrov went to his handler, Lieutenant-Colonel Nikolai Kulyabko, chief of the Kiev security section, with a story. Two revolutionaries, he said — "Nikolai Yakovlevich" and a woman, "Nina Aleksandrovna" — had come from out of town to kill a high official during the celebrations, and were staying at his flat. Accounts differ on whom he named as the target; the Tsar, Stolypin, and the education minister all appear. He later admitted the whole tale was invented.

The ticket. This is the piece that gives the story its terrible neatness. To be of use, Bogrov told the police, he would have to be able to point the terrorists out — and so he needed to be where the officials would be. Kulyabko gave him a pass to the theatre. Alexander Spiridovich, who ran the Tsar's personal security in Kiev and was in the theatre, records in his memoir that his adjutant reported one of the police entry permits found on the assassin. The lie was not merely believed; it was designed so that the security police themselves would place their informer where the ministers were. In an account quoted in Spiridovich's memoir, the inquiry established that Kulyabko ordered Bogrov home only after the second act had begun — when he was already in the theatre.

The end. Bogrov was seized on the spot. A military court tried him within a few days of Stolypin's death, and he was hanged at the Lysaya Gora fortress on 12 September (25 September in the West), eleven days after the shooting. The speed is itself part of the record. Everything he might have said under a longer, slower, more searching interrogation went to the gallows with him.

The inquiry. A Senate inquiry under Maximilian Trusevich examined the security arrangements and, in early 1912, named four senior officers for inaction and abuse of authority: P. G. Kurlov, the deputy interior minister responsible for the Tsar's protection; Spiridovich; Verigin, a vice-director of the Police Department; and Kulyabko. A criminal investigation into charging them followed. On the Russian accounts, Nicholas told the new prime minister, Kokovtsov, in October 1912 that he would stop the case, and he signed the resolution in January 1913. Kurlov, Verigin and Kulyabko lost their posts; Spiridovich was investigated but kept his. By one Russian account only Kulyabko was ever tried, and lightly punished. Spiridovich, in his own memoir, is blunter than any judge: "hypnotised" by the presence of the Emperor, he says, he had no thought but for the Emperor's safety, and he had been, in his words, "professionally irresponsible." The negligence is not a theory. It was found by the state's own investigators and confessed, in part, by one of the men concerned.

Why did he do it?

Bogrov gave several reasons, and they do not all fit together. He said that in mid-August a group of anarchists had come to him, having found out about the police connection, and told him to redeem himself by killing a prominent official; the alternative, he said, was to be exposed, which he called worse than death. He said he had gone to the police in the first place for revolutionary purposes, to learn how they worked. He said, too, that he acted with the welfare of the Jews in mind, and that he had refused to shoot the Tsar because a regicide by a Jew would bring pogroms. That last point is in the interrogation record; it is the seed of everything Solzhenitsyn later built on Bogrov's Jewishness.

Historians take different roads from there. Most — Abraham Ascher, George Tokmakoff, Jonathan Daly, Anna Geifman — see a coerced and unstable man trying to buy his way back: the anarchist ultimatum, the shame of the informer, in Geifman's reading a suicidal wish. One fact complicates the neat version: in 1910, a year before the ultimatum, Bogrov had gone to a Socialist Revolutionary leader, Yegor Lazarev, and asked for the party's blessing to kill Stolypin; the party declined. The wish, in other words, was his own before anyone forced it. Others, among them Victoria Khiterer and Sergei Stepanov, emphasize revenge for recent anti-Jewish violence and persecution; Kiev in 1911 was a grim place for Jews, and Mendel Beilis had been arrested there that July on a ritual-murder charge. The link to Bogrov rests on Lazarev's later account of what Bogrov told him in 1910 and on Bogrov's own statements, neither of which can be independently verified. Paul Avrich called the act a purely personal one, and one encyclopedia entry concludes, not unfairly, that his true motive was never established.

Three stories about the police

Everyone agrees the police failed. The argument is over what kind of failure it was.

Incompetence. The evidence for this is the most direct. Responsibility for the Tsar's visit was split among several men and services — Kurlov, Kulyabko, Spiridovich — and a split like that invites each to assume another is watching; the whole apparatus, moreover, was built to trust its own agents. Nothing was more natural, inside that system, than to believe a source of three years' standing. Richard Pipes, as the reference literature summarizes him, defends the police against the charge that they arranged the killing.

Complicity. The evidence for this is circumstantial but not nothing. A police officer put the assassin in the theatre; the assassin was tried and hanged in eleven days; the inquiry was stopped by the Tsar for reasons that remain unknown; and Russia had already seen what a police agent could do. Yevno Azef, the head of the Socialist Revolutionary Combat Organization, had for years been a paid Okhrana agent, and the killings of Plehve and of Grand Duke Sergei were carried out under his direction; and the scandal of his exposure in 1908–09 left a lasting suspicion of what the police knew and when. One Duma deputy, I. P. Pokrovsky, compared the Kiev affair directly to Azef's. Alexander Kerensky later relayed second-hand talk that a prosecutor had told Stolypin's son-in-law that the deed was done at Kurlov's instigation. Marc Ferro accepts the involvement of the security services, and W. Bruce Lincoln, as the reference works summarize him, points instead to conservative reactionaries in the State Council, whose hatred of Stolypin ran deep after the crisis of March 1911.

The lone act. Bogrov, on this view, needed no one. He was an informer with a pistol and a story, and the police were fools; what looks like a plot is what a sloppy and self-protective bureaucracy looks like afterwards, when it buries its own mistakes.

The honest verdict is narrower than any of these. Negligence is proven. Instigation is not. And a cover-up of negligence is not evidence of a plot: the officers had ample reason to hide incompetence, quite apart from any guilt in the killing, because their careers depended on it. What keeps the second story alive is the buried inquiry, the speed of the hanging, and the fact that key documents were reportedly published in redacted form and that an emigre compilation of documents (Serebrennikov, New York, 1986, 333 pages) appeared long before, but a fuller documentary volume of some seven hundred pages did not appear until 2003. The record we do have points to a state that could not protect its own prime minister. It does not point, without a great deal of help from the imagination, to a state that killed him.

What Solzhenitsyn does with it

Solzhenitsyn was open about his method. The Red Wheel is not a novel in the ordinary sense, and he said so: he loaded it with documents, and whole chapters are made of newspaper extracts, because he wanted to offer proof, not mood, and, in a line quoted by Gary Saul Morson, he had "never taken a vow of fidelity to the novel form." The Stolypin cycle in the expanded August 1914 runs to some fifteen chapters by the count in Willetts's translation (chapter 8 and chapters 60 to 73), and the material added to that edition includes chapters on Bogrov and, according to the published summaries, the suspected involvement of the secret police.

That method sorts the material into three layers, and a reader can tell them apart.

The documented layer is what is set out above: the informer, the invented terrorists, the ticket, the police confusion in the theatre. A reader should trust the outline here. The Bogrov story, as it comes down to us from the investigation and from the officers' own memoirs, is stranger than most inventions, and Solzhenitsyn had no need to improve it.

The imaginative layer is everything a court would not accept: Bogrov's thoughts on the way to the theatre, what Kulyabko was thinking when he signed the pass, the small talk in the room, the pacing and the light. No one recorded these. The only testimony about them comes from men with reasons to shade it — an informer who lied professionally, officers facing prosecution — and much of it was given under pressure of time or self-interest. Any scene built on them is reconstruction, and the more vivid it is, the more of it is the novelist's.

The interpretive layer is where Solzhenitsyn takes sides. By the published summaries, the novel presses the suspicion of police involvement. His later nonfiction Two Hundred Years Together (2001–02) goes further on the Jewish dimension: he writes that he saw no reason to conceal that Stolypin's killer was a Jew, and that Bogrov believed, as his own testimony suggests, that the act would serve the Jews' interest. The book drew heavy criticism — Richard Pipes found him too eager to exonerate imperial Russia, Yohanan Petrovsky-Shtern concluded that it belongs in the canon of Russian-language antisemitic writing, others accused him of selective quotation — but that criticism is aimed mainly at the later work, not at the novel. Solzhenitsyn's answer, as Morson reports it, was that critics who read a charge against the Jews into Bogrov's being Jewish are demanding the censorship of history.

Both sides have a point, and the point is narrow. Bogrov's Jewishness is a fact, and so is his own claim to have acted with Jewish interests in mind; a novelist who hid either would be falsifying. But the statement that a man said he acted for a cause is not the statement that the cause explains the act, and most historians explain Bogrov by the ultimatum, the shame, and the state of his nerves. The fair charge against the Solzhenitsyn treatment is one of emphasis: it takes a documented claim and gives it a weight the professional literature does not.

This is the general pattern of Solzhenitsyn as a historian, and it is worth stating plainly: the frame is sound, the scene is shaped. The outline of the Bogrov chapters can be trusted. The interior is a novelist's. And where the chapters press the suspicion of the police, they present a legitimate question as somewhat more settled than it is.

Three questions to ask of any dramatized scene:

Who could have recorded this? If only Bogrov or Kulyabko was in the room, the scene is reconstruction.

Who benefits from this version? An informer, a colonel facing prosecution, and a general writing his memoirs all had a story to protect.

Does it match the documents? The ticket, the alias, the hanging date and the inquiry's finding can be checked; the mood of the evening cannot.

So, was it true?

Mostly, and in the parts that matter. The Bogrov story as told is not a legend. It is a case the state's own investigators substantiated on its main points and then buried. The freedoms Solzhenitsyn takes are those of any dramatist working from testimony: he fills the silence with a mind, a voice, and an evening. And the place where the novel goes past the evidence is the place where the evidence itself gives out, which is the question of what the police wanted. On that, Solzhenitsyn asks a legitimate question, and where the chapters lean on it they leave it looking a little more answered than it is. The next reader will have to keep both facts in view: that a young man stood in a theatre that night on a police ticket, and that nobody has ever proved anyone put him there on purpose.

A note on certainty: Bogrov's career as an informer, the false story to Kulyabko, the ticket, the shooting and Stolypin's death, the hanging, and the charges against Kurlov, Spiridovich, Verigin and Kulyabko are firmly documented. Dates are given Old Style with the Western date beside them, and some secondary sources muddle the two; the ones used here were checked against more than one account. What happened to the officers afterwards is less clear: English and Russian accounts differ on when the Tsar halted the case and on Kulyabko's sentence, and this essay follows the Russian sequence (inquiry in 1912, case stopped in January 1913) while leaving the sentence open. The account of Bogrov's motives rests on his own interrogation, which cannot be independently verified, and the historians named here disagree with one another. The positions attributed to Pipes, Ferro and Lincoln come from published summaries of their work, not from a fresh reading of the books, and should be checked before being quoted. The account of Solzhenitsyn's chapters is drawn from the reading and from published descriptions of the novel; it has not been checked against the text passage by passage, and readers who want to test a specific scene should compare it with the documents named above.
