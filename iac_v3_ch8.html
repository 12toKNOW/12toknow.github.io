The top of her life

By the summer of 1880 she was a woman of forty-seven with a full house and a standing place, and if the record had ever been going to let her rest easy on one of its countings, this was the one.

The fourth man with a book came up the road that June — J. M. Bynum, walking Beat 3 of Alcorn County, the fourth census taker of her life and the second to find her a settled wife on her own ground. She was used to them by now. She had watched them come every ten years since she was a girl at the loom, the man and the ledger and the ruled columns, and she had learned across a lifetime exactly what they could do to a woman and exactly what they could not, and she had no particular dread of this one. There was nothing in her line, this time, to dread. She was forty-seven and she was a farmer's wife of forty-seven, and there was no shame in that number and nothing about it that the house would need to shave or soften; the years, having spent her whole youth making her a woman the record had to lie about to be kind, had at last carried her all the way over to the other side, to the plain broad country of the middle-aged wife, where the truth costs nothing again because nobody is measuring it.

So the marshal wrote her down at forty-seven, and forty-seven she was. He found the household at the fullest and best it would ever be. Freeman was fifty, a farmer on his own place — the landless day-labourer of twenty years back grown into a settled man the county counted among its property-holders. The three sons were around them, growing: the eldest coming up on a man's height, the middle boy Robert Edwin ten years old and long past the four-months' cradle the third book had caught him in, the youngest still a boy. It was a good house, a full house, a rising house on the family's dirt. Mary Jane stood in the middle of it at forty-seven, a matron of standing where she had once been the aging daughter nobody could marry, and if she had been asked, that June, to weigh her life, she would have had to say — quietly, not being a woman who said such things aloud — that it had come out well. Late, and hard, and by roads nobody would have chosen for her. But well. She had a husband and a place and three living sons, and the record, for the second counting running, had let her be exactly her age.

The one thing it still would not do was give her her name. The marshal wrote her, as the clerks always wrote her, by her initials — M. J. — the way the marriage return had written her thirteen years before, the way the record had been writing her the whole of her married life: two letters and the surname, never Mary Jane, never the whole of her. She was used to that too. It had stopped being a wound years ago and become a fact of her, like the ache in the arm from the babies, like the sound of the mill under her girlhood — the record would give her initials and not a name, and that was simply how the record held her, and she expected nothing else.

She could not have known, and it would not have consoled her if she had, that even the initials were not safe. A hundred years on, when a stranger's hand came to index that page and make the old book findable, it would look at the M. J. the marshal had written and read it as H. J. — mistake the very first letter of her, so that the woman who had never once been given her whole name would end by being not even reliably given her right initials. She was M. J. on the page and would be H. J. in the finding, and Mary Jane nowhere in either. But that was a hundred years off and no concern of a June morning in 1880, when the counting of her own household was the easiest thing the record had ever done to her, and she had every reason to think the book had, for once, simply passed her by without doing any harm.

It had not passed her by without doing any harm. It had only done the harm six pages away, in another household, under another woman's roof, to somebody Mary Jane loved more than she loved her own good name.

The word

Her mother was in the book too, that June, and the book had written one word beside her, and the word was mother.

Mary Jane knew, in her life, what the census only recorded — that Mary Polly had given up her own house. It had happened somewhere in the years nobody had watched closely, the way such things happen to the very old: not a decision so much as a slow yielding, the body asking a little more each season than the ground would give back, until the day came when the widow who had taken the Rorie farm the summer Reuben died and carried it through the whole of the war and held it after, alone, for years — the woman who was the standard of that family, the fixed point the whole neighborhood had measured itself against for a generation — could no longer keep a place of her own, and let it go, and went to live in a daughter's house. Mary Jane had known it as it happened. It was the kind of thing a family knows and does not make a ceremony of: the mother getting old, the mother needing keeping, the mother going to one of the girls. It was ordinary. It was what happened to the very old. It had happened to Grandmother Sarah in Mary Jane's own girlhood, and Mary Jane had stood in that house at twenty-four and washed the old body afterward and learned the office over it. Now it was her mother's turn to be the old woman taken in, and Mary Jane, who was forty-seven and had a full house of her own, felt it the way you feel your mother growing old — as a grief with no event in it, a thing you cannot fight because there is nothing to fight, only the slow turning of the generations bringing your mother down the same road her mother went down.

What Mary Jane did not see, because there was no reason on earth for her to see it, was the page.

The same book that had counted her own thriving household — the full house, the risen husband, the three sons, her own right age — had counted her mother too, six pages further on, most of a beat away, in the household of a man named Hamlin whose wife was very likely one of Mary Polly's own daughters; and in the column that asks what a person is to the head of the house, the marshal had written, for Mary Polly Hubbard Rorie, seventy-five years old, born in Tennessee before there was a country to be born a citizen of, the single plainest and heaviest word the census had for her. Not head. Not keeping house. Mother. The woman who had built and buried and held, who had kept the family's Bible in her own hand for forty years and set the family's births and marriages and deaths down in it and been the still point the whole line turned on — reduced, on the page, to what she was to somebody else's household. Somebody's mother. A dependent in a daughter's house, given the warm place out of the draft, counted at the end under a younger woman's roof, exactly as her own mother had been counted before her.

It is a small word to carry so much. Mother. In the ordinary run of the census it means nothing heavier than a fact of the household — this old woman belongs to these people, is kept by them, is theirs. But set against everything Mary Polly had been, it is the whole surrender of a life in seven letters. She had come to the end of what her body could keep, and she had done the only thing there was to do, which was to give the ground up and be taken in; and the record, which had spent Mary Jane's whole life failing to say who Mary Jane was, said with perfect and terrible economy exactly who Mary Polly had become. Mother. Not the head of anything. Not the keeper of anything. The old woman in the corner of a child's house.

And six pages back in the same book was her daughter, forty-seven and thriving, at the very top of her life.

One fate, twice

Here is the thing the 1880 book did, laying those two households down in its pages a few sheets apart, that neither the marshal nor Mary Jane nor anyone alive that June could see: it was not counting two women at all. It was counting one fate, twice.

Because Mary Jane, at forty-seven, in the full flush of the best years she would ever have, was looking straight at her own ending and did not know it. Everything the record had just written of her mother it would one day write of her. Thirty years on — in another decade, in the same county, on the same family dirt — a census taker would come up a farm road and find Mary Jane herself grown old, her own house given up, her own body past the keeping of a place, taken into the household of one of her sons; and in the column that asks what a person is to the head of the house he would write, beside her name, the same word the marshal wrote beside her mother's this June. Mother. The woman who was watching her mother taken in was watching, without the least idea of it, a rehearsal of her own last act — the shape the house had made of its women one after another as far back as anyone could count, the grandmother taken in and then the mother taken in and then, in her turn, when the years had done with her what they do, the daughter. She had helped make her grandmother ready for the ground at twenty-four and thought herself one of the young hands. She was one of the young hands still, at forty-seven, watching her mother go the way of the grandmother. But the road only ran one direction, and she was on it, and the same word was waiting at the end of it for her that had already fallen on the two women ahead of her in the line.

She could not see it. That is the mercy and the cruelty of a life lived forward: she stood at the top of hers, with her house full and her sons growing and her husband a man of standing, and she grieved her mother's coming-down the way any daughter grieves it, as her mother's, as a thing happening to somebody else — and she could not turn the six pages of the book that would have shown her it was a thing happening to herself, thirty years slow. Nobody at the top of a full house can. The end of a life is always six pages away, in a part of the book you have no reason to turn to, written in a stranger's column in a word you would not think to look for.

The family had come apart across the beat by then, the way a family does when it has stayed long on one ground — not scattered, never scattered, but spread: her brother William Hubbard one beat off on the one side, her brother Fred one beat off on the other, her mother taken into a daughter's house at the far end of Beat 3, Mary Jane and her own household at the near end of it, the whole intermarried connection dispersed across the same few square miles of old Tishomingo dirt that nobody had ever left. The ground had held. Everyone was still on it, or near it. What had moved was only the roofs over them — which household held whom, which of the girls had the mother now, which name the county wrote over the same fields it had written another name over ten years before. The dirt was the dirt. The people kept to it, and the map and the households and the census words rearranged themselves around the people the way weather rearranges itself around a hill, and the hill stays.

And somewhere in the moving-around of it, quiet, unremarked, the keeping was passing down. The Bible her mother had kept for forty years and stopped keeping after Ezekiah — the book with the births and the marriages and the deaths in Mary Polly's careful hand, and the blank line where Mary Jane's wedding was never written — was in the world still, in the hands of the old woman who could no longer keep a house, and it would come down, in time, the way the ground came down and the office of laying-out came down, from the mother's hands toward the daughter's, along the same line the surrender ran. Mary Polly had a decade or so left to her, no more; she would die somewhere around the turn of the nineties, an old woman in a child's house, and be laid out by younger hands the way she had once helped lay out her own mother. But that was still ahead, that June. That June she was only the word in the column, six pages on from her thriving daughter — mother, seventy-five, taken in — while Mary Jane stood at the top of her one good stretch of years and could not see, in the same book that held them both, that she was reading her own future in her mother's line.

She was forty-seven. Her house was full. The record had been kind to her, that once. And it had told her, in the same breath and on the same pages, exactly how it would end — if only she had known to turn to where it said so, which she did not, and could not, and was not meant to.
