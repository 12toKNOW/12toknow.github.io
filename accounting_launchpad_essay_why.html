An Essay · Why Accounting

I Run a Heavy-Equipment Company. An Accounting Degree Is How I Got Here.

The flagship essay in the series — the whole thesis, planted: accounting isn't a destination, it's a launchpad.

I'm the chief operating officer of a heavy-equipment dealership. The questions on my desk are about rental-fleet utilization, branch margins, and capital-allocation calls with a lot of zeros on them. None of that is what people picture when they hear the word accounting. And yet an accounting degree is the entire reason I can answer any of it.

My father was a CPA. It provided well for our family and he seemed to like the work, so I followed him into it — a B.S. and a Master of Accounting, both at UAB, working at a CPA firm the whole way through. When I finally passed the CPA exam, I walked into the managing partner's office and asked what I'd earned for it. He looked at me and said, "You get to keep your job." That was my first professional lesson, and it wasn't really about accounting — it was never be outworked. The second lesson took me two more decades to see clearly: the degree I'd just earned was not a destination at all. It was a launchpad.

We have a habit of teaching accounting as a destination — a place you arrive at. Get the degree, sit for the CPA, join a firm, climb toward partner. It's an honorable path and plenty of good people are on it. But I want to make the opposite case across this series: accounting is the best launchpad in business, and almost no one describes it that way to the eighteen-year-old deciding whether to major in it.

The zigzag is the argument

Look at where the launch actually took me, because the zigzag is the whole argument. I left the firm to become CFO of a temporary-staffing agency — a grand title for a man who was the entire finance department, no controller under him, not even an assistant. Then controller at a manufacturer: a smaller title at a bigger company that somehow felt like a promotion. Somewhere in there I went back for a master's in accounting I didn't strictly need — I already held the license, this was before the 150-hour rule — because I could see graduate degrees coming for the profession and I did not intend to be the one without one. Then the move everyone called illogical: I left a good controller's job, took our household income to near zero, and went and got a PhD — a double major in accounting and logistics that people considered a strange pairing. Years later, running operations for an equipment company, that strange pairing turned out to be precisely the training the job required. I taught for thirteen years and became Director of a school of accountancy — the supposed destination — and then I left that chair to become an operator, stepping into the seat my own father had once held at a company I'd worked for decades earlier. Full circle, and not one step of it a straight line.

Here's the thing buried in that zigzag: at every single turn, the accounting degree didn't box me in. It handed me a key.

What it actually teaches

So let me be specific about what it actually teaches — the part that does the launching.

It's the language the whole enterprise is written in. Learn to read a set of financial statements and you can read any business on earth: the choices it has made, the constraints it's under, and the places it's quietly lying to itself. That isn't a bookkeeping skill. It's literacy in the exact thing every operator, founder, and executive is doing all day. Most people run companies half-fluent in that language. You'd be fluent.

It trains judgment under uncertainty. Accounting looks like a world of rules, and beginners think that's the job. It isn't. The real work is the estimate, the materiality call, the honest "it depends" — years of learning to make a defensible decision when the answer isn't clean. That muscle carries into every hard seat in an organization.

It's a trust credential. The auditor's discipline is simple and rare: don't assert what you can't tie to evidence. Carry that habit into any room and people start letting you near the decisions that matter, because your numbers — and eventually your judgment — can be trusted. Trust is the currency that actually moves you.

And it's portable. Every organization that exists has to account for itself. The skill doesn't expire, doesn't stay behind when you switch industries, and doesn't care whether you're in Manhattan or, like me, in Birmingham.

The honest part

Now the honest part, because this series is not going to be a highlight reel.

The degree is a promise, not proof. I have four professional certifications and better than twenty published papers, and not one of them taught me how to meet a payroll or make a capital call with real people attached to the outcome. Credentials are just the world telling you what you were taught. You don't have knowledge until you have experience. Accounting didn't make me an operator. It gave me the door. The operating itself — including the mistakes I'd rather not itemize — is what turned a degree into knowledge. The launchpad is real, but it only works if you actually launch. Standing on it is not the same as leaving the ground.

That's the thesis I'll build this series on: accounting as a launchpad to operating leadership, told for once by someone who used it that way instead of describing the view from the top of the straight line.

But my story is exactly one data point. n = 1. I'd be violating my own first rule if I let it stand in for the whole picture — and the whole picture is the only thing worth handing the next generation.

So I'm opening this up.

If accounting launched you somewhere you never planned to go — into operations, into founding something, into a boardroom or a classroom or a life you genuinely didn't see coming — I want to hear it, and I want to feature it. Not the study tips. The trajectory. Where did the degree actually take you?

One question, and it's the whole invitation: What did accounting launch you into that you never saw coming?

Answer in the comments or send it to me directly. I'll collect and credit the best of them as the series goes. The future professionals we're all a little worried about will learn more from a hundred real trajectories than from any one person's advice. Mine included.

— DeWayne
