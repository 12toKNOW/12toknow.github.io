The Later Wars

A war the family remembers but cannot yet prove, and a war fixed to the day in a Bible and an estate ledger — told strictly by what each record will bear.

Status of this story: a war is exactly where a family history is most tempted to round up, so this page marks the tier on the claims themselves.

The War of 1812

A rumor of a life

The family's memory of the War of 1812 rests on one man, and it rests lightly. Aaron Searcy — the father of Freeman Jones Searcy, and the ancestor whose identity a Revolutionary pension file finally settled (see The Three Aarons) — was, the tradition holds, a soldier "in a war fought before Mississippi was a place." That is the whole of what the family carries: not a battle, not a year, a rumor of a life. What can actually be documented sits on either side of the soldiering, not on it.

DocumentedAaron Searcy was born 7 May 1790, a son of Captain William Hargrove Searcy — set down in the father's own hand in the family register preserved in a Revolutionary War pension file.
TraditionThat Aaron served as a soldier in the War of 1812. The family carries it only as neighborhood memory — "they said" — with neither the war's action nor its year attached.
ProbableA War of 1812 service index does list an Aaron Searcy — a Private in Captain Shaw's Company, 5th Regiment, North Carolina Militia. Our Aaron was a young man in North Carolina at exactly that time, so the record is a plausible match for him.
OpenWhether that militia private is our Aaron is not proven. Aaron Searcy was not a unique name in that generation — the whole difficulty of The Three Aarons — and no pension or muster record has yet tied the militia man to the man born in 1790. A National Archives War-of-1812 service or pension tie would settle it.

Around that unproven middle, the documented Aaron is solid enough: he married Martha "Patsy" Jones in Bibb County, Alabama, in 1819 Documented; he was commissioned a Justice of the Peace in Bibb County, 1823–1825 Documented; and he had come west to Tishomingo County, Mississippi, by 1850, where he dies within the decade. The soldier is the one part of him the record has not yet caught — and this page would rather leave the gap open than fill it for the sound of a fuller sentence.

The Civil War

The brothers' war

Two lines meet in this family in northeast Mississippi, and it is the other line — the Rories, into whom Freeman married in 1867 — that the Civil War is documented through. Reuben Rorie had three sons, and the record is unusually clear about what the war did to them.

DocumentedAll three Rorie sons — Ezekiah B., William Hubbard, and James Frederick Winters ("Fred") — served in the Confederate army (military and estate papers; the family record).
DocumentedEzekiah B. Rorie enlisted at Kossuth in March 1862, about thirty-one, a married man holding roughly $2,000 in land. He was killed near Atlanta on 22 July 1864 — the day of the Battle of Atlanta — recorded in the Rorie family Bible: "killed July 22nd 1864 in Atlanta, Georgia."
OpenEzekiah's regiment is not pinned, and the manner of his death is not recorded. The family history refuses, in its own words, to invent "the particular field, the particular hour, the manner of it… for the comfort of inventing them."

What the war left behind of Ezekiah is an estate, opened in 1865 for his two minor children, and the estate holds the war in a single detail no one could have invented:

DocumentedEzekiah's 1865 estate inventory lists gold, silver, and old-issue paper — and, among the assets, a claim on his brother Fred ("to be settled in cotton money") and an account with his brother William ("to be paid in Confederate money"). Both surviving brothers owed the dead one, in currencies that died with the country.
There is the whole war in one line of an estate ledger: a dead man owed money by his living brothers, in money that was already worthless.
DocumentedWilliam Hubbard Rorie survived and came home. He named two of his sons for Joseph E. Johnston and Robert E. Lee — the plain evidence of his Confederate service and allegiance.
OpenWilliam's regiment is unconfirmed. A particular Mississippi regiment has been suggested but not proven, and the account deliberately does not assert it.
DocumentedFred (J. F. W.) Rorie also survived, settled two beats over, raised a large family, and named a son E. B. for the brother killed at Atlanta.

The war was close enough to be heard from the Rorie ridge. Shiloh was fought about thirteen miles north on 6–7 April 1862, and Corinth — the rail-crossing town that had risen out of the pinewoods only a few years before — was besieged that spring and fought over again that October, becoming a hospital and a charnel house within sight of the family's country Documented context. Reuben Rorie himself, old and failing, died in August 1862 — during the war, though not in it Documented.

The soldier the record cannot place

And then there is Freeman. The man the whole Searcy line runs through was, in 1860, thirty years old, landless, a hired day-laborer boarding in his brother's house. Whether he took any part in the war that killed one of his future brothers-in-law is the single largest open question of this generation.

OpenWhether Freeman Jones Searcy served in the Civil War — for either side, or not at all — is not established. The Confederate pension records that could settle it have not been retrieved, and the family history asserts neither service nor its absence.

That blank is deliberate, and it is the point of a page like this. It would be easy to hand Freeman a war — he was the right age, in the right place, and his in-laws wore the grey. But "the right age and place" is not a record, and the honest state of the account is that Freeman married Mary Jane Rorie in 1867, two years after the war, and what the war was to him personally is a thing the paper has not yet said.

Why the tiers are on the page

Read together, the two wars are a small lesson in reading a family's records honestly. One death is fixed to a single day because someone wrote it in a Bible. Two homecomings are proven not by muster rolls but by the names grieving men gave their sons. A soldiering tradition has a matching name in an index but no proof it is the right man. And a central ancestor's entire war is, for now, a blank the record has not filled. Marking each of those differently — documented, probable, tradition, open — is not hedging. It is the difference between a family history and a family legend, kept visible right on the sentence.
