In the summer of 1785, when this chapter opens, the DeGraffenreid line of Virginia and the Johnson line of North Carolina sat at two seats two days’ ride apart, joined by a marriage of six years’ standing, by three living daughters with more coming, and by a small family Bible, eight inches by five and bound in dark calf, whose fly-leaf had been carrying the family’s names since 1714.

The DeGraffenreid seat was at Lunenburg County, Virginia, on the south side of the James River drainage where Christopher VI’s son Tscharner had built a house in the early 1750s and had kept it since. Tscharner was sixty-two that summer, a Justice of the Peace of long standing, the keeper of the family’s documentary record since his father’s death in October 1742 had passed him the Bible at nineteen. He had married Mary Baker, the daughter of Colonel Henry Baker of Chowan, North Carolina, on the fifth of July of that same year 1742; she had died in the mid-1750s, before the granddaughter who would carry her name was born, and Tscharner had married three times in the years after. The keeping of the Bible had gone on through all of it. He lived at Lunenburg with the Bible on the parlor desk and with his unmarried son Vincent — twenty-two that summer, a Continental Line veteran of seven years’ service who had walked back from Yorktown to Lunenburg in the spring of 1782. The Bible’s keeping was Tscharner’s, and the seat at Lunenburg was the family’s documentary center.

The Johnson seat was at Rockingham County, North Carolina, on the cleared eighty acres that Gideon Johnson Jr. was holding under his mother-in-law Sarah Vass’s deed of attorney of 19 December 1783. The cleared field belonged formally to Sarah Vass, by the Caswell grant of 13 November 1779 of four hundred acres in what had then been Guilford County and was now Rockingham — the county itself had been created in 1785, partitioned from Guilford, with its new seat at Wentworth being established that year on the records the chapter’s heroine would consult when she had cause. Sarah Vass administered her dead husband’s estate from her own seat, also in Rockingham County, within a morning’s ride of her daughter’s house; she had been administering the estate since September 1779, when the Lunenburg Order Book had named her administratrix of Baker DeGraffenreid’s effects. Her husband Baker had ridden out at the outset of the Revolutionary War, in late 1775, and had not come back; the date and place of his death were not in the documentary record then and would not be at any point across the chapter’s thirteen years.

Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson, the daughter of Sarah Vass and the dead Baker, was twenty years old in July 1785. She had married Gideon Johnson Jr. — Private of the 2nd North Carolina Continental Line under Captain Armstrong and Colonel Martin, fifteen months’ service in 1776 and 1777 under pension file S4456 — on Thursday 18 November 1779 at her mother’s borrowed house on the south side of the Dan River, in what was then Guilford County, with her grandfather Tscharner — a Justice of the Peace of long standing — riding south to make the marriage and set it down in the family Bible, which would stand as its only record. She had borne three living daughters in the six years since: Sarah, born 9 October 1780; Mildred Baker, born 11 November 1782; Nancy, born 14 July 1784. A fourth daughter, Ursula, would come in December 1787; a fifth, Polly Baker, in April 1790; five more children would follow across the seventeen years after, ten in total to live to adulthood. The household was hers to run; the kitchen was Old Hetty’s at the hearth from five in the morning; the cleared field was Gideon’s by Sarah’s deed of attorney; the family’s record was Tscharner’s at Lunenburg, two days’ ride north.

The chapter spans the thirteen years from the summer of 1785 to the second week of February 1794. Across those years the household would receive — by post-rider on the route through Hairston’s Cut to Halifax and on to Lunenburg, by traveling man at the kitchen door, by newspaper passed up from the courthouse at Wentworth, by letter from the grandfather at Lunenburg in his particular formal-informal English with French phrases at the sentence-turns — the fragments of a country still in the process of assembling itself. None of the country’s events would happen to the household. All of them would arrive there as fragments, and the household — Mary Baker at the parlor desk with the ledger and the letters; Gideon at the cleared field; Old Hetty at the kitchen; Young George at the lot; three daughters and then four and then five at their working — would price each fragment and proceed.

The Bible at Lunenburg was the family’s documentary center at the opening of this chapter. It would not be at the close of it. In the second week of February 1794 Tscharner would die — at seventy-one, in his bedchamber at Lunenburg, with the cause and the exact day unrecorded in the surviving sources — and the Bible would come south by wagon to the daughter of his dead son’s wife. Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson would become, in the small hours of a night in her own parlor at Rockingham, the first woman in the line to hold the Christopher VI Bible. She would write the formal French death entry in her grandfather’s manner, in her own hand. She would dip the quill a second time and write the two words underneath, in English — we continue — in the same small clear hand she had been signing letters in for fifteen years. She would close the Bible. She would blow out the candle.

That is the chapter’s arc. The thirteen years between are the keeping that earns the two words.

The First Birthday

She woke at the small sound the one-year-old made before she was fully awake.

The sound was not crying. The sound was the soft half-question of a child who has come up out of sleep into the day before her mother has, and who has not yet decided whether to make the day begin. Mary Baker lay on her side with her face to the cradle and listened. The cradle was at her own side of the bed, between the bed and the east wall, where she had moved it three nights ago when the night-heat had become too much for the close room at the back. The window above the cradle was open four inches. The air at the window was cooler than the air in the room. It smelled of the cleared field and the corn at the third week of June’s laying-by and the standing oak at the head of the lane.

The one-year-old made the sound again.

Mary Baker put her hand over the side of the cradle and onto the back inside it. The back was warm. The breathing under her palm was even. The one-year-old turned her face toward her mother’s hand and made the sound the third time and then did not make it again.

Mary Baker held her hand on the back until the breathing went long.

She got out of the bed on the side away from the cradle. Gideon was not in the bed. He had ridden out at four to be at the Caswell grant by daylight, and she had heard him go without lifting her head from the pillow, the way she had heard him go a hundred mornings in five years. He would be at the south rail of the cleared eighty by sun-up. He would not be back to the house before the supper.

She went to the wash-stand in her shift and washed her face and her hands at the basin. The water in the basin was the water she had drawn at the well at the close of yesterday’s work; it had gone the temperature of the room overnight, which on a July night at this latitude was barely cooler than the skin. She dried her face on the linen towel on the peg beside the basin. She pinned her hair up at the nape with the four pins she kept in the dish at the corner of the wash-stand. She did not look at the mirror above the wash-stand.

She did not need to look at the mirror.

She put on the day-gown over the shift and laced it one-handed at the side as she crossed to the door. The lacing had been a thing she had needed both hands for at sixteen and a thing she could do one-handed by twenty. The other hand was holding the cradle’s side because the one-year-old had begun to come awake in earnest now and was reaching with one fist for the edge of the cradle.

Mary Baker lifted her up.

She carried her down through the hall and past the door of the room where the two older girls were still asleep — Sarah on the inside of the trundle, Mildred Baker on the outside, their two heads at the bolster the way they had slept since Mildred Baker had been old enough to leave the cradle two years and four months ago — and down the back stair to the kitchen.

The kitchen was a little less dark than the upstairs. The east window over the dry-sink was paler than the room behind it. Old Hetty was at the hearth banking the night coals open. Old Hetty had been at the hearth since five.

Old Hetty said: Good morning to you, ma’am.

Mary Baker said: Good morning, Hetty.

Old Hetty said: She is up early.

Mary Baker said: She came up at the small sound and would not go down again.

Old Hetty said: It is the day.

Mary Baker said: It is the day.

She sat down at the corner of the kitchen settle with the one-year-old on her lap and gave her the breast under the open lacing of the day-gown. The one-year-old fed in the slow morning way she fed at this hour, half-drowsing back into sleep against her mother’s collarbone, her small fingers opening and closing on the edge of the gown. She had been weaning across the spring; the morning feed was the one feed Mary Baker had not taken away from her yet, because the morning feed was the one she had no reason to take away. The morning feed was its own thing.

The one-year-old fed.

Mary Baker counted, without choosing to, what was in the house this morning. The two older girls in the trundle upstairs. The one-year-old at her breast. Gideon at the Caswell grant. Old Hetty at the hearth. Young George out at the lot already with the milk-cow. The black-and-white dog asleep under the kitchen settle at her feet — she could feel his warmth against the side of her shoe. The kettle on its hook over the banked coals beginning to make the early sound a kettle makes ten minutes before it will sing. The flour-barrel under the long counter, three-quarters full from Tuesday’s milling. The bacon-flitch on the hook in the chimney corner, cut down to a third. The cold ham at the meat-safe in the lean-to, half a pound left from Sunday. The eggs Old Hetty had brought in last evening, eleven of them, in the willow basket by the dry-sink. The corn-meal in the painted tin on the high shelf, almost out — to be milled again on Monday.

She did not count any of this in words.

She knew it the way she knew the breathing of the child against her collarbone — by the body, without sentences.

(She had, at fifteen, counted the household in sentences. She had written lists for her mother on slips of paper at the back of her mother’s ledger — flour, salt, three pewter plates, the lavender, the second pair of stockings — and she had said the lists over to herself walking from the lean-to to the parlor and back so that she would not forget any of them. She did not write the lists anymore. She had stopped writing them somewhere in 1782 or 1783 without noticing she had stopped. She had not, until this morning, noticed that she had stopped noticing.

She thought, this morning, about the child at her collarbone.)

The one-year-old finished her feed and pulled her face back from the breast and looked at her mother and made the small grave look she made when she was full. The look was not a smile. The look was the look of a person who has had what she needed and is now considering the day ahead. The look was new in the last two weeks. Mary Baker had not seen it before the close of June.

Mary Baker said into her ear: Good morning, Nancy.

She said it in the voice she used for the one-year-old, which was not the voice she used for the two older girls and not the voice she used for Gideon and not the voice she used for her mother. The voice was its own voice. The voice belonged to the one child it spoke to.

Nancy put her hand on her mother’s mouth.

Mary Baker held the hand there for the count of one breath and then took it down.

She laced the gown at the side and stood up. She carried Nancy to the long counter under the east window and set her on her hip and lifted down the wooden bowl her own mother had carved her name into at the bottom of when she had been a child of three. Sarah Vass had given the bowl to Mary Baker the spring after Sarah’s birth. The bowl had passed from Sarah to Mildred Baker at fourteen months and from Mildred Baker to Nancy at twelve months four weeks ago. The bowl had Mary Baker’s own childhood name in her mother’s hand cut into the underside — Polly — and below it the date, 1767. The bowl was twenty-one years carved.

She poured a quarter-cup of the cold porridge from yesterday’s pot into the bowl and warmed it with two spoonfuls of new milk from the morning jug on the dry-sink and put a dab of butter in the middle. She carried Nancy and the bowl to the kitchen table and sat with the child on her lap and fed her the porridge with the horn spoon.

The first light came up at the east window behind them.

Old Hetty took the kettle off the hook and poured the boiling water through the tea-leaves in the brown pot.

Mary Baker said: Hetty, Mr. Johnson said he would take a cold dinner at the rail and not come back to the house until the supper. There will be us four to the dinner.

Old Hetty said: I will do a hominy and the cold ham and a corn-bread.

Mary Baker said: That will do.

Old Hetty said: And the little one, will she eat the hominy.

Mary Baker said: She will eat what we put in front of her by now. She is past the age for being asked.

Old Hetty said: It is the day for her.

Mary Baker said: It is.

She did not say any more about the day. Old Hetty did not say any more either. The saying of it twice between the two of them was as far as the saying went. The kitchen-bound knowing of Nancy’s first year was not the kind of knowing that wanted sentences.

(Mary Baker’s own first birthday had been the third of September 1765, in the front room at Lunenburg, with her mother Sarah Vass at twenty and her father Baker DeGraffenreid at twenty-one and her grandfather Tscharner at forty-two riding down from the upper place. Sarah had told her the story when she had been six. Sarah had told her she had carried her out to the well-stoop in her mother’s arms and they had stood for a long minute and looked at the September sky and Sarah had said you have lived a year and Mary Baker had answered with the babble of a one-year-old. The story had been Sarah’s story, not hers. She did not remember the September sky. She remembered her mother telling her about the September sky.

She would not tell Nancy this story for some years yet. Sarah had waited until Mary Baker had been six.)

Sarah came down the back stair at half past five with Mildred Baker behind her on the second tread.

Sarah said: I heard Nancy.

Mary Baker said: She is at the table with me.

Sarah came around the corner of the door and stopped at the door and looked at her mother and her little sister at the table. Mildred Baker came around the corner of the door behind her and went straight to the kitchen settle and climbed up onto it without saying anything and put her thumb in her mouth. Mildred Baker did the thumb when she had just come out of sleep. She would put it down by the time the porridge was in front of her.

Sarah said: Is Nancy eating.

Mary Baker said: She is eating the porridge from yesterday.

Sarah said: I will eat the porridge from yesterday too.

Mary Baker said: You will have new porridge. Hetty is putting it on.

Sarah said: Then I will have new.

She came across the kitchen and stood at her mother’s elbow and looked at Nancy in the lap and at the bowl with Polly and 1767 cut into the underside that she could not see from where she was standing but that she knew was there. She put her hand on Nancy’s foot. Nancy did not take her face out of the bowl. Sarah held the foot for a moment and let go and went to the settle and climbed up beside her sister.

The two older girls sat on the settle in their nightshifts and waited.

The first light from the east window was on Mildred Baker’s hair where she had not braided it for the night and it had come loose and was the colour her father’s hair had been at three. The first light at Sarah’s hair was on the small brown plait that had stayed in across the night. Sarah was four years and nine months old. Sarah was good at her hair.

Mary Baker fed Nancy the last spoonful of porridge.

She wiped Nancy’s mouth with the corner of her apron.

She set the bowl on the table.

She said to the two on the settle: Up to the room and dress. The blue gown for Sarah today. The yellow for Mildred Baker. Stockings the cotton, both of you, it will be hot. I will come up at the count of a minute and do the hair.

Sarah said: Yes ma’am.

Mildred Baker took her thumb out of her mouth and said: Yes ma’am.

The two went out the kitchen door and up the back stair. Their feet on the treads were the small uneven feet of a four-year-old and a two-year-old going up the stair the way they went up it every morning. Mary Baker listened to the sound until it stopped at the upstairs hall.

She stood up at the table with Nancy on her hip.

She carried the bowl to the dry-sink and rinsed it under the dipper of cold water Old Hetty kept at the side for the morning rinses and dried it on the towel and set it back on the long counter. The underside of the bowl read Polly 1767 against the wood of the counter. She did not turn it over to look. She knew it was there.

She said to Old Hetty: I will go up to do the hair.

Old Hetty said: I have the porridge in a minute, ma’am.

Mary Baker said: We will be down in ten.

She carried Nancy on her hip out of the kitchen and up the back stair.

The morning at her right side at the east window of the back stair was the morning at this latitude in the middle of July as it came up over the cleared field. The light at the window was the colour of new straw. The cleared field below the window had the corn at its third week of laying-by, the corn standing tall enough now that a man walking down the rows was visible only at the shoulders and the head, the dark green of the leaves taking the early light and giving back a paler green. Beyond the cleared field at the head of the lane the standing oak was the oak it had been at sixty years and would be at a hundred. Beyond the oak the road went north to her mother’s house and south to the river and then south again to Halifax.

The child on her hip was the third daughter she had carried up this back stair this summer.

The child on her hip was a year old today.

She went up the stair to do the hair.

The Letter

The mid-morning of Wednesday the ninth of January, 1788, came in through the parlor window in the gray light a January morning in the Carolina piedmont came in by, and Mary Baker, at her writing-desk in the parlor in her day-gown and the wool shawl she had taken from the peg behind the parlor door when she had come down from feeding the small one in the cradle, set the inkwell to her right hand and the quill across the inkwell and the small flat of sand on the top corner of the desk and her grandfather’s last letter on her left.

The letter had come on the Tuesday post-rider three weeks ago. It had been written at Lunenburg on the fourth of December. It had been folded for the road in the way her grandfather folded letters — once long-ways and twice cross-ways and then sealed along the long edge with the wax stamp he used for the household correspondence — and it had come down the post-route through Hairston’s Cut and across the line into Halifax and on to Wentworth at the new courthouse, and from Wentworth a boy who rode the spur down to the cleared field on the black mare had brought it the next morning. She had read it twice when it had come and once more the next day. She had put it in the parlor desk’s right drawer with the household papers. She had not answered it.

She had not answered it because the question in it was not a question she had been ready to answer in December.

The cradle stood against the south wall of the parlor where she had moved it the second week of December when the cold had come in earnest and the small bedroom at the back of the house had grown too cold to hold a child of two weeks old. Ursula was in it now, asleep, twenty-seven days old as of this morning, having fed at six and again at nine, with the long sleep between the nine feed and the noon feed that the small one had begun to take across the past week. Mary Baker had watched her go down after the nine feed and had counted the breathing as she always did, the way her own mother had taught her to count it when Sarah was new, and had carried the small one to the cradle and had laid her on her side with the small linen folded under her cheek the way she liked, and had come to the desk.

The two older girls were in the back room with the wool. Sarah had been at the carding since after breakfast; Mildred Baker had been beside her with the smaller card the dancing-master at Lunenburg had given Mary Baker the year of her own ten and that Mary Baker had carried south at her marriage. Nancy was in the kitchen with Old Hetty. Mary Baker could hear Nancy’s voice from the kitchen the way a mother hears a three-year-old’s voice in the next room — the rising of it when something was happening and the falling of it when the happening was past — and she knew without listening for it that Old Hetty had begun the bread.

Gideon had ridden out at sun-up to the south field with the older Whitaker boy. The boy was twelve and was learning the work; Gideon had been bringing him along since the fall. They would be back by noon. The cleared field’s eighty acres had been cut and stacked at the close of the corn-pulling in October, and the winter’s work was the rail-mending and the salt-pork rendering and the clearing along the south edge that Gideon had been opening across the slow months, an acre or two at a time. Today was a rail morning. Mary Baker could see the line of the south rail from the parlor window, two hundred yards across the cleared ground, where the two figures had been bent over it since the gray light had come up.

She set her hand on the inkwell and uncapped it.

She unfolded her grandfather’s letter and put it under her left hand on the desk.

She read it once more.

It was a December letter of three pages. The first page was the household at Lunenburg — Vincent at Halifax going on twenty-five, well and unmarried, riding up to Lunenburg for the November court-day and returning south; the field at Lunenburg going into winter with the usual provision; the slow ache in her grandfather’s right hand that he reported as he reported most things, plainly, without comment. The second page was the district news. The Macartys on the east place had had a son in October. The Pleasants girl had married a Petersburg man. The Reverend Father Johnson was wintering at Halifax with the older sister and was not well, though her grandfather did not say what was not well about him. The Bible was on the parlor desk where it had been for forty-five years.

The third page was the question.

Her grandfather wrote it on the third page in the plain hand he used for the things that wanted thinking about and not the formal hand he used for the entries. He wrote: Mary Baker, the talk at Wentworth’s court-day, when Gideon was there last, was for or against the ratification of the federal Constitution. I read in the Virginia Gazette and in the Petersburg paper of the question’s standing in the states and of the ten that have ratified now and of the question’s coming to North Carolina in the new year. I would have your sense of the country at Rockingham. Is the talk for or against. Is the field at Wentworth, when Gideon is there, a field that knows what it wants.

She had read that paragraph the first time in December and had not known what to write back. She had read it the second time and had still not known. She had put the letter in the right drawer and had carried the question in her head across the close of December and the opening of January — across Christmas at the cleared field, across the long quiet between Christmas and the New Year, across the first week of the new year when she had been by the cradle in the parlor and in the kitchen with Old Hetty and back at the desk going over the household ledger for the close-of-year — and the question had not resolved itself in her head into a sentence she could put on paper.

She did not know what the talk at Wentworth was. Gideon went to Wentworth on court-days four times in the year, and he came home with news, and the news he came home with was the news a man brings home from a courthouse afternoon — the price of corn at the Petersburg market and the names of the men who had been at the court and who was sick up-country and who was riding to Halifax in the new week — and she had asked him over supper the Wednesday before Christmas what they had been saying about the federal Constitution. Gideon had eaten three more bites of pork from the platter before he had answered. He had said: They are saying many things, Mary Baker. They are saying it is a fine document and they are saying it is the work of the Tidewater men and not the up-country men, and they are saying the new federal courts will eat the state courts and they are saying the new federal post will be a better post than the state post is. They are saying many things. I cannot tell you which thing the country will settle on. I am not sure the country has decided. And he had eaten the three more bites of pork and had not said anything else.

She had thought about that across the long evening. She had thought about it by the cradle the next morning and in the kitchen with Old Hetty across the days after. She had not arrived at a position. She did not know whether the country was for or against the federal Constitution. She did not know whether Wentworth’s court-day was for or against. She did not know whether she was for or against, in the sense her grandfather was asking, which was the sense of a man of sixty-five at Lunenburg who reads two newspapers and sits at the parlor desk in the evenings and turns these things over.

So she would not write to her grandfather about it. She would write to him about the keeping. The keeping was what she knew.

She took up the quill and dipped it.

She wrote the date across the top of the first sheet — Wednesday 9 January 1788, Rockingham County — in the small clear hand she had been writing the household ledger in for eight years and the letters to Lunenburg in for six.

She wrote the salutation: Honored Grandfather.

She wrote the body of the letter the way the body of a letter from a granddaughter to a grandfather wanted to be written, which was as the household’s report up the post-route to the keeper of the record at the other end. She wrote that Ursula had come safely in the small hour after eleven on the night of the thirteenth of December, six pounds and eight ounces by the kitchen scale, twenty inches by the tailor’s tape — I have written the line so you may make the entry when you are next at the Bible. She wrote that Ursula was in the cradle in the parlor for the winter and was feeding well and was sleeping as a one-month child sleeps. She wrote that Sarah was at her carding and could card a pound in an hour now, which was the rate her own mother had carded at by the same age. She wrote that Mildred Baker was beside Sarah with the smaller card and was learning. She wrote that Nancy was in the kitchen with Old Hetty and was the household’s small noise. She wrote that Gideon was at the south rail this morning with the older Whitaker boy and that the cleared field was at its winter work. She wrote that her mother was on the seat to the east and was well; she had seen her on the New Year’s afternoon and they had sat together in the parlor across an hour. She wrote that the corn in the loft was holding and would carry the household to the spring milling without strain.

She did not write about the question on the third page of her grandfather’s letter.

She closed the body of the letter at the middle of the second sheet, having said what she had to say of the keeping, and she set the quill down across the inkwell and looked at the page.

There was a half-page left on the second sheet.

She did not need it for the keeping. The keeping was on the page.

She could close the letter with her name and the place and the date and seal it for the Tuesday post-rider, and the letter would go up the route through Hairston’s Cut to her grandfather at Lunenburg, and the question on the third page of his December letter would sit unanswered in his right drawer, and he would understand from her silence on the question — he was sixty-five, he was the keeper, he read both papers — that she had not had a position to offer him.

She took up the quill.

She thought about what came next.

She had thought once, on her wedding morning at fifteen in her mother’s borrowed house on the north side of the Dan, at the dark hour at the desk before the candle had been blown out and her mother’s bare feet had come down to the parlor floor, of two words she had not said aloud and had not written. The two words had stayed in her head for eight years and twenty-two days. They had stayed in her head through the births of four daughters and the burying of none and through the small daily run of a household that had moved from her mother’s seat to Gideon’s cleared eighty under her mother’s deed of attorney, and through the slow building of the parlor and the kitchen and the back-room and the lean-to and the lot, and through eight winters and eight summers and the eight years of the marriage that the morning of the wedding had been the beginning of. The two words had not been said and had not been written. They had been the way the body had carried the marriage and the keeping and the children when the mind had not yet thought to. They had been the phrase under the phrase.

She did not know why she would write them now.

She dipped the quill again.

She wrote, on the half-page left at the second sheet, beneath the close of the keeping-letter, in the same small clear hand she had been writing the body of the letter in, in lowercase and without a period:

we continue

She wrote her name below it, in full, in the hand the household ledger had been carrying for eight years:

Mary Baker Johnson

She looked at the page.

The two words sat above her name on the half-page. They were the size of the rest of the writing on the page. They did not have a flourish or a capital or a punctuation. They sat on the page the way a thing that has been carried for a long time sits on a page the first time it is set down.

She did not think anything about them.

She blotted the page with the flat of sand. She poured the sand back into the small dish on the corner of the desk. She folded the two sheets once long-ways and twice cross-ways the way her grandfather folded letters and the way she folded letters now. She sealed the long edge with the small wax stamp Gideon had had cut for her at Petersburg in their second year — the J at the center for Johnson, the small flourish on the right that the cutter had added without asking and that she had kept because she had liked it — and she set the letter on the corner of the desk where the Tuesday post would carry it the week after.

She sat at the desk a moment longer.

The parlor was the parlor it had been at the start of the morning. The cradle against the south wall. The small breathing from inside it. Nancy’s voice from the kitchen. Sarah and Mildred Baker in the back room with the wool. The gray light at the window. The corn-stubble of the cleared ground out the window to the south, and the two figures by the south rail along the line of the eighty.

She thought about her cousin Henry at the up-country store.

Henry kept a trade-store at the head of the Smith River three days’ ride west, where the post-route ran out and the Cherokee road came down through the gap. He was her mother’s first cousin on the Vass side, a man of forty-some at the store since the close of the war, who had carried the household’s salt and the household’s iron from his store for the four years they had been on the cleared field. He extended credit at the store the way a country trade-store extended credit — in NC paper at the season’s price, or in trade at the year’s settlement.

She had read in Tscharner’s December letter that the federal Constitution had been ratified in ten of the thirteen states and that the question was coming to North Carolina in the new year. She had read in the Petersburg Gazette Gideon had brought home from Wentworth in November that the paper currency of North Carolina was trading at three to one against the Virginia paper along the up-country line. She had heard Old Hetty say, the Tuesday before Christmas, that salt at the river had gone up by a quarter in the close of November.

She did not put these three things into a sentence in her head.

She took up a second sheet of foolscap from the left drawer and dipped the quill again.

She wrote:

Henry — for the spring settlement. Do not extend on my account this season in paper. Only in trade. I will send the surplus from the back field after the milling and you will hold the credit against the year’s salt and iron at the trade price. If the paper hardens I will come to you in May and we will recount. — M. B. Johnson

She blotted it. She folded it once. She did not seal it; Gideon would carry it to Wentworth on the next court-day at the close of January and would hand it to Henry’s rider who came in from the river on the second Friday. It would go up the river road by Friday evening. Henry would have it before the close of the month.

She did not think anything about the second letter either.

She set the second letter on top of the first on the corner of the desk and capped the inkwell and laid the quill across the top of the inkwell and pushed the chair back from the desk.

She stood up from the desk.

Ursula made the small sound from the cradle that the small one made before she was fully awake. Mary Baker turned at the sound and went the four steps across the parlor to the cradle and put her hand on the back of the small one inside it and waited for the breathing to go long. The breathing went long. She stood by the cradle a moment longer with her hand on the back of the child and looked, through the parlor window on the south, at the two figures by the south rail of the eighty in the gray light of the January mid-morning, who were her husband and the older Whitaker boy at the rail-mending in the eighth year of the marriage and the third year on the cleared field.

She took her hand off the cradle.

She went to the kitchen to see what Nancy was doing.

Interlude — The Constitution in North Carolina

The federal Constitution that Tscharner DeGraffenreid had asked his granddaughter about in his December letter of 1787 had been signed at Philadelphia on the seventeenth of September of that year and sent out to the states for ratification across the autumn. Delaware ratified first on the seventh of December, eleven days before Tscharner sat at his parlor desk at Lunenburg to write the December letter. Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Georgia, and Connecticut followed across December and the first week of January 1788. By the morning Mary Baker sat down at her own writing-desk at Rockingham, six of the thirteen states had ratified. Four more — Massachusetts, Maryland, South Carolina, and New Hampshire — would ratify across the spring and early summer, bringing the count to ten and crossing the nine-state threshold the new federal government required. The Constitution would take effect.

North Carolina would not ratify with the ten. The Hillsborough Convention, sitting from the twenty-first of July to the fourth of August of 1788 — six months and twelve days after the morning of Mary Baker’s letter — would decline to ratify by a vote of one hundred eighty-four to eighty-three. The state would not ratify until the Fayetteville Convention of the twenty-first of November of 1789, sixteen months after Hillsborough and twenty-two months after Mary Baker’s letter. For those fourteen months between the two conventions North Carolina would sit outside the federal Union — its currency the unbacked state paper, its mails outside the new federal post, its courts outside the new federal jurisdiction. The Rockingham piedmont would feel the absence at the trade-store and the courthouse and the post-route, in ways the household at the cleared field would price one fragment at a time across the long winter and the spring and the summer that followed.

Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson, sitting at her writing-desk on the morning of the ninth of January of 1788, would not have known any of this. She would not have known that the Hillsborough vote would go against ratification, nor that the Fayetteville vote sixteen months later would go for it. She would not have known what the country was about to decide. She wrote the letter she could write with what she knew, and she signed it as she had begun to sign letters that morning, and she sealed it and set it on the corner of the desk for the Tuesday post.

The Drover

The kitchen door at the back of the cleared eighty stood open on the afternoon of Friday the third of June, 1791, and the early-June light from the cleared ground came in across the kitchen floor in a long bar that ran from the threshold to the hearth and caught the edge of the table where Old Hetty had set out the bread and the salt-pork and the buttermilk. The day had come up dry and warm. The spring planting had been finished into the corn-rows along the east field the week before. The cleared ground out the kitchen door was green with the corn at six weeks and ankle-high. The flax along the south edge was past its blooming. The piedmont farm in early June was past the breaking of new ground and was at the long tending the season turned to next, and Mary Baker had been at the parlor desk after the noon dinner with the spring settlement ledger open in front of her and Sarah on the stool at her right with the small slate.

She had been showing Sarah how the credit-and-debit column at the right side of the page took the entries from the left. Sarah was ten years and eight months and would be doing the ledger in earnest by twelve and carrying it by fifteen, the way Mary Baker had carried her own mother’s by the same age. The lesson today had been the column of the spring’s pork rendering against the credit Henry held at the up-country store. Sarah had got the addition on the third try. The first two she had carried the wrong digit. The third she had carried the right one, and Mary Baker had said nothing about the first two, because the first two were not the lesson; the third was.

Sumner came in by the kitchen door at the hour past two, with Old Hetty calling him in from the lot.

Mary Baker heard the call from the parlor. She set the quill across the inkwell. She told Sarah to come in fifteen minutes and finish the column. She rose from the desk and went through the back room and into the kitchen, untying her apron from the waist and tying it again at the knot she used when company was at the back door.

Sumner was a Halifax man of forty or thereabouts, a drover who had come up the post-route through Hairston’s Cut on the chestnut gelding she had seen tied at the lot-rail through the kitchen window. She had seen him through twice before — once in the fall of the year before, on his way south to the cattle-sale at Halifax, and once in the early spring of this year on his way back up to Lunenburg with the cattle-money in his belt — and she knew his name and his route and the kin he had at Halifax who connected him to the Wades on her grandfather’s east place. He had a long brown face and a road-coat the dust of three days on the route had got into and a hat he had hung on the peg at the kitchen door when Old Hetty had called him in. He took the bench at the kitchen table where Old Hetty pointed him and he washed his hands at the basin Old Hetty set in front of him and he ate the bread and the salt-pork and drank the buttermilk in the unhurried way a man eats who has eaten at three kitchens up the route in the past two days and knows there will be a fourth before he sleeps.

Mary Baker stood at the kitchen with her hand on the back of the bench across from him and asked after the route.

He told her. He had come up from Halifax on the Wednesday. He had laid over at the Wade place on the Wednesday night. He had come on the post-road through the Cut on the Thursday and had slept on a settle at Hairston’s. He had come on the spur down to her gate this morning and would go on north to Lunenburg through the close of the day and would lay over there a night and would go on west to the up-country store at the head of the Smith River on the Sunday or the Monday. Henry the store-keeper was a man he traded with twice in the year and would trade with again on this trip; he had a fold of bills in the belt from the spring cattle-sale that he meant to put down against Henry’s iron account.

She nodded. She did not say anything about Henry.

He drank the buttermilk and set the cup down and looked at her across the table with the comfortable steadiness of a man who knows what he has to tell the household at his next stop. He said the thing he had been carrying. He said: Mrs. Johnson, I was through Halifax in the March. I saw your brother Vincent and Martha at the house there. They were well. The child was born some weeks before. A boy. The name and the day I cannot give you; I was at the house an hour and not at the entry-Bible. The boy was thriving. Martha was up and about the house. Your brother was at the field. I told him I would be coming up the route in the early summer and would name him at the houses I came to.

Mary Baker did not answer for a count.

Then she said: I thank you for the carrying of it, Mr. Sumner.

He bent his head over the cup once, the way a man bends his head when an errand has been done.

She said: Mother is on the east place. She would want to know.

He said: I had thought to ride by the east place tomorrow before the Cut. Mrs. DeGraffenreid was well in the spring.

She said: She is well now. I saw her Sunday.

He bent his head over the cup again. He went back to the salt-pork.

Old Hetty was at the hearth with her back to the table, doing something with the next thing on her own afternoon — Mary Baker did not look. Nancy was somewhere in the lot with the older Whitaker boy who came in some afternoons; Mary Baker could hear her voice from outside the kitchen door. Ursula and Polly Baker were down in the parlor in the long afternoon nap the small ones still took. Mildred Baker was in the back room with the wool. Sarah was at the parlor desk over the column.

Sumner ate.

Mary Baker took down the small writing-board from the shelf above the kitchen window where Old Hetty kept the household marketing-slate. She did not need it for the words she was holding in her head, but she set it on the table and ran her hand across the slate-face the way her mother had run her hand across the slate-face when she had been thinking through something across the kitchen table, and she said to Sumner, while he was eating: What did the man at the store say about the salt at the spring.

Sumner finished what he had in his mouth. He said: Eighteen shillings the bushel, the time I was through. Henry had said so on the first Tuesday in May, when I had stopped there going down. The freight from Petersburg had been on time across April and the salt was at the May figure. I had no cause to ask again on the way up.

She said: Eighteen.

He said: Eighteen the bushel. I am near sure.

She said: I thank you.

He nodded.

He finished the bread. He finished the salt-pork. He drank what was left of the buttermilk and set the cup upside down on the rim of the plate the way a man on the route sets a cup when he has finished. He said he would be on by half past, because he wanted Lunenburg before the dark. He thanked Old Hetty for the kitchen. He stood up from the bench and took his hat from the peg and put it on.

Mary Baker walked with him out the kitchen door to the lot.

The chestnut was tied at the rail in the shade of the lot’s elm. Sumner went to him and uncinched and re-cinched the saddle and put his hand on the gelding’s neck the unhurried way a man on the route checks his horse. The gelding stood quiet. Sumner mounted. He bent down from the saddle at the rail and said: I am obliged for the kitchen, Mrs. Johnson. If there is a thing you would have me carry up to your grandfather at Lunenburg, I will carry it.

Mary Baker thought for a count. She said: Tell my grandfather that the household is well. Tell him I will write at the Tuesday post.

He said: I will tell him so.

He touched the brim of his hat and turned the gelding and rode up the spur to the post-road at the easy walking pace a man on the route used in the long-light afternoon when there was no hurry on the day. Mary Baker stood at the lot-rail and watched him to the bend in the spur where the chestnuts closed over the road, and she watched the dust come up under the gelding’s hooves where the road dust had dried in the afternoon, and the dust hung in the bar of light the way road-dust hangs in piedmont air on a dry June day, and she did not move from the lot-rail until he had gone behind the chestnuts. Then she went back into the kitchen.

Old Hetty was wiping the table. The board was where Mary Baker had left it.

Mary Baker said: Hetty, thank you for the kitchen.

Old Hetty said: Mr. Sumner was glad of it.

Mary Baker took the board and went back through the back room and into the parlor.

Sarah was at the desk on the stool, the column finished in the small even hand Mary Baker had been teaching her to write a number in, the credit and debit balanced as they ought to be. Sarah looked up. Mary Baker said the column was right, and Sarah might go and see what Nancy was at in the lot, and Sarah went, in the unhurried obedient way the older girl had been moving across the long spring.

Mary Baker sat at the desk.

She took up a fresh sheet of foolscap from the left drawer and set it on the desk. She uncapped the inkwell and took up the quill. She wrote the date across the top — Friday 3 June 1791, Rockingham County — in the small clear hand. She wrote the salutation: Honored Mother.

She wrote the body of the letter in eight or nine sentences. She wrote that Sumner the drover from Halifax had been at the kitchen this afternoon on his way up to Lunenburg. She wrote that he had seen Vincent and Martha at Halifax in the March. They were well. The child had been born some weeks before — a boy. She wrote that Sumner had not been able to give the date or the name; he had been at the house an hour and not at the entry-Bible. She wrote that Martha was up and about the house and Vincent was at the field. She wrote: Mother, when you write to Grandfather at Lunenburg, ask him to make the entry for Vincent’s child when the day and the name are firm. The man Sumner could not carry them. She wrote that Sumner would be at the east place tomorrow on his way through the Cut and would tell what he had told her. She wrote that the household here was well, the corn at six weeks, the four girls and the small one all in their places, Gideon at the south field with the older Whitaker boy. She wrote: I send this by Old Hetty’s grandson Daniel, who will bring it by the east place this evening, so you have it before Sumner. She closed it: Your daughter, Mary Baker Johnson. She signed.

She blotted the page with the flat of sand.

She folded the sheet once long-ways and once cross-ways and sealed it with the small wax stamp.

She set the sealed letter on the corner of the desk where Daniel would find it when he came to the kitchen for it at four.

She did not write to Lunenburg. She had said she would write at the Tuesday post. Sumner would carry the word ahead, and the Tuesday letter would carry the household’s hand. The letter up to her grandfather would be the season’s letter of the keeping. It could wait the four days.

She took up the spring settlement ledger and turned to the page where Sarah had been working.

She wrote, at the line below the day’s entry where she had been writing the household’s marketing-figures across April and May: Salt, eighteen the bushel at the store, per Sumner the drover, 3 June 1791. She wrote it in the ledger because the figure was now the one she had set against the credit Henry held for the year’s salt and iron. The fall salt-buy would be six bushels for the year, the way the household bought it. Six bushels at eighteen the bushel would be a hundred and eight shillings. She had been carrying twenty the bushel in her head since the close-of-April figure Gideon had brought home from the courthouse — the figure he had got from a man at Wentworth who had been through Henry’s that month — and twenty had been the figure she had set against the credit Henry held for her. Eighteen was the better news. The household would be on the right side of the credit by twelve shillings on the year’s salt against what she had been holding for.

She did not write that recognition into the ledger. The recognition was not the ledger’s business. The ledger’s business was the figure.

She closed the ledger and set it on the corner of the desk above the day’s letter and capped the inkwell and laid the quill across the inkwell.

She sat at the desk a moment.

The June afternoon light had moved across the floor of the parlor while she had been writing and was now on the far edge of the carpet where the small one’s cradle had been in the winter and was now folded against the wall. Polly Baker was on a small pallet at the south wall in the long afternoon nap; Ursula was on the settle by the cold hearth in hers. Mary Baker could hear Mildred Baker in the back room turning the wool. From the lot she could hear Nancy’s voice and Sarah’s voice and the older Whitaker boy’s — the three of them at something at the lot’s south corner; she could not make out what. The household was at the long quiet of a piedmont afternoon between dinner and supper at the close of the spring.

She thought about Vincent and Martha at Halifax with the new boy.

She thought about her grandfather at Lunenburg, who would not know the boy was on the world until either Sumner reached him this evening or the Tuesday letter reached him next week. She thought about the entry her grandfather would want to make in the Bible when the day and the name came firm. She thought that he would not press her mother for the date but would wait for it, the way he waited for the things he waited for, and would make the entry when the entry could be made.

She thought about Henry at the up-country store, who would be receiving Sumner on the Sunday or the Monday with the spring cattle-money and would put it down against the iron account. She thought that Henry would have the right salt-figure as of this afternoon. She thought that she would have it from Sumner herself on his return down the route in five or six weeks if she had any cause to want it again, but the eighteen the bushel was the figure she was working from now, and she had set it in the ledger, and the eighteen would stand until something replaced it.

She did not know that the eighteen had stood, at the store, only across April. She did not know that the freight from Petersburg had come late at the close of May because the road through Person County had washed in a Tuesday rain, and the salt at the store had gone up to twenty-three the bushel on the third of June — the same day she was writing eighteen into the ledger — and would stay at twenty-three across the rest of the summer. She did not know that Sumner, riding north this afternoon, was carrying the April figure to her in good faith and was a month behind on it without knowing.

She closed the ledger.

She went to see what Nancy and Sarah were at, in the lot.

Six weeks later, on the afternoon of Friday the fifteenth of July, 1791, a second drover came up the spur from the post-road. He was a younger man, Mary Baker had not seen before, a man named Halsey who was riding for the Halifax cattle-buyer and was coming north with the summer’s first drive. He stopped at the kitchen door at the hour before three. He ate at the kitchen table the way Sumner had eaten six weeks before. He talked about the route. He talked about Petersburg and the road through Person County that had washed in May and had been mended in June and was passable now but not at the speed the spring freight had wanted. He talked about Henry at the up-country store, where he had stopped going down to Halifax the week before. He named, at one point in the conversation, the salt-price at Henry’s. He said: Twenty-three the bushel. The freight came late through May. Henry has had it at twenty-three since the third of June.

Mary Baker did not move.

She said: Twenty-three.

He said: Twenty-three, since the third of June.

She said: I had it at eighteen on the third of June.

He thought a moment. He said: Eighteen had been the April figure. The third of June, when the freight came in late, was when Henry put it up.

She said: I had a man through here on the third of June who had it at eighteen.

Halsey said: He had had it on his way down. He had not had it on the way up. The salt was at twenty-three by the time he was at Hairston’s, but the man would not have known unless he had gone back through Henry’s.

She said: I see.

He went on eating.

She stood at the kitchen with her hand on the bench-back, the way she had stood at it on the afternoon of Friday the third of June, and did not say what she was working out in her head. Six bushels at twenty-three the bushel was a hundred and thirty-eight shillings. She had been holding for a hundred and eight shillings the year’s salt. The household was now thirty shillings on the wrong side of the credit she had set against Henry’s account, against the figure that had been in her head. She had been working all of May and the first half of June and three weeks of July from the eighteen, and the figure had been wrong since the third of June, and she had set it into the ledger on the third of June after Sumner had told her so in good faith, and Sumner had not known. The ledger had been wrong since the third of June. The spring settlement she had built around it had been wrong since the third of June. She would have to recount.

She thought, standing at the bench-back, that the thirty shillings was the cost. The cost was small. The household would absorb it. The corn would carry. The fall pork would carry. The wool would carry the way wool always carried. Gideon need not be told; the household ledger was hers and the figure was hers and the recounting was hers.

She said to Halsey: I am obliged to you.

He bent his head over the cup.

She walked him out to the lot when he had finished. She watched him to the bend in the spur the way she had watched Sumner. Then she went back into the kitchen and through the back room and into the parlor and sat at the desk.

She took the ledger from the right drawer. She opened it to the third of June. She did not strike the eighteen through. She wrote, in the line below the eighteen, in the same small even hand: Salt at the store at twenty-three the bushel since 3 June, per Halsey 15 July 1791. The April figure had not held.

She closed the ledger.

She set it back in the right drawer.

She sat at the desk for a count of perhaps half a minute and looked out the south window of the parlor at the cleared ground, where the corn was at the eleven-week and was waist-high along the rows, and where Gideon could be made out at the south fence with the older Whitaker boy who was thirteen now and putting in a full hand on the half-days, and she thought that the afternoon was the afternoon a Friday afternoon in the middle of July had a right to be. She thought that she would not say anything about the salt to Gideon. She thought that the thirty shillings was the cost of having had Sumner through on the third of June and not having had Halsey, and that the cost of having had Sumner was also the carrying of the news of Vincent and Martha’s boy. She thought that the carrying of the news of the boy was worth the thirty shillings on the salt many times over, and that she would not have traded the one for the other had the trade been offered, but that the trade had not been offered: the same man had been both the carrying of the news and the wrong figure on the salt, and the keeping had taken both as the man had carried them and would absorb both as the keeping absorbed what came in by the kitchen door.

She did not put that thought into a sentence in her head either.

She stood up from the desk.

She went through the back room and into the kitchen, where Old Hetty was at the supper-pot, and she went out the kitchen door to the lot to see what Nancy was at in the long July afternoon.

The Lamp at the Reading

The supper had been cleared by half past six on the evening of Tuesday the twenty-fourth of January, 1792, and Mary Baker had carried the last of the dishes through to the kitchen and had set the children to their evening tasks and had come back to the parlor and built up the fire and lit the lamp on the parlor table before she had let herself think about the paper Gideon had ridden home with from Wentworth.

The paper was folded in his coat pocket. The coat was on the peg by the parlor door. He had come in at four with the dusk already gathering along the south rail of the cleared field and the cold close on his face and the chestnut blowing in the lot, and he had taken the coat off and hung it and had said over his shoulder, I have the Petersburg paper, Mary Baker. I will read it after supper. He had not said what was in it. He had gone through to the back room to wash, and he had eaten his supper, and he had not said what was in it across the meal either, with Sarah and Mildred Baker either side of the table and Ursula on the bench beside him and the small one on Mary Baker’s knee and Old Hetty going to and from the kitchen, and Mary Baker had known, by the not-saying, that what was in the paper was not the day’s price-of-corn news or the names of the men who had been to the court.

She had asked, once, as the supper was clearing: Was the court the usual. And Gideon had said: The court was the usual. The paper has the news from the upper country. And he had not said anything else.

She had cleared the dishes. She had set Sarah to the dish-towel and Mildred Baker to the cup-shelf. She had carried Polly Baker upstairs and had set her in the cradle by the south window of the upstairs back room and had stood beside the cradle until the small one’s breathing had gone long, and she had come down to the kitchen and had told Old Hetty to bring Nancy in from the back when she had finished the supper-pot and to put the small girls up. She had built the fire up in the parlor. She had set the lamp on the parlor table in the place she set it on winter evenings when the household had reading to do. She had pulled the parlor settle a little closer to the table. She had sat down in her own chair at the desk, where she could see the table and the settle without turning her head.

Sarah came in from the kitchen at a quarter past seven with her hair newly braided and the parlor robe over her day-gown and her hands carrying the workbasket from the back room. She had Mildred Baker behind her and Nancy holding the back of Mildred Baker’s gown. The three of them came across the carpet and Mildred Baker climbed onto the settle and Nancy climbed up beside her and Sarah set the workbasket on the floor beside the settle and took the settle’s right-hand end where she would have the lamp’s light on her sewing.

Sarah was eleven years and three months and fifteen days. She had been doing the household’s plain sewing across the autumn and was now beginning the better needlework her mother had been carrying her through across the winter — the marking of the small one’s caps with the worked letter, the hemming of the sheets on the small turn. Mildred Baker was nine years and two months and fourteen days. Mildred Baker was at the carding still; she did not sew the better work, but she was old enough now to sit by the lamp in the evenings and to be quiet while the older ones read or worked. Nancy was seven and a half. Nancy had a small slate on her lap and was writing her letters.

Gideon came in from the back room with the paper in his hand.

He took down his chair from beside the fire and set it on the parlor side of the table and sat down in it. He laid the paper on the table in front of him, folded the way the post-rider folded papers for the road. He took his reading-spectacles from the small case he kept inside the breast of his coat — he did not yet need them for the working scale but had begun to use them by lamp-light across the past two winters — and put them on. He unfolded the paper.

It was a Petersburg paper. The Virginia Gazette and Petersburg Intelligencer across the top in the heavy black type of the masthead. The date was the seventh of January, 1792. Seventeen days old. He had paid the boy three pence for it at the court and had brought it home for the household reading.

He looked across the table at Mary Baker.

He said: The news from the western country is hard, Mary Baker. The federal force under General St. Clair was beaten badly at the upper Wabash on the fourth of November. The Gazette has the casualty list across two columns. I had heard the rumour at the court last week but had not had the names until tonight.

Mary Baker did not answer for a count of perhaps three breaths.

Then she said: Read it, Mr. Johnson.

She said it in the voice she used by lamp-light in the evenings when the reading was the household’s business and the children were gathered to listen and the saying was for all of them. She did not say it sharp. She did not say it soft. She said it the way her own mother had said read it, Vincent in the Lunenburg parlor in the December of 1781 when Vincent had come home from the war with the paper from Richmond and the news of Yorktown had been three months old and her mother had wanted the household to have it from a son’s voice and not from her own.

Gideon read.

He read first the paragraph at the head of the column — the Gazette’s plain account, dated from the Pittsburgh post: that General Arthur St. Clair, in command of a federal force of approximately fourteen hundred regulars and levies and militia, had been encamped on the upper Wabash some ninety miles north of Fort Washington on the night of the third of November; that the camp had been attacked at first light on the morning of the fourth by a force of Native warriors under the Miami chief Little Turtle and the Shawnee chief Blue Jacket, with Delaware and other contributions; that the engagement had run three hours; that the federal force had been broken and had been driven from the field; that the wounded who could not walk had been left to the field; that the federal dead were not yet fully counted but were known to be above six hundred, with the wounded above two hundred; that General St. Clair had survived but was relieved of command pending the inquiry the President had called for; that the killed at the field were the heaviest loss of federal arms in any engagement with the Indian nations since the country had been a country.

He stopped a moment after the paragraph.

He took his spectacles off and wiped them on the linen on the table and put them back on.

He read the column.

The column was the casualty list by regiment, written out by the Gazette in the form the federal adjutant had printed it. Colonels first, then majors, then captains, then lieutenants and ensigns, then the sergeants and corporals and privates by company. Gideon read the column with the unhurried care of a man putting a name into the room because the name should be in a room somewhere. Colonel Oldham of the Kentucky militia, killed in the first volley. Major-General Richard Butler, second in command, killed on the line. Major Ferguson of the artillery, killed at the guns. Major Heart, killed. Major Clark, killed. Captain Bradford, killed. Captain Phelon, killed. Captain Kirkwood, killed on the line — the Kirkwood who came through Hairston’s Cut in the spring of 1789, Gideon said, lifting his head from the page; I had not known he had been to the Wabash. He went back to the page. Captain Newman, killed. Captain Smith, killed. Captain Purdy, killed.

The lamp on the parlor table had been trimmed by Old Hetty at the close of the afternoon to the working height for the household’s evening reading. The wick had been set to a quarter-inch above the brass collar. The flame at the wick was the steady yellow flame the lamp gave when the oil was at the line. Sarah, at the settle’s right-hand end with her sewing in her lap and the lamp’s light across the page of the worked letter on the small one’s cap she was hemming, had her eyes on her work for the first dozen names and then had not had her eyes on her work, and her hands had gone still in her lap, and she had not put them back to the work, and she was looking past the lamp at her father’s face across the table while he read.

(Sarah, at the settle on the night her father read out the casualty list from the upper Wabash, would remember the lamp going lower as he read — the wick burning down the oil at the well below the collar, the flame at the wick paling a little in the second hour without anyone trimming it — and would remember it again on the fourth of February 1809 in her own kitchen in Cumberland County, her own first daughter four days old asleep in the small carved cradle beside the hearth, when her husband would come in from the field with the Fayetteville paper and would read out across the kitchen table the names of men killed in some other federal engagement on some other frontier she would not be able to find on the map, and she would understand, then and not before, what her mother had been carrying that winter at twenty-seven, at her own parlor lamp in Rockingham, without saying.)

Gideon read on.

He read the captains to the end of the column. He read the lieutenants — Spear, McMath, Boyd, Brock, Warren, Hopper. He read the ensigns. He read the sergeants. The list of the private soldiers was the longer part of the column, and Gideon read it at the same care, by the company-letter and the regimental designation, name by name, the way the adjutant had printed it. He did not skim. He did not pause for breath where the breaks fell. He read it as it had been printed. The reading took past eight o’clock.

Mildred Baker, at the settle, had put her head against Sarah’s shoulder somewhere in the middle of the list of the privates. Nancy had set the small slate aside and had drawn her knees up under her gown and had her arms around them. Polly Baker was upstairs in the cradle. Ursula had been put up by Old Hetty with the others in the back. The fire in the grate had burned down to the bed of coals; Mary Baker had not got up to add the wood. The parlor was at the lamp.

Gideon read the last of the privates.

He folded the paper.

He took off his spectacles and put them back into the case and put the case back inside the breast of his coat. He sat with his hands on the folded paper a moment. The brass of the spectacle-case caught a small reflection of the lamp on his coat-front and threw it onto the underside of the table.

He said: That is the column.

Mary Baker said: I thank you, Mr. Johnson.

She said it in the voice she used to thank him for the reading on a winter evening. The voice did not say more than the words. The two of them had been together at the lamp for bad news before — the news of the south Carolina disasters in the early eighties, the smallpox at Halifax in the autumn of 1786, the Pleasants girl’s brother dying at the close of 1789 — and the I thank you was the formula the household had built across those readings. The formula did the carrying.

Sarah was looking at her mother.

Mary Baker did not look back at Sarah immediately. She held her eye on the folded paper at her husband’s hand. She let the silence in the parlor go the length the silence wanted — the count of perhaps a half-minute, with the coals settling in the grate and Mildred Baker’s breathing against Sarah’s shoulder gone to the slow rhythm of a child going to sleep where she sat — and then she looked at Sarah and held her eye for the count of one breath and said: Mildred Baker is asleep, daughter. Help me get her up.

Sarah stood. She set her sewing on the workbasket. She and her mother lifted Mildred Baker between them off the settle, with Mildred Baker’s head against Mary Baker’s shoulder and her arms going around her mother’s neck without coming fully awake, and Mary Baker carried her to the foot of the parlor stair. Nancy stood up from the settle and came to her mother’s hip the way a seven-year-old came when she did not want to be left in the parlor with the news. Mary Baker said: Up, all three, to the back room. Sarah, see Nancy to her bed and look in at Ursula and the small one. I will be up.

Sarah said: Yes ma’am.

The three of them went up the stair, Sarah with the candle from the parlor table, Mildred Baker against her mother’s shoulder going up in Mary Baker’s arms, Nancy at the rail behind Sarah on the stair. Mary Baker carried Mildred Baker to the room and laid her on the bed beside Ursula already there and pulled the quilt up over her shoulder and went to the cradle and put her hand on Polly Baker’s back for a count and came out to the hall. Sarah was at the door of Nancy’s room with the candle. Nancy was inside in the nightshift she had put on before the parlor reading and was already in the bed. Mary Baker said good-night to Nancy at the door and turned and put her hand on Sarah’s shoulder and held it there a moment and said: To bed, daughter. Sarah said yes ma’am and went into her own room with the candle. Mary Baker stood at the head of the hall until the line of candlelight under Sarah’s door had moved to the right side of the room, where Sarah set the candle on the small table by her own bed.

She went back down the stair.

Gideon was at the parlor table where she had left him. He had taken the paper out again and had it open at the column. He was looking at it without reading it. He looked up at her when she came in and folded the paper again.

She said: Have you eaten enough at the supper.

He said: I have eaten enough.

She said: I will write to Vincent at Halifax tonight. The post-rider goes Thursday. I will send the letter to him by the spur.

He said: Vincent is at Halifax.

She said: He is at Halifax. Martha is there with him and the boy. Mother will not have heard the news at the east place yet. Vincent will hear it from the men at Halifax by the end of the week if he has not heard it tonight. I will write to him so he has a sister’s letter as well as the men’s news.

Gideon nodded.

He said: I had thought to ride down to Halifax myself in the second week of February. I will hold the ride a fortnight on the news. The roads will be the better in the back end of the month and I will have your letter to carry by hand if you want it carried.

She said: I will have the letter for you Thursday. The post will be faster than your ride. But if you ride after the letter is gone, you may carry word the letter cannot.

He said: I will go in the last week of February.

He sat with his hands on the folded paper a moment longer and then stood and put the paper on the desk beside her writing-place and put his hand on her shoulder once and went up the stair to the bedroom.

Mary Baker stood at the parlor table.

The lamp on the table had burned its oil down to a quarter-inch below the brass collar. The flame at the wick was the steady pale yellow flame the lamp gave when the oil was at that line; in another twenty minutes it would begin to gutter. The coals in the grate had banked themselves down to the red bed under the white ash that a piedmont fire banked itself to in the second hour without wood. The parlor was at the bottom of its evening.

She sat at the writing-desk.

She took a half-sheet of foolscap from the left drawer. She uncapped the inkwell. She took up the quill. She wrote the date across the top — Tuesday 24 January 1792, Rockingham County — in the small clear hand she used for letters to kin. She wrote: Brother.

She wrote the body of the letter in seven or eight sentences. She wrote that Mr. Johnson had come home from the court at Wentworth this afternoon with the Petersburg paper of the seventh and that the paper had carried the casualty list from the upper Wabash. She wrote that General St. Clair’s command had been beaten at the field on the fourth of November and that the federal dead were above six hundred. She wrote that the household at Rockingham was well, the four older girls in their beds, the small one in her cradle, Mr. Johnson home from the court without harm. She wrote that she had not yet had the news at the east place but would write to Mother in the morning. She wrote: Vincent, I am writing this evening because the news is the news and a sister’s letter is a sister’s letter and the post is the post. Martha and the boy are well I trust. Write me when you can. She closed it: Your sister, Mary Baker Johnson. She signed.

She did not say anything in the letter about the column of names. She did not say anything about the lamp. She did not name Captain Kirkwood, who had come through Hairston’s Cut in the spring of 1789. She did not name what she had been carrying at the parlor table at the reading, because the carrying was not the letter’s business; the letter’s business was the news and the household’s well and a sister’s hand at her brother’s name.

She blotted the page with the flat of sand. She folded it once long-ways and once cross-ways and sealed it with the small wax stamp. She set the sealed letter on the corner of the desk where she would find it in the morning to put up for Old Hetty’s grandson Daniel to carry to the post at Wentworth on the Thursday.

She did not write to her mother. The letter to her mother would be the morning’s letter. The morning’s letter would want the morning’s hand.

She capped the inkwell.

She laid the quill across the inkwell.

She sat at the desk a moment with her hands flat on the desk-top and the lamp at her right hand burning its oil down to the bottom of the line.

She thought about Sarah at the settle.

She thought that Sarah had been at the settle for the reading of the column and had heard it through, and that Sarah had not asked anything when the reading was done, and that Sarah had taken Mildred Baker up to the bed and had gone to her own bed with the candle without saying anything to her mother in the hall. She thought that Sarah was old enough now, at eleven, to hear the reading without needing to be told what to make of it, and old enough to know that her mother was not in a place this evening to tell her what to make of it either. She thought that this was something a daughter learned by being at the lamp on the evening her father read out the names. She thought that her own first reading like this had been the news of the August fighting in the second year of the war, when she had been not quite thirteen and had been at the Lunenburg parlor with her mother and her grandfather and Vincent at the lamp, and her grandfather had read the names from the Richmond paper, and her mother had not said anything when the reading was done either, and she had taken the small ones up to the bed.

She thought that the news was its own carrying. She thought that the keeping would absorb the news the way the keeping had absorbed the salt-figure in the summer and the question her grandfather had asked her in the December of 1787 about the ratification — the way the keeping absorbed what came in by the door of an evening — and that she would write to her mother at the east place in the morning and to her grandfather at Lunenburg by the Tuesday post, and that there was no more to be done with the news tonight than had been done.

She did not put that thought into a sentence in her head either.

She blew out the lamp.

The parlor went to the red of the coals.

She stood at the desk a moment and let her eyes find the room at the new light, and she could see the settle where the three of her daughters had sat at the reading, and the workbasket on the floor beside it with Sarah’s worked-letter cap folded at the top, and the chair where Gideon had been at the reading, and the corner of the table where the Petersburg paper of the seventh of January was now folded under the inkwell. She stood there a count of perhaps half a minute.

Then she went up the stair to the bedroom, in the dark she had walked the stair in for the seven years and a quarter since they had built the house on the cleared eighty.

She did not carry a candle. She knew the stair.

Interlude — The Inquiry and the Letters

The Petersburg paper of the seventh of January 1792 that Gideon Johnson had read out at the parlor lamp on the Tuesday evening of the twenty-fourth had named the casualty list and had not named what would follow. What would follow was an inquiry. In the House of Representatives on the second of February of 1792, a motion was made to form a committee to investigate the federal defeat. On the twenty-seventh of March the House approved a select committee of seven members under the chairmanship of Thomas FitzSimons of Pennsylvania, the first congressional investigation of an executive-branch action in the country’s history. The committee took testimony across the spring and delivered its report on the eighth of May. The report cleared General St. Clair of personal fault — commending his preparation and his coolness in the engagement — and placed primary blame on the Quartermaster General, Samuel Hodgdon, and the contractor William Duer, whose bankruptcy across the same spring compounded the federal disgrace. St. Clair had resigned his military commission on the seventh of April, a month before the report.

The President had moved already on the replacement. By an act of the second Congress on the fifth of March 1792 the federal force was reconstituted as the Legion of the United States; on the thirteenth of April Major General Anthony Wayne, who had taken Stony Point in the Revolutionary War, was commissioned its senior officer. Wayne spent the summer recruiting at Pittsburgh and the winter of 1792 and 1793 drilling the Legion at Legionville on the Ohio. Two years and four months later, on the twentieth of August 1794, the Legion would engage a confederated Native force under the Shawnee war chief Blue Jacket on the Maumee River and would carry the field. The Treaty of Greenville of the third of August 1795 would cede most of present-day Ohio and parts of Indiana to the federal government, opening settlement north of the Ohio River. The Native side of these engagements — the councils, the commands, the names that the Shawnee and Miami and Delaware used among themselves, the counts of their own dead — the federal record does not preserve at the resolution at which it preserves itself.

Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson, sitting at her writing-desk at Rockingham in the spring of 1793, would not yet have known any of this. She would not yet have known the news from France either — what had been done at Paris in January, and what the messengers were carrying out across the Atlantic. She wrote the morning’s letters with what she knew, and she set them on the corner of the desk for the Tuesday post.

The Letter from Lunenburg

The letter from her grandfather had come down with the post-rider on the second Tuesday of October 1793 and had sat on the writing-desk in the parlor for three days and the half of a fourth before Mary Baker had come to it. She had seen the seal on the morning it came in — her grandfather’s wax stamp, the long fold he folded letters in, the small clear superscription in his hand on the outer face — and she had set it on the desk to the right of the inkwell, in the place she set letters from Lunenburg that wanted the proper hour for their reading, and she had gone on with the morning.

The morning of that Tuesday had been the cellar — the cabbage carried from the kitchen yard and packed in the straw by the north wall with Old Hetty and Sarah and Nancy, the afternoon then at the parlor desk on the accounts of the cleared field. Wednesday had been the cloth, the last of the linen wove off the loom in the back room a fortnight earlier, the bleaching done, the cutting at the parlor table for the small ones’ shifts for the coming year. Thursday had been the kitchen and the bread and the children. The letter had sat on the desk through the three days. She had walked past it half a dozen times. She had not opened it.

She came to it on the Friday evening.

The supper had been cleared by six. Old Hetty had taken the small ones up. Sarah was at her sewing on the parlor settle with the lamp on the table beside her; Mildred Baker and Nancy were in the back room with the wool. Gideon was at the desk under the south window of the parlor with the cleared-field accounts open in the ledger and the inkwell to his right hand. Mary Baker had sat down in her own chair at her writing-desk on the north side of the parlor, with the lamp on the desk’s right-hand corner trimmed for the evening reading, and she had taken her grandfather’s letter from the place on the desk where it had sat the three days and a half. She had broken the seal.

It was a long letter. Four sheets, written close, in the hand her grandfather kept for the things that wanted thinking about — not the formal hand he wrote the Bible entries in, but the parlor hand he wrote the household letters in, the one she had been reading since she had been old enough to read at her mother’s knee. The date across the top of the first sheet was the second of October 1793. Eight days on the post-route between Lunenburg and Wentworth in October was about right.

She read.

The first sheet was the household at Lunenburg. Her grandfather was sixty-nine. His right hand was the right hand still — the ache he had reported in the December of 1787 was the ache he reported now, no worse and no better. The bay mare he had bought at the Petersburg horse fair in the spring of 1792 was foaling in November; he expected the colt would be a good one. The wheat in the long lot had come in well, and the price at Petersburg in September had been better than the September before. He was wintering at Lunenburg as he wintered most years now, with the Bible on the parlor desk and the two newspapers and the small Latin Cicero his own grandfather Christopher had given him at his fifteenth birthday in 1739. The Cicero is now eighty-five years old by my count, he wrote, and I read it as a man of sixty-nine reads what was given to a boy of fifteen, which is to say with the same eye and a different ear. I do not know if I have arrived at the Cicero my grandfather meant me to arrive at. I read it again every winter and I wait to see.

Mary Baker held that sentence a moment with her hand flat on the page.

She had read the Cicero. Her grandfather had given her her own copy on her fourteenth birthday at Lunenburg, in the autumn of 1779. She had it in the parlor desk’s left drawer behind the ledger. She had read it before her marriage and once at twenty in the year Sarah came, and not since.

She went on.

The second sheet was the news from France.

Her grandfather had written it across the page in the close hand he wrote the long passages in, with French phrases at the sentence-turns. He had had the news from the Petersburg papers across the spring and summer. The execution of the French king at Paris on the twenty-first of January had reached the American papers in the last week of March; her grandfather had read it in the Petersburg paper of the third of April and had read the longer account in the Gazette of the tenth, et j’ai pleuré sur la page — and I wept upon the page — a sentence he had not written to her in a letter before, and which she read twice.

He had written across the spring of the citoyen Genêt’s arrival at Charleston in April and his progress north to Philadelphia by the middle of May. He had read the Aurora and the Gazette of the United States on the question of American neutrality across the summer. The President’s proclamation of the twenty-second of April was in the papers; the Genêt commissions and the privateers at the Carolina ports and the recall the French government had ordered in August were in the papers; the Convention’s measures at Paris and the Committee put up at the beginning of June were in the papers. La République française, he wrote, est devenue ce que je n’avais pas su qu’elle deviendrait. Le grand espoir de quatre-vingt-neuf s’est trouvé être quelque chose de plus dur. — The French republic has become what I had not known it would become. The great hope of eighty-nine has turned out to be something harder.

He had a paragraph then on the long memory.

He wrote that his grandfather Christopher had spoken French at the writing-desk at Williamsburg in the years 1713 and 1714, and that the French Christopher had spoken had been the French of Vevey and of the Pays de Vaud — the Bernese country south of the lake — and that the French Tscharner was writing tonight at the parlor desk at Lunenburg was the same French, brought down across four generations and across an ocean, et je le sens à présent comme une chose qui ne m’appartient pas tout à fait — and I feel it now as a thing that does not quite belong to me. The men at Paris had made a new word for citizen and a new calendar and a new measure for the year, and the old French of the Pays de Vaud was a language now of fewer mouths in fewer rooms a long way from Paris. He wrote that he had not thought, as a boy of fifteen reading his grandfather’s Cicero, that he would live to see the French language go into the place it had gone into across this year.

Mary Baker stopped at the bottom of the second sheet.

She read the paragraph again.

The lamp on the corner of the desk was burning the steady yellow flame it gave when Old Hetty had trimmed it in the afternoon. The parlor was quiet. Sarah on the settle had her needle through the worked letter on the small one’s cap and was watching her mother read without saying anything. Gideon at the south-window desk was looking at the ledger column in front of him and was not looking up.

Mary Baker turned to the third sheet.

The third sheet was the news from Philadelphia.

The yellow fever had come in at the Arch Street wharf in the first week of August. The federal government had evacuated the city in the first week of September; the President had gone to Mount Vernon. The dead by the close of September were said to be above three thousand. The autumn rains had come in the last week of September and the cold had been expected to break the contagion in October. Her grandfather had a clerk’s son who was a clerk himself now at Philadelphia in a counting-house on Front Street and had not had word from him since the letter the boy had sent in the last week of July. He did not know if the boy was living. He expected he would know by the close of October.

Quant à moi, he wrote then, in the close hand he wrote the personal things in — and Mary Baker read the sentence at the close of the third sheet with her hand on the page and the lamp on her right hand — quant à moi, je me porte bien. — As for me, I am well.

She read the line.

She read it once and once again. The French was the same French her grandfather had been writing her at the sentence-turns since she had been a girl. Je me porte bien was the formula he had been signing his letters off with for forty years. The formula was the household’s. The formula was the formula.

She read on.

The fourth sheet was the household news her grandfather did not put in the formal places. Vincent at Halifax was well; his Martha was carrying a second child for the spring; the boy at three and a half was talking. Her mother on the east place at Lunenburg was well, was wintering with the older sister and the younger boys, was sending her love. The Macartys had had a daughter in August. The Pleasants girl, now Mrs. Macarty, had been at Lunenburg in September with the child. The Bible was on the parlor desk where it had been for fifty years; her grandfather had made the entries through the September ones and would make the October entries at the close of the month. He closed: Ta lettre du mois de juillet est arrivée au commencement de septembre et m’a fait plaisir comme tes lettres me font toujours plaisir. Écris-moi de la maison à Rockingham. Ton grand-père qui t’aime.

Your letter of July arrived at the beginning of September and gave me pleasure as your letters always give me pleasure. Write me of the household at Rockingham. Your grandfather who loves you.

The signature was Tscharner DeGraffenreid, in the full formal name he signed his letters to her with, with the small flourish on the right of the d the household had carried for five generations.

She folded the four sheets along their original folds. She set the letter on the desk to her left.

She sat at the desk a moment with her hands flat on the desk-top and the lamp on her right hand burning its steady yellow flame.

She thought about the cleared field.

She thought that Gideon had spoken at the supper of the south rail and of the chestnut blowing in the lot on the hard north wind that had come in across the Wednesday, and that the rail at the south end of the cleared eighty was going to want the work in the next ten days before the ground hardened. She thought that the rail was a half-day’s work for two men and that Old Hetty’s grandson Daniel was at his uncle’s place at the river through the close of October and that Gideon could have the Whitaker boy across the line for the half-day on the strength of the corn-crib labor he had had off them in September. She thought that the rail-mending could be done before the cold came in if she said so to Gideon in the morning, and she would say so.

She thought about Sarah at the settle.

Sarah was twelve years and two days. Her birthday had been the Wednesday past, the ninth of October. She had been put on the carding at six and at the plain sewing at nine and at the better needlework across her eleventh winter and was now beginning the more particular hand-work her own mother had begun at twelve. Mary Baker had had her at the worked letter on the small one’s cap for three weeks. Sarah was carrying the work steadily and a little ahead of her year. The accounts-book entries for the household preserves Mary Baker had begun letting Sarah write across the September were in Sarah’s hand now in the right-hand column, beside Mary Baker’s left-hand totals. Sarah did not yet know she had been put to the second hand of the household ledger. Mary Baker had not said so. The doing was the mother’s putting her there; the saying would be the daughter’s coming to it.

She thought about the letter on the desk to her left.

She thought that her grandfather at sixty-nine wrote the French at the sentence-turns the way he had written it across the forty years she had had letters from him, and that the je me porte bien was the formula, and that she would write back. She would write tonight, and she would write him of the cellar at Rockingham and of the linen off the loom and of Sarah at twelve at the better needlework and of Polly Baker at three and a half who had begun in September to ask after her grandfather she had not met. She would write of the cleared field and the south rail and the chestnut and the winter coming on. She would write him of the French news as she had it from the Petersburg Gazette and of her own thought on the question of American neutrality, which her grandfather had asked her for and which she now had a position to offer.

She sat at the desk a moment longer.

She uncapped the inkwell.

She took up the quill.

She wrote the date across the top of the first sheet — Friday 11 October 1793, Rockingham County — in the small clear hand she used for the letters to Lunenburg. She wrote: Grandfather.

She wrote the body of the letter in the unhurried hand she wrote her grandfather’s letters in, with the household first and the country after. She wrote that the four older girls were well and at their winter work; that Sarah at twelve was at the better needlework; that Mildred Baker at ten and a half was at the plain sewing; that Nancy was eight in November and could read aloud across a page from the primer; that Ursula at three and three quarters was learning her letters at the desk in the mornings; that Polly Baker at twenty months was beginning to ask after her grandfather she had not met. She wrote that the cleared field had come in well, the corn-crib full, the chestnut blowing on the north wind across the week. She wrote of the cellar and the linen and the rail at the south end. She wrote that Old Hetty was the same Old Hetty.

She wrote then of the country.

She wrote that the news from France had come in to Wentworth in the last week of March and that the household had had the Gazette of the Wednesday afternoon. She wrote that Gideon had read the king’s death across the parlor table at the supper. She wrote that the talk at the cleared field’s south rail and at the Wentworth court-day in April had been the talk of men who did not yet know what to think but knew they did not want what was coming if it came on the wind from the east. She wrote that her own thought, since her grandfather had asked her for it, was that the country at Rockingham did not want a French war, did not want a war of any kind, was tired in the bone of war. She wrote that the President’s proclamation of April had been read at Wentworth with the relief of men who had hoped someone in Philadelphia would say the thing they could not yet say themselves; she did not know if it was the right proclamation but she knew the country was glad of it.

She wrote of the yellow fever then, in two or three sentences, in the way she would write of a contagion at a distance — that the household had had the news of Philadelphia in September from Gideon at the court-day; that the count of the dead had been heavy; that her grandfather’s clerk’s son she would hold in her thought until her grandfather had word; that the autumn rains were said to be breaking the contagion now and she hoped it was so.

She wrote then of the Bible.

She wrote that she had thought across the September of her grandfather at the Bible on his parlor desk, and of the entries he had been making for fifty years now, and of the je me porte bien at the close of his letters across the forty years she had read them. She wrote that she had begun, in the small evenings of the September, to write her own letters to Sarah and Mildred Baker — letters they did not yet know she was writing, letters she was keeping in the parlor desk’s left drawer behind the ledger for her daughters to read when they were of an age her grandfather had been to her. She wrote that the practice had come from his letters across her own years, and that the keeping of letters was a thing she had learned at his desk by reading the letters he had written to her mother.

She wrote then the two words.

She wrote them as she had written them on the half-page beneath her keeping-letter on the ninth of January 1788, in the small clear hand of the body of the letter, in lowercase and without a period, on the line beneath the close of the letter’s body and above the formal close:

we continue

She wrote her name below it in the formal hand she used for the close of the letters to her grandfather:

Your granddaughter, Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson

She blotted the page with the flat of sand. She folded the sheets — three of them, written close on both sides — once long-ways and twice cross-ways the way her grandfather folded the letters he sent her and the way she had folded the letters she sent him for six years. She sealed the long edge with the small wax stamp Gideon had had cut for her at Petersburg in their second year. She set the sealed letter on the corner of the desk for the Tuesday post.

She did not look at the two words again.

She did not think about them as she sealed the letter or as she set it on the corner of the desk.

She thought about the south rail and the chestnut and the half-day’s work she would speak to Gideon about in the morning. She thought that the cleared field’s winter readiness was the household’s, and that the cleared field had had a good summer, and that the corn-crib at the north side of the kitchen-yard was full to the second beam, and that the household would carry through the winter without the strain of the winter of ‘88. She thought that the cabbage in the cellar was in good straw and the linen at the parlor table was cut to a year’s shifts for the small ones and that Sarah at twelve was at the better needlework.

She capped the inkwell.

She laid the quill across the inkwell.

She sat at the desk a moment with her hand on the folded letter and the lamp on her right hand burning down to the line.

The parlor was quiet. Sarah had taken her sewing up to the small table by the parlor settle and had gone to the back room to put up the wool. Gideon had closed the ledger and had gone to the kitchen for the water-jug. The fire in the grate had banked itself down to the bed of coals a piedmont fire banked itself to in the second hour without wood.

Mary Baker stood from the desk.

She blew out the lamp.

The parlor went to the red of the coals and the small light of the kitchen lamp coming through the doorway.

She stood at the desk a moment longer in the new light. She could see the corner of the desk where the sealed letter sat. She could see the parlor settle where Sarah had been at her sewing through the reading and the writing of the letter.

She went to the kitchen for the water-jug herself.

Gideon was at the table with the water and a small piece of the bread Old Hetty had set aside from the supper. He looked up when she came in.

He said: The letter from your grandfather.

She said: The letter from my grandfather. He is well at Lunenburg. The news from France has been heavy on him; the yellow fever at Philadelphia has been heavy on him too. He has a clerk’s son in the city he has not had word from since July.

He said: I am sorry for the boy.

She said: He will have word by the close of October. The contagion is said to be breaking.

He said: And your grandfather himself.

She said: He is well.

She said it the way she had said the household’s formulas across thirteen years of supper-table marriage, which was to say plainly and at the supper-table’s resolution. Gideon nodded. He drank from the water-jug and set it down and put his hand once on her wrist and took up the small piece of the bread.

She said: The south rail will want the work in the next ten days, Mr. Johnson. Can you have the Whitaker boy across for the half-day on the strength of the corn-crib labor you had off them in September.

He said: I had been thinking the same. I will go across in the morning.

She said: The rail before the ground hardens.

He said: The rail before the ground hardens.

She took a cup from the dresser-shelf and filled it from the water-jug and went back through the parlor with the cup and up the stair to the bedroom. She did not carry a candle. She knew the stair.

In the bedroom she set the cup on the small table beside the bed and undressed and got into the bed beside Polly Baker in the cradle and the small night-lamp burning low on the chimney-piece. She lay on her back a moment with her hands folded on the quilt and looked at the ceiling of the bedroom in the night-lamp’s light.

She thought about the south rail and the Whitaker boy across the line. She thought about the half-day. She thought about the chestnut in the lot and the cold close on the air and the cabbage in the cellar and Sarah’s hand in the right-hand column of the accounts-book.

She did not think about the letter on the corner of the desk in the parlor below.

She slept.

The Rider at the Gate

The afternoon of Thursday the thirteenth of February, 1794, had been the kitchen. Old Hetty had had the spring meat at the salt-table from the morning. Mary Baker had been at the salt-table with her since the breakfast-things had been cleared, with Mildred Baker carrying the cuts on the table’s far end and Nancy on the small stool by the south window watching Polly Baker who was at the rag-doll on the floor by the hearth. Sarah was in the back room at the loom on the last of the winter wool. Ursula was at the parlor desk with the slate and the letters her mother had set her in the morning. Gideon was at the south end of the cleared field with the Whitaker boy at the rail-mending the south rail had wanted across the autumn and that Gideon had held to the end of the winter on the soft week before the ground would harden again on the cold front coming in across the Saturday.

The meat was the second hog. The first hog had gone to the salt across the first week of January. The second hog had hung in the smokehouse three weeks for the cure to settle in the cold and had come down to the table on the Wednesday. The salt was the up-country salt from Halsey’s at Wentworth that Mary Baker had bought across the cold months on the standing arrangement she had had with him since 1791. The brine was on the hearth fire. The crocks were on the dresser-shelf.

She had not heard the rider come up the lane. She did not hear the horse at the gate. She heard the gate when it opened — the iron of the latch against the post the way the gate’s iron answered the post when a man’s hand opened it from the road side — and she heard the boots on the brick of the kitchen path and the boots on the back step.

She lifted her head from the salt-table.

Old Hetty had lifted her head as well.

The kitchen door opened.

Vincent stood in it.

He had his hat in his hand. He had ridden in his greatcoat with the road’s mud on the boots and the road’s cold on his face and the look of a man who had not shaved since the Tuesday. He had ridden hard.

He said:

Mary Baker.

She set the knife down on the salt-table. She wiped her hands once on her apron without looking at her hands. She looked at her brother’s face.

Vincent said: Grandfather died Tuesday evening at Lunenburg. Mother sent the boy out to me at Halifax in the night. I came up this morning. The funeral is Saturday at the meeting-house. Mother is at the house at Lunenburg with the older sister and the boys. He went in his sleep in the afternoon and they did not know it until Liza brought up the supper and found him in the chair at the parlor desk.

He did not say anything else for a count of perhaps half a minute. Mary Baker did not say anything in that half-minute either. Mildred Baker at the end of the salt-table had stopped with a cut in her two hands and was looking from her uncle to her mother and back to her uncle. Nancy on the stool had not moved.

Old Hetty said: Come into the kitchen, Mr. Vincent. You will want the fire and a cup.

Vincent came in. He set the hat on the table by the door. He took the greatcoat off and laid it across the chair-back. He went to the hearth and put his hands above the coals and stood a moment with his back to the room, and his shoulders were the shoulders of a man who had been on the road for thirty-six hours and had come to the first warmth he had had.

Mary Baker said: Mildred Baker, take Polly Baker through to the parlor. Tell Sarah in the back room her uncle is here. I will come through in a moment.

Mildred Baker set the cut down on the small board at the table’s end. She took Polly Baker up from the floor by the hearth and Nancy off the stool and went through the kitchen door into the parlor and the parlor door closed behind her.

Mary Baker stood at the salt-table a moment longer with her hands flat on the wood.

She went to the hearth.

She stood beside her brother above the coals. She put her hand on his arm above the elbow. She held it there a count of perhaps four breaths. Vincent put his own hand once on the back of her hand and took it away and put his hand back to the coals.

Old Hetty had set the cup on the dresser-shelf and was pouring from the kettle. She brought the cup to Vincent at the hearth. She said: I will have a supper for you in the half-hour. You will sleep here tonight, Mr. Vincent.

Vincent said: Yes. I will go back to Halifax tomorrow.

Mary Baker said: You will not ride to the funeral.

Vincent said: I cannot. Martha is within three weeks of the child. I told her I would be back by Saturday night.

Mary Baker said: Then I will not write to Mother to expect you. I will write to her tonight.

Vincent said: I told Mother I would come to Rockingham and tell you. I will carry your letter back to Halifax in the morning and put it up at the post there for the Tuesday rider. It will reach Lunenburg before the week is out.

She nodded.

She went to the back of the kitchen to the small water-stand and washed her hands and the apron-front in the basin. She dried her hands on the towel on the rail. She put the apron up on the peg. She came back to the table and put the knife and the salt-jar and the crocks into the order the table wanted for the supper Old Hetty would set onto it in the half-hour.

She said: I will go through. Come when the cup is done.

She went through the kitchen door into the parlor.

Sarah was at the parlor table with Mildred Baker and Nancy on the settle and Polly Baker on the floor at her mother’s chair. Ursula had come out from the desk and was standing beside Sarah at the table. Sarah was thirteen years and four months and four days old. She was at her full height now, or near enough — taller than her mother by half an inch, with her mother’s hair in the braid down her back and her father’s eyes. She had her hand on Mildred Baker’s shoulder. She looked at her mother.

Mary Baker said: Your grandfather DeGraffenreid died Tuesday evening at Lunenburg. Your uncle Vincent has ridden up to bring the word. He will go back to Halifax tomorrow. There will be a funeral Saturday at the meeting-house at Lunenburg. We will not go. The road is two days. The baby at Halifax is within three weeks and your uncle will not be at the funeral either. The Bible will come down to Rockingham in the spring, when the road and the wagon can be had together. Your grandmother will see to the sending.

Sarah said: Yes, Mother.

Mildred Baker said: Did he know he was going.

Mary Baker said: He went in his sleep at his own parlor desk in the afternoon. Liza brought up the supper and found him in the chair. I do not think he knew, Mildred Baker.

Nancy said: Will I write to him in the letter we were going to write.

Mary Baker said: No, Nancy. We will write to your grandmother instead. You may write your part of the letter to her in the morning when I have written mine.

Ursula said: Is grandfather in the ground.

Mary Baker said: He will be on Saturday, Ursula. At the meeting-house yard at Lunenburg, beside the place where your grandmother Mary Baker DeGraffenreid lies, who died in the June of 1773 a year before I was born. They will lay him there.

Polly Baker on the floor was looking at her mother and was not understanding the conversation. She reached up.

Mary Baker bent and took Polly Baker up onto her hip and stood at the parlor table with the small one on her hip. She put her free hand once on Sarah’s shoulder and once on Mildred Baker’s and once on Nancy’s. She did not put her hand on Ursula’s because Ursula was already with the others and was understanding by being there.

She said: I will be at the desk a moment. Sarah, take your sisters to the back room and the loom. Mildred Baker, come and take Polly Baker.

Mildred Baker came and took Polly Baker. Sarah took Nancy’s hand and Ursula’s and went to the back room with Mildred Baker behind them.

Mary Baker stood at the parlor table a moment longer.

She crossed to the writing-desk on the north side of the parlor. She sat down. She uncapped the inkwell. She took up the quill. She did not yet write. She sat at the desk with the quill in her hand and her hand flat on the desk-top and she looked across the parlor to the south-window desk where Gideon’s field-book was on the desk-top closed, and the inkwell to the right of the field-book, and the chair pushed in.

She thought about Gideon at the south end of the cleared field with the Whitaker boy and the rail.

She thought that Gideon would come in across the cleared field at four or half past, with the cold close on his face and the chestnut blowing in the lot, and would not yet know. She would tell him in the kitchen-yard before he came in to the children.

She thought about her mother at Lunenburg.

She thought that her mother was at her father-in-law’s parlor on the day of his death, with the older sister and the younger boys and the house to keep. She thought that her mother had buried Baker at twenty-six in some month of the war and had administered the estate for fifteen years since, and was now at the parlor desk her father-in-law had died at, with the Bible in front of her and the older sister in the kitchen.

She thought that her mother knew what had passed.

She uncapped the inkwell again — she had capped it without realizing — and took a half-sheet of foolscap from the left drawer. She wrote the date across the top — Thursday 13 February 1794, Rockingham County — in the small clear hand she used for the letters to Lunenburg. She wrote: Mother.

She wrote in the body of the letter that Vincent had come up from Halifax with the word, that he was at the kitchen now and would go back in the morning with the letter to put up at the Halifax post for the Tuesday rider, that Martha was within three weeks of the child and Vincent could not be away. She wrote that she herself could not come to the funeral, that the road was two days and the small ones were five and not yet able to be left, that she would write to her grandmother at the older sister’s place in the morning. She wrote: Mother, I have not the words for grandfather’s death. I have his letter of October on my desk and have read it twice across the autumn. I am at the desk you taught me to sit at. I will write to him no more. I will write to you.

She wrote of the keeping then.

She wrote that she would have the writing-desk on the north side of the parlor ready for the Bible when it came south. She wrote: I will keep the Bible the way he kept it. I will write the entries in the formal hand. I will write him at the close of his life, in the hand he wrote his own father in.

She wrote of the cleared field and the south rail and the salt for the meat in two sentences. She wrote that Gideon was at the rail this afternoon and that he did not yet know. She wrote: I will tell him in the kitchen-yard when he comes in from the field.

She wrote then the close:

I will write you in the morning if Vincent is still here at the kitchen by then. The post is the Tuesday rider for the next letter. I have his letter of October by me and I will keep it where I will not see it for some weeks. I am at the desk. Your daughter, Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson.

She did not write the two words.

She had not written them at any letter she had written to her mother across the fifteen years and would not write them tonight. The two words had been her grandfather’s letters and the letters were closed.

She blotted the page with the flat of sand.

She folded the sheet once long-ways and twice cross-ways. She sealed the long edge with the small wax stamp. She set the sealed letter on the corner of the desk for Vincent to take in the morning.

She capped the inkwell.

She laid the quill across the inkwell.

She sat at the desk a moment longer.

She thought about the Bible at her grandfather’s parlor desk at Lunenburg. She thought that her mother had it on the desk now, and that the older sister would have laid the cover-cloth across it the way her grandfather had laid it across at the close of his writing hour.

She did not put any of this into a sentence in her head.

She stood from the desk.

Vincent had come through from the kitchen and was on the parlor settle with the cup in his hand. He had eaten something — Old Hetty had brought him a plate in the kitchen — and was looking better than he had looked at the kitchen door. He stood when his sister stood.

He said: I went up to see her before I came in. Mother. I had a quarter of an hour with her in the kitchen at Lunenburg before I rode this morning. She is steady. She is at the parlor desk with the Bible and the older sister at her elbow. She told me to tell you the household at Lunenburg is the household at Lunenburg, that you would understand what she meant by it.

Mary Baker said: I understand it.

She did. She did not need to say what she understood. She did not say it.

She said: Sit, Vincent. The supper will be on the table in a quarter of an hour. Gideon will be in from the field. I will tell him in the kitchen before he sees the children. Sarah will bring the small ones in.

Vincent sat on the settle.

She went through to the back room. Sarah was at the loom with Mildred Baker beside her at the bobbin and Nancy on the small chair with the slate and Polly Baker on Mildred Baker’s lap. Ursula was at the small table by the window with her letters.

Sarah looked up.

Mary Baker said: Sarah. Bring them in to the supper in a quarter of an hour. Your father will be in. I will tell him in the kitchen.

Sarah said: Yes, Mother.

Mary Baker went back through to the parlor and through to the kitchen door and stood at the kitchen door a moment with her hand on the frame and listened for Gideon coming up the back step, and after a count of perhaps two minutes she heard the boots on the brick of the kitchen path and the boots on the back step. She went out to him.

She told him in the kitchen-yard, in three sentences, before he had taken his coat off.

He stood with his hat in his hand and the cold close on his face and looked at her without saying anything for the count of half a minute. Then he said: I am sorry, Mary Baker.

She said: Vincent is in the parlor. He came up from Halifax. He will go back in the morning.

He said: I will go in.

He went in past her into the kitchen and through to the parlor. She stood in the kitchen-yard a moment with her hand on the back-step rail and the cold on her face and the chestnut blowing in the south end of the lot. The afternoon was going down to the dusk. The cleared field’s south rail was finished, the new wood paler than the rail-line, the Whitaker boy already across the line on his own way home in the dusk with his coat collar up.

She looked at the cleared field a moment.

She went into the kitchen.

The supper passed. She did not eat much. Vincent ate the way a man who has ridden thirty-six hours eats. Gideon ate steadily and did not say much. The children had been told already and had no questions across the meal; Sarah carried the small ones’ attention through the meal at her own end of the table and Mildred Baker carried Polly Baker on her knee. Mary Baker sat at her place with her hands on either side of the plate and did the eating.

After the supper Old Hetty took the small ones up. Sarah and Mildred Baker went up with Old Hetty to put them to bed. Nancy stayed on the parlor settle with Ursula and the slate. Gideon and Vincent took their cups to the parlor chairs by the fire and sat across from each other and spoke at the low pitch men spoke when the day’s news had landed.

Mary Baker sat at her desk.

She did not take up the quill. She did not open the inkwell. She had written the letter she had to write and there was no other letter to write tonight. She sat at the desk with her hands folded on the desk-top and the sealed letter on the corner of the desk for Vincent and the lamp at her right hand burning steady.

She thought about her grandfather at the parlor desk at Lunenburg in the chair where Liza had found him.

She thought that he had been at the desk in the afternoon with the Bible at his right hand and the small Latin Cicero his own grandfather had given him in 1739 within reach, and the two Petersburg papers on the desk-top, and the quill in the inkwell. She thought that he had been at the desk with the writing-hand he had been keeping the family record in for fifty-two years. She thought that the chair had been the chair he had sat at for fifty years. She thought that he had laid the writing-hand down in the chair.

She thought about the October letter.

She did not take it out. She knew where it was — in the parlor desk’s left drawer behind the letters to Sarah and Mildred Baker, where she had put it on the Saturday after the writing of her reply, and where it had sat for the four months and a day. She did not need to take it out. She had read it three times across the autumn and once in the December and once in the second week of January. She knew the third sheet. She knew the close of the third sheet. She knew the formula.

The formula sat in her head a moment in the hand it had been written in, on the page she had read it on, in the lamp’s light it had been read in on the Friday evening of the eleventh of October.

She did not say anything in her head about the formula. She did not put it into a sentence. She did not think the word should. She did not think the word would. The formula sat there.

It sat there a long moment.

She heard the men at the fire. Gideon was telling Vincent of the south rail and the Whitaker boy’s labor and the chestnut blowing. Vincent was telling Gideon of Martha and the boy at four and the child coming in the spring. The men were at the fire and the woman was at the desk and the two were doing the two things the news asked of them.

She stood from the desk after a while.

She blew out the lamp.

The parlor went to the red of the coals and the fire-light on the chairs where Gideon and Vincent sat, and to the lamp by the settle where Nancy and Ursula were at the slate. Gideon looked up. Vincent looked up.

She said: I am going up. Vincent, the room across from Sarah’s is made up for you. Gideon will show you. The breakfast is at seven.

Vincent stood from the settle and looked at her.

She said: I know.

She went up the stair. She did not carry a candle. She knew the stair.

In the bedroom she undressed by the small night-lamp on the chimney-piece. Polly Baker was asleep in the cradle by the south window. The other small ones were in the front room with Sarah. She got into the bed.

She lay on her back a moment with her hands folded on the quilt.

She did not think about the October letter.

She did not think about the entry her grandfather had not made for himself.

She did not think about the Bible at the parlor desk at Lunenburg or about the wagon that would come down through Hairston’s Cut in the spring.

She thought about the supper for Vincent in the morning. She thought about the letter on the corner of the desk for him to carry. She thought about Gideon at the south rail tomorrow and the salt at the second hog and Sarah at the loom and Polly Baker in the cradle beside her.

She thought about the next thing on the list.

She slept.

We Continue

The Bible came south on the second Tuesday of April, 1794.

Mother had arranged the wagon at Lunenburg through Halsey at Wentworth, who carried the up-country salt down the post-road to Rockingham across the cold months and who had a man named Coker going south with a load of seed-corn and bar-iron in the first dry week of the spring. The roads had held wet through the end of March; the Dan had been up over the lower ford the last week of the month and had come down to its banks in the first days of April; and Coker’s wagon had come over the firmed road through Hairston’s Cut on the Monday and had lain the night at the Wentworth store, and had come up the lane to Rockingham at the middle of the Tuesday afternoon.

Mary Baker heard the wagon at the gate.

She was at the salt-table in the kitchen with the last of the second hog and Old Hetty beside her. She had been listening for the wagon since the Saturday, when the word had come down with the post that Coker was on the road; and she had told herself across the three days that she would not listen for it, and had listened for it. She heard the wheels on the lane and the wheels at the gate and the man’s voice to the team, and she set the knife down on the salt-table and wiped her hands on her apron without looking at her hands.

She went out to the wagon.

Coker had the Bible in a wooden case in the bed of the wagon, set up on the seed-sacks where the wet would not reach it, wrapped in a piece of oiled canvas over the cover-cloth Mother had laid across it at Lunenburg. There was the case, and there was a smaller parcel beside it wrapped in the same canvas, which Mary Baker would find, when she opened it that evening, to be the small Latin Cicero her grandfather had read every winter for the fifty-four years he had owned it — sent south now with a slip of Mother’s writing folded into the front of it: He would have wanted you to have it by you. S.V.D.

Coker handed the case down to her over the wagon-rail.

She took it in both arms.

She carried the case into the parlor.

She set it on the writing-desk on the north side of the parlor — the desk she had been writing her letters to Lunenburg at for fifteen years, the desk she had told her mother in the February letter she would have ready for the Bible when it came south — and she did not open the case. She stood beside the desk with her hand flat on the top of the case a moment. Then she went back out to the wagon, and gave Coker the supper and the bed in the loft over the lean-to that a man who had carried the family’s keeping two days down the post-road was owed, and saw to the team with the Whitaker boy, and went back to the kitchen and the salt-table and the last of the second hog.

The Bible sat on the desk in its case through the supper and the evening and the children’s bedtime.

She did not open it that night.

· · ·

The household kept its spring across the days after.

Gideon and the Whitaker boy and the man Gideon had taken on for the season put the corn in the cleared field across the second week of April, the field that had been four acres when she had married and was eleven acres now, the south rail finished and pale against the rail-line where the Whitaker boy had mended it in the February. Sarah was at the last of the winter wool and then at the garden-rows with Mildred Baker. Nancy had her letters in the morning at the slate and her half of the garden-row in the afternoon. Ursula was at the back-room table with the small carved animals Tscharner had whittled for the children across the years, which she had taken to keeping in a box and setting out on the table in their order. Polly Baker was five now and was at the rag-doll and the kitchen-step and the hem of whoever’s gown was nearest.

The letters had gone north and had come south across the late winter. Mary Baker had written her mother in the February and again at the close of the month and again at the middle of March. Mother had written back in March — steady, the older sister at her elbow, the household at Lunenburg the household at Lunenburg — and had written that the wagon would come down with Coker in the first dry week, and that she had laid the cover-cloth across the Bible herself, and that Mary Baker was to keep it the way her grandfather had kept it, which Mother did not need to write and wrote.

The Bible sat in its case on the writing-desk through the spring work.

Mary Baker did not write the death entry in the days after the Bible came.

She had thought, in the February, that she would write it the first night the Bible was under her roof. She had written her mother that she would. But the Bible came, and the spring came with it, and the corn wanted putting in and the garden wanted the rows turned and the household wanted its keeping, and the entry sat ahead of her the way the salt sat ahead of her at the second hog — a thing the household owed, that would be done in its hour, that was not yet at its hour. She did not name to herself that she was not ready. She did not think the word ready. She thought of the entry as the next thing but one on the list, and she did the things ahead of it on the list, and the case sat on the desk with the cover-cloth folded inside it under the lid.

She opened the case on the Thursday.

She did it in the afternoon, with the children at the garden and Old Hetty at the kitchen, alone in the parlor with the spring light coming in at the south window. She lifted the lid. She took out the cover-cloth and laid it across the back of her chair. She lifted the Bible out of the case in both hands and set it on the desk.

It was heavier than she had remembered it.

She had held it last in the autumn of 1779, on the morning of her wedding, when her grandfather had brought it to the parlor of her mother’s borrowed house and had opened it on the parlor desk for the marriage and had carried it home to Lunenburg after. She had not held it in the fifteen years since. She had seen it at the Lunenburg parlor desk on the visits — three visits across the fifteen years, the last in the autumn of 1791 — but had not held it. Her grandfather had held it. It had been her grandfather’s to hold.

She set it on the desk and did not open it.

She stood beside the desk with her two hands flat on the closed cover a moment, the way she had stood beside the case the day the wagon came. The cover was the calf-leather it had been, darkened at the corners and along the spine to the color of old harness where four generations of hands had held it. The clasp was the brass clasp. She did not work the clasp.

She put the cover-cloth over it and went back to the kitchen.

She had decided, standing there with her hands on the cover, that she would write the entry that night, after the household was up to bed. She had not said it to herself in a sentence. Her hands had gone to the cover-cloth and had laid it over the Bible the way a thing is covered that is to be come back to in a fixed hour, and she had known by the laying of it that the hour was that night, and the knowing had come up after the hands had moved, the way the knowing came up after the hands moved at the salt-table and at the loom and at the children’s hurts. She would write it tonight. She had decided it with her hands and her mind had read the decision off her hands and had agreed with it.

She went and turned the garden-rows with Sarah until the supper.

· · ·

The evening came on like the other evenings of the planting week.

The supper passed. Gideon had been in the field to the last light and ate the way a man eats at the close of a planting day and went to the parlor fire after with the field-book to set down the day’s rows. The children were put up. Old Hetty took the small ones; Sarah and Mildred Baker went up after; Nancy and Ursula were at the slate on the settle for the half-hour and then went up. Mary Baker sat at the writing-desk with the lamp on the right-hand corner trimmed to the evening’s height and the Bible under its cover-cloth at the center of the desk, and she did the mending in her lap that she did at the lamp on the evenings the household had its quiet, and she did not work the clasp of the Bible while Gideon was at the fire.

Gideon set the field-book down at half past nine and banked the fire and stood.

He looked at his wife at the desk, and at the covered shape at the center of the desk, and did not say anything about it. He had not said anything about it across the three weeks it had sat in its case. He knew what it was and whose keeping it had come into and that the keeping was hers and was not his to put a word to. He had married into the DeGraffenreid keeping at twenty in 1779 and had carried it the way a man carries the thing his wife carries — at her elbow, with the wood split and the field cleared and the road’s news brought in, and the keeping itself left in her hands where it was hers.

He said: I am going up. Do not sit too late, Mary Baker.

She said: I will not be long.

He went up the stair.

She sat at the desk and listened to his step on the stair and the door of the bedroom and the house going quiet above her. She heard the small night-noises of a house with seven people asleep in it — the timber settling at the cool of the night, the chestnut blowing once in the south end of the lot, the kitchen lamp Old Hetty left low going out at some hour she did not mark.

She did not write the entry then.

She had thought she would write it after Gideon went up, in the first quiet of the house. But she sat at the desk with the lamp on her right hand and the Bible under its cloth and the mending finished and folded in her lap, and she did not lift the cloth, and she understood after a while that she had not yet come to the hour either. The hour was not the lamp-hour with the house just gone quiet. The hour was later, in the small hours, when the night had gone all the way down and there was nothing in the house awake but her. She had known her grandfather wrote the formal entries in the small hours. He had told her so once, on a visit, at the parlor desk at Lunenburg — that the formal entries were not day-writing or evening-writing, that they were the writing a man did when the house was asleep and the entry was between him and the page and no one else, and that she would understand it when she came to keep the record herself.

She had not understood it then. She understood it now.

She trimmed the lamp down. She did not blow it out. She left it at the low flame at the right-hand corner of the desk. She set the folded mending in the workbasket. She put the cover-cloth straight over the Bible.

She went up the stair to the bedroom.

· · ·

In the bedroom she undressed by the small night-lamp on the chimney-piece. Polly Baker was asleep in the cradle by the south window. Gideon was asleep on the far side of the bed, on his back, the planting-day’s work taking him down into the sleep a field-day takes a man down into. The other children were in the front room. The house was at the bottom of its night.

She did not get into the bed.

She sat on the edge of the bed in her shift with her feet on the floor and her hands folded in her lap, the way she sat the small while she sat most nights before she lay down, when the day was being set down and the next day was being taken up. The floor was cool under her feet. The night-lamp on the chimney-piece gave the small low light it gave. Gideon’s breathing was the long breathing of a man asleep. Polly Baker’s breathing was the small quick breathing of a child asleep in a cradle.

She sat at the edge of the bed and did not lie down.

She was not ready for the day to end.

She knew, sitting there with her feet on the cool floor, what the ending of the day was. The day that was ending was the last day in which the Bible at the desk below her was a thing she had not yet written in — the last day in which the keeping had come to her but had not been taken up by her hand. The next day, in the small hours of it, she would go down and write the formal French entry in her grandfather’s manner in her own hand, and would become, at the writing of it, the keeper of the record in act and not only in the wagon’s delivery of the case. The day she had lived through had made her the keeper. The day that came after the feet left the floor was the day she became the keeper in the doing.

She sat at the edge of the bed a moment longer with the day held at its edge.

The day does not end until the feet leave the floor and rest on the bed.

She let her feet leave the floor.

She lay down. She drew the quilt up. She lay on her back with her hands folded on the quilt and Gideon’s breathing on her right and the cradle’s breathing at the window and the night-lamp low on the chimney-piece, and she understood, lying there, that she had let the day end, and that the next time she rose it would be to go down to the desk; and the understanding came up after the lying-down, the way it came up after the hands moved, the body having ended the day before the mind had said the day was ended.

She slept a few hours.

· · ·

She woke in the small hours.

She did not know the exact hour; the house had no clock that struck in the bedroom, and the window was the dead black of the deep night, the hour with no edge of dawn in it. She woke the way she had told her body to wake, the way her mother had taught her to wake to a sick child or a banked fire that wanted tending — all at once, awake, with the thing she had woken for already in her hand before her eyes were open. She had woken to go down to the desk.

She got up without waking Gideon. She took the wool wrapper from the chair at the foot of the bed and put it over her shift. She took the candle from the chimney-piece and lit it at the night-lamp and went out onto the stair-head and down the stair, the candle in her hand throwing the stair-rail’s shadow up the wall and bringing it down again behind her as she came.

The parlor was cold. The fire was a bed of grey ash with no red left in it. She set the candle on the writing-desk on the right-hand corner where the lamp stood in the evenings, and she sat down in her chair at the desk, and she took the cover-cloth off the Bible and folded it and laid it on the back of the chair beside her.

She worked the brass clasp.

She opened the Bible to the front, to the fly-leaf and the leaves of the family record after it, where the entries ran from her great-grandfather Christopher’s hand at the top of the first leaf down through the generations in the hands that had kept the record after him. She knew the leaves. She had read them as a child at Lunenburg, on her grandfather’s knee at the parlor desk, the French entries her grandfather read out to her and turned into English for her, born, married, died, the dates in the old style and the new, the names she had the whole shape of the family in before she could read the French herself.

She found the place for the entry.

It was the foot of the leaf her grandfather had kept the deaths on, beneath the entry he had made for his own father Christopher in October 1742, beneath the entry he had made for his wife Mary Baker in June 1773, beneath the entries for the children of the line who had died across his fifty-two years of keeping. There was room at the foot of the leaf for one more entry. Her grandfather had left the room. She understood, looking at the room left at the foot of the leaf, that he had left it knowing the entry that would go there would be his own and would be made in another hand, and that the hand he had left the room for was a hand he had taught to write at the parlor desk at Lunenburg a long time ago.

She uncapped the inkwell.

She took up the quill and trimmed the nib with the small knife from the desk-drawer and dipped the quill and held it a moment over the well to let the first heavy drop go back.

She wrote the entry.

She wrote it in French, in the formal hand her grandfather had written the formal entries in, in the French he had taught her at the parlor desk at Lunenburg — the French of the Pays de Vaud brought down across four generations, the French he had written her in his letters, the French he had written his own father’s death entry in fifty-two years before in this same hand at the top of this same leaf. She wrote the day and she wrote the name and she wrote the age, seventy-one years and two months, and she wrote the formula for the close of a life that the entries on the leaf had carried since Christopher’s hand had carried it: that he had been called, that he had gone, that the line stood. She wrote it the way he had written it for his father and the way Christopher had written it before him. She wrote it in the hand he wrote his own father in, the way she had told her mother in the February she would.

(Sarah Johnson — Sarah Hubbard, at the kitchen table in her own house in Cumberland County, North Carolina, with the candle at her hand and her own children long asleep and the slow careful letters coming off the nib in the hand her mother had taught her — would write this same formula at the foot of this same leaf, for her mother, at some January of 1823 in a country her mother would by then have moved away into and died in; and would understand, then and not before, writing the death of the woman who had taught her the writing, what her mother had been doing at the desk at Rockingham in the spring of 1794 with the candle burning down, taking up the hand that had been her own grandfather’s, and would feel it come up in her after the hand had moved, the way her mother had taught her that the knowing comes up after the hand.)

She signed the entry.

She set the quill down across the mouth of the inkwell.

She read what she had written. The entry stood as the entries above it stood. The hand was the formal hand, and the French was his French, the French of the leaf. She had written it the way he had kept it, and the keeping was kept, and the record had not broken at his death but had carried across into the hand he had left the room for.

She sat at the desk with the entry written and the candle burning down and the parlor cold around her and the house asleep above her.

She had done the thing the keeping asked of her.

She thought of her grandfather at the parlor desk at Lunenburg with the Bible at his right hand and the Cicero within reach and the formal entries done in the small hours across fifty-two years. She thought that the keeping he had handed her was kept. She thought of the next morning — of the letter she would write to her mother to say the Bible was come and the entry was made and the keeping was hers and kept; of the letter she would write to Vincent at Halifax; of the corn in the cleared field and the garden-rows and the small ones at the breakfast. She thought of the next morning, and that was as far as the thinking went.

She took up the quill again to cap the inkwell.

She held it a moment over the page.

She was not thinking, in the moment she held the quill over the page, of writing anything more. The entry was made. The keeping was kept. There was nothing more the record asked of her tonight. She held the quill over the page in the small motionless moment a hand holds before it is set down, and the candle was low at the corner of the desk, and the parlor was cold, and the entry sat finished on the leaf beneath the long line of the hands that had kept it.

Ink ran from the nib.

The blot landed on the fly-leaf beneath the formal entry, a single dark mark the shape of an irregular oval, smaller than her thumbnail.

She looked at it.

She dipped the quill again.

She wrote, in her own small clear hand, in English, in the same hand that had signed letters to her mother and entries in the household ledger and notes to her brother during the war for fifteen years —

we continue

She closed the Bible.

She blew out the candle.

Interlude — The Keeping Forward

The fly-leaf of the Christopher VI Bible carried, by the spring of 1794, eighty years of the family’s hands. Christopher had written the marriage entry on it in French in the small upper room at Charleston on the wedding night of the twenty-second of February 1714, in the hand the Baron at Worb had told him to write it in. His son Tscharner had kept the record after him from October 1742, when the Bible came to Lunenburg in a young man’s trunk, through fifty-two years of marriages and births and deaths set down in the same formal French at the parlor desk. The leaf Mary Baker opened to in the small hours of an April night in 1794 was the leaf those two hands had filled. The entry she wrote at the foot of it, for her grandfather, was the first entry in the Bible made by a woman’s hand since Barbara — and the two English words she wrote beneath the blot were the first words in any language not French to go onto the family record’s leaves at all.

What the surviving record establishes of the Bible after that night it establishes thinly. Tscharner DeGraffenreid died in the second week of February 1794 at Lunenburg, at seventy-one, the cause and the exact day unrecorded; he was buried in the family ground beside the elder Mary Baker, who had gone before him in June 1773. The Bible passed south to his granddaughter — not by a documented instrument, for the documentary basis of the transfer is the structural fact of the family rather than a deed or a will, but by the plain circumstance that Baker DeGraffenreid was twenty years dead with no grave, and his daughter was the one who would keep the record now. She kept it at Rockingham for sixteen years.

By 1810 the household had moved again — west and south into the new country, to Williamson County in the part of the western lands that had become the state of Tennessee in 1796, the Bible going with the family as it had gone with Tscharner to Lunenburg and with Christopher across the ocean before that. Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson died there in the January of 1823, at fifty-eight; the family records disagree by six days on the date, the Chadwell Bible giving the first of the month and the Reynolds line the seventh. Her own death entry was written, as her grandfather’s had been written by her, in another hand of the line.

The two words she wrote on the fly-leaf in the spring of 1794 were not invented for the Bible. She had been writing them at the close of letters and ledgers and journals her whole adult life, the way her mother had written them, the way the household had said of itself across the wars and the absences and the prices paid one fragment at a time that it was still, against the evidence of any given winter, standing. That she wrote them in the Bible only once, and only there, and only beneath the entry that made her the keeper, is the reason the page can carry the weight it carries. The line went on through her children and their children into the century after, and into the volume that begins where this one ends.
