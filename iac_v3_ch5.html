The summer the mill went quiet

She knew her father was dying by the mill, before anyone said it, because the mill went quiet and stayed quiet, and a mill that had run the whole of her life did not go quiet for any small reason.

It was the second summer of the war, the August of 1862, and Mary Jane was twenty-nine. The country had gone strange in a way she had never seen it go and would spend the rest of her life measuring other troubles against. There was a town thirteen miles north that had come out of the woods in her girlhood, and armies had been fighting over it since the spring — a great battle in the early summer that they had heard as a far grumble on the north wind, days of it, like weather that would not break, and then the news coming down the ridge in pieces, thousands dead up there where the rails crossed, the town she had watched get built now a thing men killed each other to hold. The war was thirteen miles off and it was also in the house. It was in the empty places at the meeting-house where the young men had been. It was in the coffee gone and the salt gone and the cloth gone, in her mother turning last year's dresses and letting out seams and making do the way the whole country was making do. It was in the price of everything and the worth of the money, which fell and went on falling until a dollar was a thing you spent fast because tomorrow it would buy less. And it was, that August, in the quiet where the mill had been.

Reuben Rorie was seventy-one or seventy-two — the record never quite settled which, the way it never quite settled anything about her people — and he had been failing through the spring, slow at first and then not slow, and by the deep of the summer he kept to the house and then to the bed, and the wheel at the race stood still because the man who had built it and read the creek and kept the gearing true for forty years was not there to start it. Mary Jane had grown up inside the sound of that wheel. She had told herself, at the loom as a girl, that the wheel was the busiest thing on the place and went nowhere; and now the busiest thing on the place had stopped, and the silence where it had been was the loudest thing in the county to her, louder than the far guns, because it was the sound of her father going out of the world.

She helped nurse him. That was the daughter's office and she had it in full, the way she had every office the house handed the daughter who stayed. She sat with him in the close hot room with the shades drawn against the light that hurt his eyes, and she wet his lips and turned him and listened to the breath go wrong in him by degrees, and she did it with the steadiness she had first found in herself over her grandmother's body five years before and had had occasion to use more than once since. He did not say a great deal at the end. He was a man who had never said a great deal; he had built things instead. He had come off the Yadkin a young man with a trade in his hands and had made, out of raw Mississippi ground, a mill and a farm and a standing and a name — a name the clerks could never spell, but a name — and he had married a girl of fifteen and kept her fifty years and put ten children into the world with her and buried some and stood up the rest, and now he lay in the drawn room while the wheel he had built stood still, and one August day he stopped breathing, and Mary Jane, who was in the room, closed his eyes.

He died in the second summer of the war, and the war swallowed his dying the way it was swallowing everything. There was no long procession, because the young men who would have made one were gone to the fighting or dead in it. There was a burying, and the neighbors who were left came, and the Methodist man read over him, and they put him in the ground on his own place; and then the world went back to its war and forgot him, because the world had a great deal to attend to that summer and one old miller in Tishomingo County was a small thing against it.

And here is what the record made of Reuben Rorie's death, the whole of it, for a long time: nothing. Not a will proved, not an estate opened, not an inventory taken, not a line in any book of the county's that was made for the deaths of men of property. The man who had built the standing place they all lived on went out of the record the way a stone goes under water — a ring, and then the surface closing, and then nothing. The one trace of his dying that survives in any public book of that county is a single line, entered years later by a clerk who was settling somebody else's affairs entirely: in the estate of a neighbor named Morrison, in the column of debts owed to the dead man, there stands the notation Reuben Rorie decd 1862 — Reuben not as a man who died and left an estate, but as a debt on another man's ledger, a sum the neighbor's heirs might try to collect from whatever Reuben had left. That is the whole of him in the county's death-books. He built for forty years and came down, on paper, to four words in the margin of another man's accounts.

Mary Jane did not know that, that August, and would not have cared if she had. She knew only that her father was in the ground and the mill was quiet and the war was on the wind, and that the house on the ridge, which had been the fullest thing in the county when she was a girl, was down now to a handful — her mother, herself, Nancy, and the war taking the rest of the world away piece by piece. She was twenty-nine, and she had buried her grandmother and now her father, and she was still exactly what she had been at seventeen and at twenty-seven: a daughter in her father's house. Except that it was not her father's house anymore. Her father was dead. And the question of whose house it was now — a question that in most families the courts and the men would have answered, with papers and appraisers and a division among the heirs — was answered, in this one, by her mother, without a paper and without a court and without asking anyone at all.

Her mother took it.

What her mother did, and what it cost to see it

Mary Polly Rorie was fifty-eight years old the summer her husband died, and she did not give up the house, and she did not go to town, and she did not send for a lawyer or an appraiser or any of her married sons to come and settle what their father had left. She simply kept on. She got up the morning after the burying and did the work of the place, and she did it the next morning, and the morning after that, and by doing it she became, without any document ever saying so, the head of the household and the keeper of the farm — a widow of fifty-eight running a war-shrunken place on her own authority, on the plain strength of being the one who was there and would not stop.

Mary Jane watched her do it, and the watching was one of the great educations of her life, and it went into her the way the important things went into her, without a word said.

Because there was a thing her mother might have done and did not do, and Mary Jane was old enough now to see the not-doing and to understand it. A widow of property, in that country, in the ordinary run of things, went to the Probate Court. Her husband's estate was opened and administered; an appraiser walked the place and wrote down what was on it — the stock and the tools and the gear of the mill and the household goods and every other thing a man died owning — and the whole was valued and set down in a book, and the widow took her dower and the heirs took their shares, and the law, grinding slow, divided a dead man's world among the living he had left. It was how it was done. The books of that county are full of it, widow after widow, estate after estate, the appraisers' careful columns marching down the pages.

Mary Polly did none of it. She never opened her husband's estate. She never had the place appraised. She never took a dower or forced a division, and the reason, as near as it can be reached, was the plainest reason there is: she did not have to, and going to court in the middle of a war to divide a farm she was perfectly able to run herself would have been a foolishness, and Mary Polly was not a foolish woman. Her children were grown. There were no minors to force the court's hand, no small heirs whose shares the law had to protect. There was only a competent widow of fifty-eight and a farm that needed running, and so she ran it, and let the law alone, and the law — which only moves when someone makes it move — left her alone in return.

Mary Jane, watching, understood that this was strength, and understood what kind. It was not the strength of a woman who does a large brave thing on a single day. It was the other kind, the kind she came from and would spend her own life practicing — the strength of getting up every morning and doing the work in front of you and not stopping, of carrying a place on your own back through four years of war and ruin because there is no one else to carry it and it must be carried. Her mother was the standard. Mary Jane could not have put the word to it then, but she felt the fact of it: that in the worst years the county had ever seen, the woman who held that ridge together was her own mother, a widow, working, keeping the thing alive by main strength and refusal; and that this — not a wedding, not a girl's good fortune — this was what a woman of her line was, at her fullest and her best.

And Mary Jane was twenty-nine, and then thirty, and then thirty-one, and then thirty-two, and she was in it beside her mother the whole time, doing the daughter's half of the endless work, and she was watching her mother be the thing the house had never once given her the chance to be. That was the private grief folded inside the plain years, and she never spoke it and half the time did not let herself feel it: that she was thirty years old and had never had a house of her own to keep, a farm of her own to hold, a place the holding of which would have made her, too, the standard of something — that she was a grown woman of proven competence spending the strength of her best years holding up a place that was her mother's, in a life that had handed her every duty of a matron and none of the standing, and that she would go on doing it, as far as she could see, until there was no one left to do it for. She did not resent her mother for it. You could not resent Mary Polly; she was the finest thing in the county and Mary Jane knew it. She resented, if she resented anything, only the shape of her own years — and even that she mostly folded up and put away, because there was a war on and a farm to run and no profit in it.

The war ground on and the place bled the way every place bled. The news came down the ridge and it was bad and then worse. And in the summer of 1864 it came to the door in the way she would remember longest: word that Ezekiah was dead. Her brother — the boy of seventeen the first marshal had counted beside her, grown into the prosperous one, the note-holder with the two-thousand-dollar farm and the young children — had been killed in the fighting east of Atlanta, three hundred miles from the ground he was raised on, in the July of that year. And Mary Jane watched her mother receive it, and watched what her mother did with it, and what her mother did with it was go to the Bible.

The Rorie Bible sat on the shelf in the parlor where it had sat all Mary Jane's life, and her mother had kept it for forty years — the births and the marriages and the deaths of the family entered in it in Mary Polly's careful round keeping-hand, the record the family made of itself when no other record would. And when the word came about Ezekiah, Mary Polly took the book down and opened it to the deaths and wrote her son's name and the day he died and the place, in the same hand she had used to write his birth in, near thirty years before, on a page a little further up. Mary Jane was there. She saw her mother do it. And she did not know, and her mother did not know, that this would be the last thing Mary Polly ever wrote in that book — that the hand which had kept the family's record for forty years would set down her son's death and then, worn past the point a keeping-hand can bear, close the book and not take it up again; so that the deaths-page of the Rorie Bible ends there, at Ezekiah, in the summer of 1864, with everything that died in the family afterward going unwritten. But that belongs further along. What Mary Jane saw, that day, was only her mother writing her brother into the book with a steady hand, and she understood that this too was the standard — that you kept the record, that you wrote your dead down, that the keeping did not stop for grief, because the keeping was how the family held on to its own.

By the time the war ended, in the spring of 1865, Mary Polly had carried the place three years a widow and would carry it more. The farm had come through — smaller, poorer, the money worthless, the mill idle, but the ground still theirs and the household still standing, held together by an old woman's refusal to let it fall. In a year or so the heirs would at last do the one thing with the law that had to be done — not administer the personal estate, which Mary Polly never touched, but divide the land, because land held by a dead man passes to all his heirs together and cannot be run that way forever; and there would be a court petition and a commissioner and a sale of Reuben's ground for division among his children, and that sale, and who stood at it, and what it set in motion for Mary Jane herself, belongs to the next turn of this account. What belongs here, at the end of the widow's years, is the plain shape of what Mary Polly did: she took a farm no one gave her, in the worst years the country had, and she held it, and she asked no court's leave to do it.

And that plain shape, that refusal to go to law, which is the very thing that made her the standard — that is the thing this chapter has to turn and look at from the other side. Because the farm Mary Polly took and held and never carried to any court was not only ground and stock and an idle mill. There were people on it. And the same refusal that makes her the standard is the reason we do not know their names.

The three, and the reason the book cannot name them

There were three people held on the Rorie place, and they were there before the war and through the war, and they were freed off that ground in 1865, and this book owes them what it can give, which is less than they are owed and more than the record wants to allow — and it owes them, first, the plain fact that it cannot tell you their names.

Not their names, not their people, not the country they or their mothers were carried from, not the road by which they came onto Reuben Rorie's ridge, not one word any of them ever said. The record that reaches us gives them exactly this and no more: that in the late summer of 1860 the assistant marshal Porter Walker — himself a man who had held two enslaved people ten years before, no clerk observing the thing from outside it — rode up the ridge with the government's second book, the one kept for slaves, and wrote against Reuben Rorie's name three human beings, entered not by name, because that book did not ask for names, but by a number and a letter each: a woman of sixty-five, a man of thirty-six, a girl of seven. And beside them, in the ninth column, a figure: one slave house. A structure standing in the Rorie yard, built at some point in the decade before, in which three people lived who were counted as property and left off every page that might have said who they were.

Hold them there a moment, because it is nearly all we have. A woman of sixty-five in 1860 — born, then, around 1795, in the last years of the old century, which means she had been alive through the whole history of the thing that held her, and had very likely been carried or sold more than once to arrive, old, on a ridge in north Mississippi in a house not her own. A man of thirty-six — born around 1824, in his full strength, the labor of the place very likely resting on his back. And a girl of seven — born around 1853, on that ground or brought to it as an infant, a child who had known nothing in her life but that yard and that house and the family that owned the ground she was born on. Three people, three generations near enough, one house. They had names. Of course they had names — names their own people called them by in that house in the yard, a woman's name and a man's name and a child's name, said a thousand times in the ordinary business of three lives, at the fire and in the field and in the dark. Not one of the three came down to us. The book that would tell you what Reuben Rorie was worth can tell you there were three of them and how old and what sex and what color, and it cannot tell you what to call them, because to the people who made the record they were a sum and not a set of names, and the sum is what survived.

Now here is the hard thing, the thing this chapter has to say plainly because plainness is the only honesty available: it did not have to be this way, and the reason it is this way runs straight back through Mary Polly.

Because that county did name enslaved people. It named them constantly. The very books that swallowed Reuben Rorie's death — the probate books, the inventories and appraisements and estate divisions of Tishomingo County — are full of the names of the enslaved, page after page, because when a slaveholder died and his estate was administered, the law required that every thing he owned be written down and valued, and the enslaved were written down among the things, by name and by price. The books of that county carry them by the hundred: Rhody, Jane, Betty, Milly, Jack, Eli, Dick, Caroline, Sarah, Lee, Sam, Prince — and, in one estate, in a line a person does not easily read twice, a Negro girl slave named Mary, aged seven years, valued seven hundred dollars. A girl of seven, named and priced, in the same county, in the same books, in the same years. The machinery for writing an enslaved child's name onto a permanent public page existed and ran and ran. It was a monstrous machinery — it named them in order to value them, it named them the way you name livestock in an inventory, it named them as property — but it named them, and its naming is the reason we know a great many of the enslaved people of that county by name at all.

It never ran over the three on the Rorie place. And the reason it never ran over them is that Reuben Rorie's estate was never administered — no inventory, no appraisement, no division — because his widow was a competent woman of fifty-eight with grown children and no need of the court, and she kept the farm herself and never went to law. The three were on that place in 1860 as part of the personal estate, part of the very five thousand dollars that seated the family at the front of the meeting-house. When Reuben died in 1862 they passed, in the eyes of that world, to his widow along with the rest of what he left, and Mary Polly held the place and them with it and never opened an estate — and so no appraiser ever walked the Rorie yard writing down what was in it, and the one kind of document that would have set those three names on a page was the one document Mary Polly's competence made unnecessary. She kept the farm without the court. The court is where the names would have been written. She did the thing that makes her the standard, and the thing that makes her the standard is precisely the thing that left three people nameless.

That is not an irony to be admired for its shape and it is not a paradox to be turned over for its cleverness. It is a plain hard fact and it should sit heavy. The strength that this book honors in Mary Polly Rorie — the refusal to go to law, the carrying of a place on her own back — is the same act, the identical act, that erased three people from the record more thoroughly than the county's monstrous machinery would have. Both things are true of the one woman and the one act, and the book sets both down and does not soften either, because to honor the strength and hide the cost would be to do to these three exactly what the record already did: use them, and then leave them out.

And then, in 1865, they were free.

What that day was on the Rorie ridge, no record says and this book will not invent. Freedom came to the enslaved of north Mississippi not on one clean morning but raggedly, over the last year of the war and the months after it, as the armies moved and the word spread and the old power failed — and it came to the three on the Rorie place at some point in that unfixed stretch, and one of the plainest and most enormous facts of their lives, the day they ceased to be owned, is a day this book cannot date and would not presume to picture. What can be said is only what freedom meant in that country in those months: that the road opened. That a person who had been held could go — could leave the ground they had been born on or carried to, could go to look for the people slavery had sold away from them, could go toward the places freedom was gathering. And thirteen miles north, at Corinth, freedom had been gathering for years already. All through the war, from the autumn of 1862, there had been a camp there — a contraband camp, they called it, on the east side of the ruined town, where the formerly enslaved came in from the countryside by the hundred and then the thousand, more than six thousand at its height, and built themselves a place: a settlement with a church and a hospital and streets laid out and houses numbered, with a school where children learned by day and grown people by night, with hundreds of acres worked for wages that were actually paid. It stood only thirteen miles from the Rorie yard, in the same years the three were held there, a whole town of freedom up the road from a house of bondage. Whether any of the three ever walked to it, this book does not know and will not say they did. It is set down here only because it was there — because while three people were being counted as property on Reuben Rorie's ridge, thirteen miles off there were freed people living in numbered houses and drawing wages and learning their letters, and both of those were Tishomingo County in the same year, and the book that tells the one owes the reader the other.

After freedom, the record loses them, and the losing is itself the last thing to be said. The census of 1870 was the first the United States ever took in which the formerly enslaved were counted as people, with names, in households of their own — the first document in the whole history of the country that might have told us what the three called themselves once they were free to be called it. It does not hold them. On the old Rorie ground, and on the ground where the Searcys and the rest of that neighborhood were counted in 1870, there is no household of theirs to be found — no woman near seventy-five, no man near forty-six, no young woman near seventeen, taking the name Rorie or any name traceable back to that place. They did not stay on as the family's freed neighbors, the way many freedpeople did; and the name-blind index that has never once been able to find this family cannot find them either. Where they went, whether they lived, what they made of the freedom that came to them old and grown and half-grown on that ridge, the reachable record does not say. The woman of sixty-five in 1860 would have been seventy in 1865, and a woman freed at seventy in a ruined country may not have had long, and may lie in unmarked ground within a mile of where she was held, and be past any finding. The man and the girl walked out of the record into lives this book cannot follow.

So this is the whole of the accounting, and it is thin, and its thinness is the point. Three people were held on the Rorie place: a woman, a man, a child. They were there through the war. They were freed off that ground in 1865. They had names, and the names are gone, and they are gone because the one document that would have preserved them was never made, and it was never made because the woman this book calls the standard kept the farm without the court. They are not a figure in Mary Jane's story and they are not a shadow cast to deepen anyone's chapter. They are three people this account owes an accounting to, and this is it — offered in their own right, in the one place the book set aside for them, with their namelessness laid at the door of the exact act that caused it, and with no pretense that the book has given them back anything the record took. It has only refused to pretend they were not there.

They were there. A woman of sixty-five, a man of thirty-six, a girl of seven, and one house standing in the yard. The book sets them down as the record allows, which is barely, and grieves the rest, and goes on — because the chapter must go on, the way the years went on for everyone left on that ridge; but it does not go on as though the yard had been empty. It was not empty. It is only that the ones who could have told us are the ones the record was built to forget, and it forgot them well, and the most this account can do is say so, and say that it was wrong, and mean it.
