Landmarks & Monuments

Reading Russia in stone — how a thousand years of power wrote itself into buildings, from the Kremlin's walls to the Lubyanka's cells.

Architecture is the most honest record a state keeps of itself. Walk the list below and you can trace the whole arc of this guide — faith, empire, revolution, and terror — written in brick, marble, and gilded fountain. Each entry is here for what it means, not just how it looks.

The pictures will follow — for now, the stories. Each card has a slot ready for an image when we add one.

The old heart — Moscow & faith

The Moscow Kremlin

Moscow · 15th century onward

The fortified heart of Russian power for five centuries — a triangle of red-brick walls and towers begun under Ivan III with imported Italian architects, enclosing cathedrals, palaces, and, since 1918, the seat of the Soviet and then Russian state. "The Kremlin" is now simply shorthand for the government itself.

St. Basil's Cathedral

Red Square, Moscow · 1555–61

The riot of colored onion domes that says "Russia" to the world. Ivan the Terrible raised it to mark the conquest of Kazan; a legend (unproven) says he blinded the architect so it could never be equaled. Its swirling, asymmetric silhouette is like nothing in Western Europe.

Cathedral of Christ the Saviour

Moscow · 1883 · dynamited 1931 · rebuilt 1997

A parable in stone. Raised to thank God for the defeat of Napoleon, it was blown up by Stalin in 1931 to clear ground for a never-built Palace of the Soviets — the site became an open-air swimming pool — and rebuilt after the USSR fell. Faith, terror, and revival at one address.

Imperial St. Petersburg

The Winter Palace

St. Petersburg · 1754–62 (Rastrelli)

The Romanovs' vast green-and-white Baroque residence on the Neva. The storming of it in October 1917 became the revolution's founding image (helped along by Eisenstein's camera). Today it anchors the Hermitage, one of the greatest museums on earth.

Peterhof

Near St. Petersburg · from 1714

Peter the Great's answer to Versailles — a palace above the Gulf of Finland fronted by the Grand Cascade, a gilded staircase of fountains that runs without a single pump, driven entirely by gravity from distant springs. Imperial ambition rendered as landscape.

The Peter & Paul Fortress

St. Petersburg · founded 1703

The city's birthplace and its two faces of power. The cathedral within holds the tombs of nearly every Romanov tsar from Peter the Great to Nicholas II — while its cells were the empire's most feared political prison, holding, over the years, Peter's own son Alexei, Dostoevsky, Bakunin, Gorky, and Trotsky.

The Bronze Horseman

St. Petersburg · unveiled 1782

Falconet's equestrian statue of Peter the Great, commissioned by Catherine the Great, rearing atop a single colossal granite boulder. Pushkin's 1833 poem made it the symbol of the imperial state's crushing weight over the individual — the statue that comes to life and hunts its victim through the flooded city.

The Soviet stamp

Lenin's Mausoleum

Red Square, Moscow · 1930 (Shchusev)

The severe red-and-black granite step-pyramid where Lenin's embalmed body has lain on display since 1924 — a secular shrine, and the reviewing stand from which the Soviet leadership watched the Red Square parades. Communism's holy of holies.

The Moscow Metro

Moscow · from 1935

Stalin's "palaces for the people" — subway stations built as marble, mosaic, and chandelier-lit cathedrals of socialism, propaganda you descend into on your way to work. Grandeur deployed as a political argument, dug in part by the same era's forced labor.

The Seven Sisters

Moscow · late 1940s–1950s

Seven wedding-cake skyscrapers raised under Stalin to give Moscow a skyline to rival New York's — the tiered, spired style the world came to call "Stalinist Gothic," later exported to the capitals of the Eastern bloc.

The Lubyanka

Moscow · building 1898, secret police from 1919

The yellow-brick headquarters of the secret police — Cheka, OGPU, NKVD, KGB, FSB — on the square that carried Dzerzhinsky's statue until 1991. Its inner prison was the first stop for countless victims of the Terror; a bitter joke called it the tallest building in Moscow, "because you can see Siberia from the basement."

Stalin's Near Dacha (Kuntsevo)

Near Moscow · 1930s

The green-painted country house where Stalin actually lived and worked for his last two decades, hosted the all-night Politburo dinners Montefiore chronicles, and died — felled by a stroke in March 1953, as his terrified guards waited hours before daring to enter the room.

The White Sea–Baltic Canal

Karelia · 1931–33

Not a palace, but a monument all the same. A 227-km canal dug largely by hand by Gulag prisoners, at a cost of tens of thousands of lives, and trumpeted as proof that forced labor could "reforge" the enemy. A structure whose true building material was people.

And one for the ego

Tsereteli's Peter the Great

Moscow River · 1997

A 98-metre colossus of Peter astride a fantastical ship — one of the tallest statues in the world, and routinely voted one of the ugliest, with the added irony that Peter loathed Moscow. A monument less to the tsar than to the swagger of the post-Soviet 1990s and its favored sculptor, Zurab Tsereteli.
