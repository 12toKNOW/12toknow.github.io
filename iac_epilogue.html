Epilogue

987 words · about 4 min read

An afterword to all three volumes.

Mountain Brook, Alabama — 2026

I have just told you, at the end of her own volume, how Mary Jane died — the homemade coffin her sons made, the country churchyard at Pine Grove, the certificate with no doctor's name on it. I will not tell it again. Here at the very end of the three books I want to step back from her, and from all of them, and say what I have come to think the whole of it was.

She is the point, and not because she was the greatest of the people in these pages. Because she was the plainest, and the plainest is where the thing shows clearest. The priory and the baron and the grant and the pension files — the whole documented apparatus of the two volumes that come before her — are here because they arrive at her. None of the great events of the country happened to these people. All of them arrived as fragments — a casualty list, a marshal at the door, a letter in a grandfather's particular French — and the household priced each fragment and went on. That is what the family did. In England it looked like a name signed at the bottom of a herald's pedigree to keep a seat. In Virginia it looked like a girl of fifteen writing two words at the foot of a letter to a brother at the war. In Mississippi it looked like a widow keeping a farm the court never touched and a daughter watching her do it and taking it up in her turn. The costume changed every century. The act did not.

And it is one act, carried by one line, and I want to say plainly how the line runs, because it is the thing I did not know when I began and the thing I most wanted to be true. Barbara Tempest Needham came down a gangway at Charleston in February 1714 and married Christopher de Graffenried, and she came. Their grandson's daughter Mary Baker DeGraffenreid Johnson sat at a desk in Lunenburg County on a November night in 1779 and wrote we continue, and she was the first woman in the line to hold the Bible in her own hands. The keeping went on down the way it always went — sideways, through daughters, into other names: her daughter Sarah Johnson, who married a Hubbard and carried it west into Tennessee; Sarah's daughter Mary Polly Hubbard, who married a millwright named Reuben Rorie and carried a worn black-calf Bible into her son's house in Mississippi, a widow, in her own lap; and Mary Polly's seventh child was the girl at the loom. So the woman who held the line on a Mississippi ridge in 1920 was Barbara's own descendant, six generations down, and the book Mary Polly carried in her lap was the far end of the same keeping that began on a fly-leaf in a formal French no one could read. She came. We continue. She held the line. I did not arrange that. I found it. It was already there, waiting in the records for anyone who would read them all the way to the end.

There are silences in these three books that I could not close, and I have left them open on purpose. I do not know for certain where Baker DeGraffenreid died, or when; there is a date he was last seen and a date his wife signed herself his administratrix, and four years of dark between them, and I did not try to fill it. I do not know the names of the three enslaved people who were held on the Rorie farm, a woman and a man and a girl, because the estate that would have named them was never opened, and the same competence that let a widow keep a farm without the court is the reason their names are lost. Nor do I know what became of the girl Anseline, a fifth part of whom a Tennessee will handed down to a daughter of this line in 1828, a generation before those three were held on the same family's Mississippi ground — her name is in the deed, and her fate is not. I have said all of this in the volumes where these people belong, and I will not tuck it into a footnote here, because they are not anyone's metaphor and I will not use them as one. The record made those silences, or let them fall, and a family history that respects its people has to respect the holes in them too. The blank does not stay blank; somebody always fills it. My whole discipline has been to fill it only with what the page will bear, and to leave it open where the page bears nothing.

So here I am at the other end of it, holding the book — three books — the way each of them held it in turn, and understanding at last that holding it is the whole inheritance. There was never any treasure. There was never any title that outlived the man who held it. There was a Bible on a parlor desk and then a box in a closet and then a chart somebody started and did not finish, and there was always, in every generation, one person who did not throw it away. I am that person now. This is what I did with it.

To whoever breaks this open a long way off and a long time from now, and reads down the fly-leaf to the place where the entries stop and finds their own name is not yet written there: the keeping is yours now. Set the known thing down. Do not invent to fill the silence. Hold the line where you stand on it.

We continue.

— DLS
