Russia and the Bottle

Not a national failing but a four-century codependency — how the state and the bottle grew up needing each other.

You notice it because it refuses to stay in the background. Open almost any Russian book, in almost any century, and sooner or later the bottle is on the table — in the peasant's hut and the officer's mess, in Dostoevsky's slums and the Soviet kitchen, at the wedding and the wake and the ordinary grey Tuesday. It is tempting to file this under national character and move on. That would be the lazy reading, and the wrong one. The deeper story is not that Russians drank; it is that for four centuries the Russian state needed them to — that the bottle and the treasury grew up together in a codependency that outlived the tsars, outlived the commissars, and helped bury them both.

The tavern and the treasury

The dependency was built into the machinery of the state early. From the sixteenth century the crown spread the kabak — the state tavern, where the people could drink only what the state sold, and the profit flowed to the throne. By the nineteenth century the arithmetic was staggering: liquor revenue, through the monopoly and the tax on spirits, supplied at times close to a third of the entire ordinary income of the Russian state. The government was not merely tolerating drunkenness; it was manufacturing, licensing, and banking on it. Under the corrupt "tax-farm" system, private concessionaires who had bought the right to sell vodka grew rich enough that when peasants launched a wave of temperance revolts in 1858–59 — swearing off drink and smashing up taverns — the state treated sobriety itself as a threat to revenue and moved against the abstainers. Sergei Witte rebuilt the arrangement as a formal state monopoly in the 1890s. The throne needed the people drunk very nearly as much as the people reached for the bottle.

The workers' state runs on vodka

The pattern was too useful to die with the tsars. In 1914, mobilizing for the World War, Nicholas II banned the vodka trade — a genuinely bold stroke of public health that also blew a gaping hole in the budget just as the state could least afford it, and some historians count that lost revenue among the quiet fiscal wounds that weakened the regime before 1917. The Bolsheviks kept the prohibition at first, and Lenin was contemptuous of a state that would finance itself from its people's ruin. Yet by 1925 Stalin had brought the state vodka monopoly back — the bottle even earned the nickname rykovka, after the head of government — on the frank argument that the revenue was a necessary, temporary evil to help pay for industrialization. Socialism, it turned out, would be built partly on the proceeds of drink. The temporary evil became permanent, and by the Brezhnev decades Soviet consumption of spirits had climbed to among the highest in the world.

Tsar and commissar disagreed about almost everything — but not about this. Both discovered that a monopoly on vodka is one of the easiest taxes a poor state can collect, and neither could bring itself to give it up.

Gorbachev's war on drink — and the boomerang

The last Soviet leader was the one who finally tried to break the habit — and the attempt shows just how deep the dependency ran. Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign, launched in 1985, slashed production, raised prices, restricted sales, and even tore up vineyards. In pure public-health terms it worked: alcohol-related deaths fell and life expectancy ticked measurably upward for two or three years, a rare and real saving of lives. But the state had just cut off one of its own largest revenue streams in the middle of a deepening budget crisis, and the people simply turned to samogon — home-brewed moonshine — on a scale that stripped the shops of sugar. The campaign was quietly abandoned, and the hole it tore in the budget is now widely counted among the pressures that helped push the tottering Soviet economy over the edge. Alcohol had helped weaken the tsarist state in 1914 and the Soviet one in the 1980s — the same lever, pulled twice, with the same recoil.

The bottle in the books

Your original observation holds all the way down: the drink is in the literature because it was in the life. Dostoevsky gave the theme its definitive face in Crime and Punishment — the clerk Marmeladov, who drinks his family into starvation and his daughter into the street, and knows precisely what he is doing. A century later Venedikt Erofeev wrote Moscow to the End of the Line, a delirious prose-poem of a drunken train journey that became the great literary monument to Soviet alcoholism. Around these towers sits a whole vocabulary the language needed and English lacks: zapoi, the days-long bender that swallows a man whole; samogon, the still in the shed; and the rituals — the toasts raised and answered, the third of them drunk in silence for the dead. The bottle is in every period and nearly every author because it was, for most Russians in most centuries, simply there.

The reckoning

And then the bill came due, in the plainest currency there is. When the Soviet system fell and the cheap vodka flowed without restraint into a decade of poverty and despair, Russia suffered one of the great peacetime demographic catastrophes of the modern age. Male life expectancy collapsed into the high fifties by the mid-1990s — a level more associated with war than with peace — and careful studies have laid a large share of the deaths of working-age Russian men, by some estimates approaching half, at the door of alcohol. The thread that had run so cheerfully through the wedding songs and the toasts ended here, in the mortality tables. The same drink the state had sold its people for four hundred years was, in the end, killing them faster than any of its wars.

The third toast and the demographic collapse are two ends of a single thread — the conviviality and the catastrophe were never separable, because the hand that poured the drink was so often the hand of the state.

Not character, but structure

It is worth saying clearly what this essay is not arguing. There is no such thing as a people born to drink, and "the Russian soul" explains nothing here. What explains the pattern is structure: a spirits-and-binge culture rather than a wine-with-dinner one; the poverty and the long dark winters that made cheap oblivion attractive; and, above all, a state that for four centuries kept choosing revenue over sobriety and building the choice into its own budget. Plenty of nations drink; what set Russia apart was the deliberate, top-down marriage of the bottle to the treasury, sustained across every kind of regime. The tragedy was not in the character of the people. It was in the arithmetic of the state — and in how faithfully, tsar to commissar to the wreckage of the 1990s, that arithmetic held.

Further reading: Mark Lawrence Schrad, Vodka Politics: Alcohol, Autocracy, and the Secret History of the Russian State; David Christian, Living Water: Vodka and Russian Society on the Eve of Emancipation; Stephen White, Russia Goes Dry: Alcohol, State and Society; Fyodor Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment; Venedikt Erofeev, Moscow to the End of the Line. On the mortality crisis, the demographic work of Nicholas Eberstadt and the 2009 Zaridze Lancet study.

A note on certainty: the fiscal dependency, the temperance revolts, the 1914 and 1985 prohibitions, and the 1990s mortality collapse are well documented. What remains argued is degree — how much lost vodka revenue contributed to each state's fall, and what exact share of Russian male deaths to assign to alcohol (estimates run wide, and are given here as ranges). This piece rejects "national character" as an explanation on principle, not merely on evidence.
