In the early autumn of 1640, in the small estate room off the great hall of the priory at Little Wymondley, a man named Eustace Needham sat at a heavy oak table going through his Michaelmas rents.

It was the day after the feast of St Michael and All Angels, the twenty-ninth of September having been the quarter-day on which the priory’s tenants brought in the rents they owed for the second half of the agricultural year. The table he sat at had been in the room, he supposed, since his great-grandfather’s time — possibly since the Surveyor’s. A pewter ink-pot and a quill stood at his right hand. A leather ledger lay open in front of him. Beside it sat a cloth bag, half-full of coin: silver shillings from the larger tenants, copper farthings from the smaller ones, a few thin gold pieces from a man who had paid in for a full year’s tithe in advance. The window beside him faced east into the priory court. Beyond the window, the chapel that had once been the priory church stood across the cloister yard in the soft September light.

He was about forty-five years old that autumn. He had been at the priory all his life. He was the great-grandson of John Nedham who had signed the 1572 visitation; he was the great-great-grandson of James the Surveyor who had received the place from Henry VIII a hundred and two years before. He had inherited the headship of the family from his father John of Welwyn — a transfer of the headship from the cadet line to the senior line that had happened by way of a property arrangement whose details he had been hearing about all his life. He had been married twice. He had buried the first wife in the chancel of the parish church ten years before; he was now married to a Wingate cousin from Lockleys at Welwyn, with two more daughters by her in the years since. He had a son George — twenty-two years old that Michaelmas, riding the back fields somewhere with the tenant Will Champion to look at a section of hedge that needed laying before winter. He had a second son Luke, eighteen, at his books in the room upstairs. He had three daughters in the house: Lettice, from his first marriage, the eldest, already promised to a Bedfordshire gentleman; and two more, Frances and Margaret, from the second.

He was not a remarkable man. He was a Hertfordshire country gentleman of the second tier — armigerous, propertied, lord of two manors, holder of the rectory of Wymondley Parva by lay impropriation, head of a household of perhaps twenty people including the family, the senior servants, the working tenants who came up to the house on quarter-days, and the curate who took meals in the great hall during the weeks his own house was being re-roofed. He had no Court connection. He had no Privy Council relation. He had no seat in Parliament. He had never been knighted. He had no public profile to lose. He was a steady working manager of a steady working estate.

It was an ordinary morning. The harvest had been in for three weeks. The corn from his own demesne stood in the great barn — the one that had been the priory’s tithe-barn in the canons’ time, before his great-grandfather James had been given the place by King Henry. The tithes from Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire had come in the previous week, brought by a carter who had walked the team up over two days from the parsonage there. The estate was solvent. The roof of the chapel needed re-leading and Eustace had set aside the money for it. The Michaelmas rents were coming in better this year than last. By noon the ledger would be filled. By evening Eustace would know whether the year had been a good one or only an indifferent one. The signs were good.

What was not ordinary that morning was a letter.

It had come up from London the previous afternoon by a man on a tired horse and was now sitting at his right hand, half-folded, the seal already broken. It was from his cousin John in Welwyn — the younger John Needham who had inherited what was left of the Welwyn estate when Eustace’s father had died. The letter was short and businesslike and the news it carried was not.

The king had called a Parliament. Not the brief one that had been summoned in the spring and dissolved within weeks — that one had already passed into history as the Short Parliament. This was a new one. The writs had gone out at the end of September. It would sit in November. And the men who were going up to it, Eustace’s cousin wrote, were not the king’s men. They were the country’s men. There would be a reckoning over the king’s eleven years of personal government, over Ship Money, over the Scottish war, over Strafford and Laud. The cousin did not say so outright; he was not a fool and he knew that letters could be read on the road. But the implication was plain enough between the lines.

Eustace read it again. He folded it carefully and put it in the box on the table where he kept his important papers. Then he sat for a while looking at the window, at the priory court beyond it, at the chapel that had been the priory church and was now his family’s place of burial, at the wall of the cloister where his father and his grandfather were both buried under the same flag-stone with a small brass plate.

What was about to happen to the kingdom over the next eighteen years would, by any measure, be the most consequential political and military convulsion in English history between the Wars of the Roses and the Industrial Revolution. The king would be tried and executed by his own Parliament in January 1649. The monarchy would be abolished. England would become a republic for eleven years. A standing army would dictate the terms of national life. The bishops would be put down, the Church of England’s Prayer Book would be banned, the country’s parish-by-parish religious life would be reorganized under a Westminster Assembly of Presbyterian and Independent divines. The Scottish kingdom would invade England twice. The Irish would rebel. Three hundred thousand Englishmen would die — about one in twenty of the adult male population, a proportional casualty rate higher than the First World War would inflict three centuries later. The country house Eustace’s great-grandfather had received from Henry VIII would sit, at the eye of all this, a few miles from a major battlefield in 1645 and four miles from a major sequestration committee in 1644, and would have to make decisions every year about how to keep the priory intact when half the gentry families of the home counties were losing theirs.

He did not yet know any of this on the day after Michaelmas 1640. What he knew, that morning, was that the king had called a Parliament that was not going to be his, and that a long quiet patch of Hertfordshire gentry life was about to come to an end.

This chapter is the story of what he did about it.

The Welwyn cadet generation

To understand how Eustace ended up at the priory it is necessary to take one step back, into the cadet branch of the family that had been quietly maintained at Welwyn through the early seventeenth century.

When George Nedham — the heir who had signed the 1572 visitation as son and heir is 16 yeres old, and who had ridden into Kent in September 1581 to marry Margaret Stile at Beckenham — succeeded his father John in the priory at the close of the sixteenth century, he carried the senior line forward into the Stuart period. He became, in the historian’s notation, George I of Wymondley. He held the priory from his father’s death (1591 or 1592) through the long Elizabethan twilight and the Jacobean opening, until his own death at the age of seventy on 27 June 1626. The Wymondley Parva parish register preserves the burial entry verbatim, in the v9 Urwick reading: 1626. Buried George Needham, Esq., June 27th. John Marshall, minister. He was laid in the chancel of the parish church, under the same flag-stones where his father had been buried thirty-five years before and where his great-grandfather James had not been buried because James had died at Boulogne.

George I had been busy. He had been the lay impropriator of Wymondley Parva rectory from at least 1614, the year the episcopal visitation preserved the formal record of his patronage — Rectory impropriate with Geo. Headham, Esq. The minister a stipendiary. Bern. Jones, M.A. — meaning that he held the rectory’s tithes and paid Bernard Jones, M.A., out of those tithes to serve as the parish’s stipendiary curate. He had erected the 1605 monument to his father and grandfather in the chancel of the parish church, with the inscription Chauncy would copy out a hundred years later: James Needham, of the ancient Family of the Needhams in Derbyshire, came into this County of Hertford, in the year of our Lord, 1536. He was advanced by King Henry the eight, for his Service in England and France, and lyes buried in our Ladies Church in Boloigne; he had issue John, who had George, who dedicated this Monument to their Memory, Anno 1605. Beneath, in Latin: Satius est mortis quam Natalis dies — Better is the day of death than the day of birth, Ecclesiastes 7:1. The motto was characteristic of George I. He was a man who, in his late forties, with a wife buried in the chancel and a young heir growing up at the priory, had begun to think about the family’s memorial tradition.

The heir George I was preparing was his son Eustace — born about 1595, at the priory, in the Wymondley Parva chapel where he would later baptize his own children and bury his own father.

But Eustace was not George I’s only adult son. George I had three sons who survived into the seventeenth century: Eustace, the eldest, who would inherit the priory; John, the second son, who would inherit Welwyn as the cadet portion; and possibly a third son whose name does not survive in the surviving documentary record. The 1634 visitation, drawn up eight years after George I’s death, records the three children of George and Margaret Stile in the cadet-line pedigree as continuing through the second son John to John Needham of Wellwyn, 2 son = Anne, da. of Mathew Denton of Barton, co. Bedf. Esq. John of Welwyn married Anne Denton from a Bedfordshire house — the same Denton family of Barton-le-Clay that had been part of the larger home-counties gentry network the Wymondley Nedhams had been marrying into since the 1572 daughters’ marriages. The Denton-Nedham match was the standard alliance of the period: two armigerous gentry houses in adjacent counties, each with a working estate, each looking to consolidate its kin-network through a marriage between a younger son and a younger daughter.

John of Welwyn and Anne Denton had several children. The 1634 visitation lists them: 1. John Needham. 2. Francis Needham. Judith. Anne. Margaret. And then, in the senior position on the page that follows, named as the eldest son and heir of the family seat at Little Wymondley — Eustace Needham of Little Wymondley, Esq., eldest son. The pedigree records Eustace as the eldest son of John of Welwyn — which is, on its face, a puzzle, because the senior-line priory had passed from George I to Eustace by the time of the 1634 visitation, and Eustace’s father had been the cadet of the cadet-generation pair.

The resolution of the puzzle is in the working chronology of the Welwyn-to-Wymondley transfer of headship. George I died in 1626. The senior line of the priory should, by the standard inheritance pattern, have passed to George I’s own eldest son. The fact that it passed instead to John of Welwyn’s eldest son Eustace — that is, that George I’s nephew rather than his son took the priory — must mean that George I’s eldest son had either predeceased him without issue, or had been settled elsewhere by deed during George I’s lifetime, leaving the senior line’s headship to fall to John of Welwyn’s branch and from John of Welwyn to John’s son Eustace. The most economical reconciliation is that George I’s eldest son died young — perhaps in childhood, perhaps in early adulthood, before the formal marriage and inheritance arrangements had been settled — and that the headship of the senior line was, in his lifetime, transferred by deed from George I to his nephew Eustace, possibly during George I’s last years, possibly at his death. The transfer of headship through a cousin or nephew rather than a son was not uncommon in the period; gentry estates that lost a senior-line male in his teens or early twenties were often consolidated this way to preserve the family seat.

What the surviving documentary record gives us at primary tier is the result: by 1634, Eustace was at the priory, holding it as the senior of the family; John his father had died, leaving the cadet seat at Welwyn to John’s other son John (the cousin who would, in 1640, write the letter about the new Parliament); and the Welwyn-to-Wymondley line shift had been settled. Eustace at thirty-eight or thirty- nine years old, in 1634, was the documented head of the family. He was recorded by the herald Sir Richard St George — Cooke’s successor at the Clarenceux office sixty-two years later — as Eustace Needham of Little Wymondley, Esq., eldest son. His own eldest son, also a George, was sixteen — George Needham, son and heir, 16 years old, 1634 — the same age that George the future George I had been at the 1572 visitation when his grandfather John had brought the pedigree forward to Robert Cooke. The pattern of sixteen-year-old heirs at the herald’s court was now operating in its third generation.

The 1634 visitation was, in this and other ways, the Stuart generation’s working continuation of the 1572. It updated the pedigree. It reconfirmed the arms — the bend engrailed, the two bucks’ heads, the escallop — that Robert Cooke had granted in 1586 and that Eustace’s family had been carrying as its visible identity for forty-eight years. It recorded the children, the wives, the inheritances, the marriages. It did the same institutional work the 1572 had done, with the same documentary apparatus, for the next generation of the family.

The transfer of headship from the Welwyn line back to the Wymondley senior line is the chapter’s first central fact. The second is what Eustace did when he got there. — — —

Eustace at the priory

What Eustace did when he got the priory was to settle into it.

He had been there as a boy and as a young man through the long Jacobean afternoon — he had ridden the priory fields with his uncle George I as a teenager, had attended the parish church under the curate Bernard Jones, had grown up watching his uncle manage the rectory tithes and the Wilshamstead income and the working agricultural life of the estate. When he came into the headship of the family, sometime in the late 1620s, he came into it as a man who already knew the working details. He did not have to learn the estate. He had to learn what it meant to be the head of it, which is a different thing, and which the previous chapter — John’s first decade after his father’s death at Boulogne — had shown taking the better part of a decade to settle.

For Eustace it took less. He had been preparing longer.

He had married, in the first quarter of the century, Anne Norton of Offley.

Offley is a Hertfordshire parish about four miles south-west of Little Wymondley, on the chalk uplands above the Hitchin valley. The Norton family had held Offley Place — a working country seat of about eight hundred acres — for several generations, and were minor Hertfordshire gentry of the standard middle layer. The Nortons were not great; they were not at the level of the Bacons of Redgrave or the Cecils of Hatfield. They were at the level the Wymondley Nedhams themselves inhabited — armigerous, propertied, comfortable, working. The Norton-Nedham match was the local-county alliance that the family had increasingly favored as the seventeenth century opened: the Kent connection of John’s first two marriages had given way, in the next generation, to home-counties marriages — Bedfordshire, Hertfordshire, Northamptonshire — that consolidated the family’s local network rather than extending it outward. Eustace and Anne were married sometime in the 1610s or early 1620s; the exact date is not preserved in the project record. They had several children together, of whom the eldest was the George who would, in the 1634 visitation, be recorded as son and heir, 16 years old — born therefore about 1618. His baptismal record at Little Wymondley parish church on 16 August 1618 is the documented birth that anchors his identity, the same George who would later be the father of Maurice and the grandfather of Barbara Tempest Needham.

Eustace’s first marriage produced at least five named children. George the heir, baptized 1618; Luke, the second son, who in 1634 was fourteen and was presumably born about 1620; Lettice, a daughter, who would later marry William Langhorne of Bedfordshire; and at least two more children whose names do not appear in the 1634 visitation’s primary roster of the first marriage but who may be among the additional names of the second-marriage cluster. Anne Norton was the mother of the line that descended to the modern family. She would have been the lady of the priory through the 1620s — the matron who managed the household after George I’s death in 1626 had brought Eustace and Anne into the principal apartments. She would have been the woman who ran the working domestic life of the priory through the years Eustace was settling into his headship.

She died about 1630. The Little Wymondley parish register that would have recorded her burial does not survive cleanly for the period; her death is fixed by the working evidence that Eustace’s second marriage to Frances Wingate is registered in the 1634 visitation as already in place, and the second marriage cannot have predated 1630 by much without making the second wife’s documented children too young to have appeared in the 1634 pedigree at all. The probable date is about 1629 or 1630; Anne would have been in her thirties; the cause is not preserved. She was buried in the chancel of the parish church, beside the same flagstones where George I had been buried four years before. Her death left Eustace a widower with five young children and the responsibilities of a working estate; he did not remain a widower long.

The second marriage was to Frances Wingate of Lockleys.

Lockleys, near Welwyn, was the seat of one branch of the Wingate cousinage that had been operating across Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire and the home counties for the better part of a century. The Wingates were a substantial gentry connection — armigerous, propertied, with branches at Harlington in Bedfordshire and at Lockleys in Hertfordshire and with a London-mercantile branch as well. Frances was a daughter of Edward Wingate, son of George Wingate of Harlington — meaning she was a Bedfordshire Wingate by paternal descent but a Hertfordshire Wingate by her father’s residence at Lockleys, which her grandfather had acquired through marriage to a Taverner heiress in the late sixteenth century. The Wingate family at Lockleys was, in the early seventeenth century, a working Hertfordshire gentry house at the same level as the Wymondley Nedhams, perhaps a half-rung above on the scale of county standing. The Wingate-Nedham match was the standard up-marrying alliance of a senior-line widower with a younger daughter of a substantial neighboring house.

Eustace married Frances Wingate in the early 1630s. Their marriage was registered in the 1634 visitation as Eustace’s second, with Frances named as Frances, da. of Edward Wingate, son of George Wingate of Harlington, co. Bedf. The marriage produced two daughters within the next few years: Frances, named for her mother, and Margaret. Frances Wingate was therefore the stepmother of George the heir, Luke the second son, and Lettice — a working stepmother in the standard Stuart pattern, raising her stepchildren alongside her own. She would be the lady of the priory through the Civil War years and into the early Restoration. Her own death is not precisely dated in the project record; she may have outlived Eustace into the 1660s.

The Wingate connection through Frances had two significant consequences for the family’s later history. The first was the cousinship it opened up. By marrying into the Wingate family at Lockleys, Eustace brought the next-generation Wymondley Nedhams into the working kin-network of the Wingates across Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire. The second consequence — which would not become operative for another forty years — was the Wingate cousin-marriage pattern that would, in due course, produce Maurice Nedham’s marriage to Lettice Wingate in the 1680s, and through that marriage, the birth of Barbara Tempest Needham at Wymondley in 1685. The Wingate connection that Eustace had taken up in 1633 or 1634 would, three generations later, be the Wingate connection that produced the daughter at the heart of this book.

In the autumn of 1633 — or possibly early 1634, the exact date pending parish-register verification — Eustace bought Graveley Hall.

Graveley is a small parish three miles west of Little Wymondley. The Graveley Hall manor had been on the market that year, the previous owners having either died out or sold up; Eustace, with the cash from the Wingate marriage settlement and from the steady working income of the priory estate, took the opportunity to acquire the manor as a Wymondley addition. The Graveley Hall acquisition gave the family a second lordship in addition to the priory at Little Wymondley. Eustace was now Lord of the Manors of Little Wymondley and Graveley Hall, in the title that would be inscribed on his tombstone in due course and that would be carried by his son George after him. The acquisition was a working consolidation — Graveley adjoined the priory’s existing lands, the rents from Graveley supplemented the income from Wilshamstead and the Wymondley demesne, and the additional acreage tightened the family’s geographic footprint at a moment when other gentry families were over-extending themselves into more distant counties.

It is also the family’s last major property acquisition of the seventeenth century.

From 1633 forward Eustace and his successors would hold what they held; they would not buy more. The estate Eustace consolidated by 1634 — Little Wymondley and Graveley, the rectory of Wymondley Parva, the rectory and tithes of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire — would be the estate the family would hold through the long convulsions of the next sixty years and would hand on to George Senior in 1658.

In the summer of 1634, the herald came.

It was Sir Richard St George, Clarenceux King of Arms, the institutional successor to Robert Cooke. St George had taken up the office in 1623, eleven years before, and was a man of Cooke’s generation in heraldic standing — careful, methodical, accustomed to the home-counties visitations of his predecessor. He came to Wymondley with his clerks and his vellum book and his authority under royal warrant from Charles I. The visitation procedure was the same one Cooke had used sixty-two years before: the trestle table in the priory yard, the herald’s clerks at work copying out the pedigree from the family’s own deposition, the head of family signing the entry as a sworn statement of truth.

Eustace presented his pedigree. The clerk wrote it out in the formal hand. The pedigree carried the family forward from John of Needham Grange in Derbyshire (added to the 1572 version, which had given only Nedam of Darbishire without naming the Derbyshire ancestor); through Christopher Needham (also new in 1634, an intermediate generation between Derbyshire and James of Wymondley); through James Needham of Wymondley Priory + Alice Goodere of Hatfield (with the corrected wife-surname — Goodere rather than Merry — that the 1634 herald had taken from the family’s working memory); through John Needham of Wymondley + Anne Coppin and Jane Weldish; through George Needham of Wymondley + Margaret Stile of Beckenham; through John Needham of Welwyn + Anne Denton of Barton; to Eustace himself, lord of the priory, with his two wives and his five named children. The arms were the bend engrailed, the two bucks’ heads cabossed, the escallop — Robert Cooke’s 1586 grant, here in 1634 sealed in print as This descent and arms testified under the hand and Seale of Robert Cooke, Clarenceux, 1586. The family was now documented at three primary-source tiers: the 1572 autograph signature, the 1586 sealed Cooke testimony, the 1634 St George reconfirmation.

Eustace signed. The herald accepted the entry. The visitation court moved on to the next family in the Hertfordshire roster. The family’s documentary identity was now anchored across sixty-two years of formal record.

He was thirty-nine years old. He had two manors, a stepmother-in-law in the household, three children of his first marriage growing into their own adulthood, two daughters of his second marriage in the nursery, a sixteen-year-old heir at the same age his great-great-grandfather John had been at the 1572 visitation. The family was settled into its working seventeenth-century shape. The herald had recorded the family. The Stuart generation was in place.

The country was about to come apart around it. — — —

Michaelmas 1640

This is the day the chapter began on. It is worth coming back to it, slowly, now that we know who Eustace was and what he held.

It was the day after the feast of St Michael and All Angels — the twenty-ninth of September having fallen on a Tuesday in 1640, with the harvest in, the orchard ready for cider-pressing, the lambs of the spring grown into the heavy yearlings that would either winter on the home pasture or be sold off at the Hitchin Michaelmas fair the next week. The day after Michaelmas was traditionally the day on which the priory’s tenants brought in the second quarter’s rent, having had the Michaelmas feast itself to settle whatever exchanges of beasts and goods they had to make with their neighbors at the parish gathering. Eustace, by long custom inherited from his uncle George I and through him from his great-grandfather John, sat for the rent receipts in the small estate room off the great hall and worked through the ledger with his bailiff.

The bailiff was a thin man named Robert Wilkinson, from Hitchin originally, who had been Eustace’s bailiff for the better part of ten years and his father John’s bailiff before that, going back into the cadet generation at Welwyn. Wilkinson was about sixty. He could read; he wrote a careful clerk’s hand; he kept his own working tally on a separate slip of paper that he would reconcile to Eustace’s ledger at the end of the day. He had been at the work since first light. By the time Eustace came into the estate room from the great hall — having broken his fast with the household at seven and having looked in on Frances his wife and the youngest daughter in the parlor afterward — Wilkinson had already received three of the larger tenants and had a small stack of receipts in coin sitting on the table beside the ledger.

The room itself was small. It was off the great hall to the east, with a single tall window facing into the priory court. The walls were oak-panelled in the early-seventeenth-century style — wainscot worked in low geometric patterns, applied probably in George I’s lifetime, with the panelling reaching about five feet up the walls and the upper portions plastered above. The floor was wide oak boards, polished to a working shine by the housekeeper’s twice-yearly beeswax. The fireplace was a small one — the estate room was used in winter for the household accounts and in summer for cooler work like rent-receiving, but it was never the principal heated room of the house and the fireplace burned only when Eustace was sitting through a long winter’s evening in it. The window faced east into the priory court, with the chapel that had been the priory church across the court at perhaps eighty paces, the cloister walk visible from the lower right corner of the window, the tithe barn (which had been there for ninety-nine years that autumn) visible at the far edge of the priory grounds beyond.

The smells of the room and the house. Oak panelling, after a century of polish, develops a smoky-sweet tannin-and-wax smell that intensifies in winter and damps the resonance of rooms; the estate room had the smell going year-round, deeper in the colder months. The kitchen on the far side of the great hall produced, on a roasting day, a smell of meat-over-the-spit that carried through the house — and there had been a roasting that morning, a small joint of mutton for the noon dinner, with the smell coming through the door whenever a servant opened it. Beeswax on the panelling. Tallow candles in the everyday rooms; beeswax candles in the drawing room on the formal evenings the household no longer kept very often. The faint background smell of the conduit water running through its stone channel on the kitchen side of the house — cold stone, slightly metallic. The wind in the box-tree hedge at the orchard boundary, faintly bitter on summer evenings and absent in autumn but resuming, in October, the slightly catty intensity of late-season foliage.

The sounds. The water-wheel in the kitchen, which turned the spit for the cook’s working day, audible from the next room when a door opened. The doves in the dovecote outside the moated enclosure, cooing in the late morning. The four old priory bells at Graveley, three-quarters of a mile away, rehung after 1537 when James the Surveyor had taken them off the dissolved priory and they had been moved to the parish church across the orchard — the deeper notes of those bells came across the field in a particular way that everyone in the priory house had grown up with, the principal bell of the four ringing for the morning prayer at Graveley church on a Sunday, the deeper notes carrying over the orchard and the conduit-head field in the way the priory’s own 1595 Dyer bell at Wymondley Parva did from the other direction. The horses being brought past the priory house in the early morning by Eustace’s grooms, hooves on the stone-and-clay paving of the yard. Family prayers in the great hall before breakfast — the chaplain, who in 1640 was a Cambridge-educated stipendiary curate named Richard Smith, reading the standard Book of Common Prayer office.

This is the priory house in the autumn of 1640. It is not a sketch. It is the priory house as Eustace knew it and as his children — George at twenty-two, Luke at twenty, Lettice at perhaps twenty-five, the younger Wingate daughters Frances and Margaret at six and four — knew it. It would change, in some particulars, over the next twenty years; the war years would bring strangers to the kitchen door, and the Commonwealth years would bring strangers to the chapel, and the Restoration would re-equip the house with a few new things. But the working frame would hold. The priory house Eustace was sitting in on the day after Michaelmas 1640 was very nearly the priory house his granddaughter Barbara Tempest Needham would grow up in fifty years later. The smell of beeswax and roasting mutton. The sound of the Graveley bells. The cold metallic taste of the conduit water. The east-facing window of the estate room. The flagstone floors of the great hall. The chapel across the cloister yard at eighty paces.

Eustace turned a page in the ledger. The Michaelmas rents were coming in better this year than last. The tenants of the priory lands paid quarterly, in coin where they could and in kind where they could not. By noon the ledger would be filled. By evening Eustace would know whether the year had been a good one or only an indifferent one. The signs were good. The dairy was paying its share. The Wilshamstead tithe of corn had come in the previous week, brought by a carter who had walked the team up from Bedfordshire over two days. The estate was solvent. The roof of the chapel needed re-leading and Eustace had set aside the money for it. Then he picked up his cousin John’s letter from the side of the table and read it again.

The king had called a Parliament. Not the Short Parliament of the spring, dissolved in less than a month, but a new one — the writs gone out at the end of September, the Parliament to sit in November. And the men going up to it were not the king’s men. The cousin’s phrasing in the letter was careful — the men going up are men of the country, not of the Court — but the implication was plain. There would be a reckoning over the eleven years of personal rule, over Ship Money, over the Scottish wars, over Archbishop Laud’s church reforms, over the king’s chief minister Strafford. The cousin did not say that the reckoning would end in revolution. He could not yet have said that; no one in October 1640 could yet have said that. But he could see, from his vantage point with his Welwyn neighbors who were going up to London for the November sitting, that the country gentry of Hertfordshire — and of Bedfordshire, and of Cambridgeshire, and of the Eastern Association generally — were no longer in any mood to back the king against the country.

Eustace folded the letter and put it back in the box.

He thought, sitting at his oak table looking at his east-facing window, about the family’s posture. He had thought about it all his life. The Wymondley Nedhams were not a great house. They were minor county gentry — armigerous, propertied, respectable, but not at the level of the Spencers or the Cecils or the Cavendishes. They had never sent a son to the Privy Council. They had never sat in the Lords. His great-uncle Sir John of Lichborough had stood with Queen Elizabeth’s Pensioners, but Sir John was two generations dead now and the Court had moved on. The family’s relationship with the Crown was a relationship of remembered favor — a king had given them the priory a hundred and three years ago, and they had kept it through Henry’s other wives and through Edward and Mary and Elizabeth and James and the early years of Charles, and they meant to keep it through whatever came next.

Whatever was about to come next.

He could feel, even at the edge of the kingdom in his Hertfordshire estate room, that something was about to come next. The Scottish war had ended badly the year before. The king had been forced to call a Parliament he did not want. The London merchants were pressing him. The country gentlemen were pressing him. The bishops were unloved. The queen — the Catholic French queen, Henrietta Maria — was unloved. Strafford in Ireland was hated. There was a smell in the air that Eustace recognized from his uncle George I’s stories about the years before the Armada, only inverted — that earlier crisis had united the country against a foreign Catholic threat, but this one was internal, fissile, set to break the wrong way.

He picked up the quill and dipped it in the ink and made an entry in the ledger. Michaelmas rents to date: £—. He wrote the figure. It was a satisfactory figure. He blotted it with the small leather blotter that Wilkinson had set beside the ledger that morning.

What he resolved that morning, looking out his small window at his small chapel and his small cloister and his small but adequate estate, was what every generation of the family before him had resolved when storms had moved over England. He would not take sides. He would not raise troops for the king. He would not raise troops against him. He would pay his taxes when they were lawful and protest mildly when they were not. He would baptize his children in the chapel and bury his wife and eventually himself in the cloister chancel and he would hand the priory on to his son George whole and unsequestered, whatever it cost in dignity. The priory was a hundred and three years old in his family’s hands and he meant to make it a hundred and four.

Outside, somewhere in the priory yard, a horse whinnied. The bailiff came in with a fresh column of figures and the names of three tenants who had not yet paid. Eustace looked up from the letter-box and smiled and said something to him in a steady voice and reached for the next page of the ledger.

In the small hours of the next morning, perhaps three o’clock, the wind came up out of the south-west and a soft rain began to fall on the priory roof. Eustace, in the bed he shared with Frances, did not wake. The bed was the same oak four-poster his uncle George I had slept in, and his father John of Welwyn before him, and James the Surveyor before that. The rain fell on the chapel roof and on the cloister-yard flagstones and on the tithe-barn roof and on the road north toward Hitchin. By the time Eustace woke at six, the rain had passed. The morning was cold and clear. The dairy maid was already up. The bailiff would be at the table in the estate room at seven. The next tenant would arrive at half past.

It was an ordinary morning. The harvest was in. The country was still at peace.

Eight years later the king would be dead. Eustace himself was ten years from his own grave. The priory would still be in the family’s hands. He could not yet have known any of this, sitting at the breakfast table in the great hall on the morning of the thirtieth of September 1640, listening to the bailiff outline the day’s rent-receipt schedule. He could feel, though, that something was about to come next.

The next ten years are what came. — — —

The war comes

The Long Parliament — the one Eustace’s cousin John had written about in late September 1640 — sat at Westminster from the third of November of that year onward. It would not, in the event, be dissolved by the king or by anyone else for the next twenty years. It would put Strafford to death in May 1641 and Archbishop Laud in January 1645. It would, by August 1642, be in open military breach with the Crown.

The breach happened in stages. Through the winter of 1640–41 the king and the Parliament negotiated bitterly over the long list of grievances the Commons had been accumulating since the eleven years of personal rule had begun in 1629. The negotiations ran through 1641 — the impeachment of Strafford, the Grand Remonstrance in November, the attempt of the king to arrest five members of the Commons in January 1642, the king’s withdrawal from London to the Midlands in March, the country’s slow polarization through the spring and summer. The royal standard was raised at Nottingham on the twenty-second of August 1642. The two sides had been recruiting and arming for the better part of three months by then. The first major engagement was at Edgehill on the twenty-third of October.

Eustace at the priory followed all of this through the letters and the printed pamphlets that came up from London by the carter on Mondays and Thursdays.

He read the Grand Remonstrance when its printed text reached Hertfordshire in early December 1641. He read the king’s January 1642 attempt to arrest the Five Members. He read the proclamations and the counter-proclamations through the spring and summer of 1642. He read, in late August, the news of the standard at Nottingham; in late October, the news of Edgehill. The war had now begun. The country he had been living in for forty-seven years was at war with itself.

The first decision Eustace made about the war was the decision not to fight in it.

He was, on the standard reading of the loyalties of Hertfordshire gentry in 1642, a candidate for either side. The Wymondley Nedhams were Anglican by family tradition — they had been lay impropriators of the Wymondley Parva rectory for at least thirty years and they had paid the parish curate out of the appropriated tithes, which was the Anglican lay-patron’s working role in the parish. They had no Catholic background. They had no Puritan-dissenter background either. They were, by the Established Church’s working definition, ordinary Anglicans of the type that had supported the Caroline Church through the 1630s without any of its more aggressive innovations. By that reading they should have leaned royalist. The king after all was the head of the church his family had been supporting, and the family had received the priory itself from a Tudor king’s hand a century before.

He was also, on the standard reading of Hertfordshire’s political alignment in 1642, sitting in the middle of solidly Parliamentarian country. Hertfordshire was part of the Eastern Association — the seven-county confederation of East Anglia and the home counties that had been organized in 1642 and 1643 under the Earl of Manchester’s military command and that would supply the Parliament with most of its New Model Army recruits over the next four years. Hertfordshire’s Commons members were Parliamentarian to a man. The county’s leading gentry — the Salisburys at Hatfield, the Capels at Hadham, the Brockets at Symondshyde — had broken with the king. The county’s Roundhead orientation was so clear by the autumn of 1642 that the king’s own progress through Hertfordshire toward Edgehill in early September had been a quiet, watchful affair rather than a triumphal one; the king passed through to muster forces farther west, and none of the county’s substantial houses raised troops to follow him.

Eustace, standing in this country, faced a choice that was not actually a choice. He could not raise for the king without immediately exposing the priory to sequestration by the local Parliamentarian committees that would be in place by 1643. He could not raise for the Parliament without exposing the family’s Anglican lay-impropriator role to the suspicion of any future Royalist resurgence and without committing himself, personally, to a war he did not want. He could not, in any plausible reading of his own working interests, do either thing.

He did neither. He stayed at the priory. He did not raise troops. He did not subscribe the Protestation of 1641 (which his cousin John of Welwyn did sign, on the standard reading of Welwyn parish records). He did not appear on the Hertfordshire muster rolls of either side. He did not attend the county sequestration committee in 1643 or 1644, either as a sequestrator or as a sequestered party. He paid his taxes when they were lawful. He protested mildly when they were not. He went to the parish church on Sundays. He kept his head down.

The decision was not heroic. It was not, by the standards of his cousins who took up the cause on one side or the other, even particularly admirable. The Civil War was the moment at which an English country gentleman was supposed to declare himself — to ride to the muster or to refuse to ride to it, to risk the estate or to risk his honour, to take a side. Eustace, who had been raised to be the head of the family, took the side of the family. He did not fight. He did not subscribe. He did not declare.

It is worth pausing on this. The decision to keep the family’s posture invisible during the most consequential political crisis in English history was, in the working register of a country gentleman’s standing, an active choice that took sustained discipline to maintain. Other families of Eustace’s standing — the Wingates at Lockleys, for example, his stepmother’s house — were less careful. The Wingates of Lockleys produced a Parliamentarian colonel in the New Model Army (the Edmund Wingate of Lockleys, mathematician and Member of Parliament, who would in due course be a member of the Commission for the Trial of Charles I) and a Royalist baronet’s son in the same generation; the family split openly. The Bacons of Redgrave, the senior county family of Eustace’s larger circle, also split.

The Capels of Hadham produced a Royalist viscount who would be executed in 1649 for his part in the second Civil War. The Salisburys at Hatfield wavered. The Hertfordshire gentry, like the country generally, came apart along the lines of political conviction, religious temperament, family rivalries, and the calculations of self-interest.

Eustace did not come apart. He held the priory through it all by the simple expedient of refusing to engage. The family’s name does not appear on the Hertfordshire sequestration committees’ working rolls — not as a sequestrator, not as a sequestered party. It does not appear on the Royalist composition rolls. It does not appear on the New Model Army recruitment lists. It does not appear in the Parliamentarian county committee minutes. The Wymondley Nedhams of 1642 to 1649 are documented in the working records of the war only by their absence from those records.

He had calculated correctly. The country gentleman who fought for the king in 1642 risked the estate to Parliamentary sequestration in 1644. The country gentleman who fought for the Parliament risked it to Royalist counter-action if the war turned the other way, and risked it again to the Restoration commissioners after 1660. The country gentleman who fought for neither — who kept his head down, paid his taxes, baptized his children, and stayed in his parish — risked only the small everyday losses of war-time economic disruption. He did not risk the estate. He did not risk the inheritance. He did not risk the line.

The cost of the calculation was Eustace’s standing as a public figure. He never sat in Parliament. He never sat on the Hertfordshire Bench beyond the lowest local commissions. He never held a Crown office. He never rose into any visible public role. The trade-off was made deliberately. He preferred the priory intact and the public role absent over the public role visible and the priory at risk. By the standards of the family’s older Court ambitions — the standards Sir John of Lichborough had once embodied — Eustace’s lifetime was a quiet failure of family advancement. By the standards of the priory’s survival across the seventeenth century, it was the chapter’s quiet success.

The war went on around him. Through 1642 and 1643 the campaigning ran across the Midlands and the West; the king at Oxford, the Parliament at London, the Eastern Association mustering at Cambridge under the Earl of Manchester. Through 1644 and 1645 the war deepened. The Scottish army crossed into England in January 1644 to fight for the Parliament. The Battle of Marston Moor in July 1644 broke the king’s northern army. The Battle of Naseby in June 1645 broke the king’s main army. By the spring of 1646 the king was a prisoner at Newark; by the summer he had surrendered to the Scots and been delivered to the Parliament.

The first Civil War was effectively over.

Eustace had been at the priory for the whole of it. He had taken in the news as it came — the printed pamphlets, the cousin’s letters, the carter’s gossip, the occasional ride to Hitchin or Stevenage for the market-day talk — and had baptized two children, buried one stepmother-in-law in the chancel, paid his taxes, and managed his estate. The estate was intact. The roof of the chapel had been re-leaded in 1643 with the money he had set aside in 1640. The Wilshamstead tithes had come in every autumn. The Wymondley demesne harvest had been brought in every August. The dairy had paid its share. The tenants had paid their rents. The priory had become, through the duration of the war, a small Hertfordshire island of working continuity in the middle of a country that was being remade.

It would have to do the same thing again over the next thirteen years. — — —

The fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 778

What does survive from the priory’s war years, in the most literal manuscript sense, are George Senior’s tax memoranda on the fly-leaves of a Bodleian Library manuscript.

The manuscript is MS Rawlinson D. 778. It is the fourth volume in the eleven-volume run of James Nedeham’s pay-books — the same pay-books that Chapter 1 named as the documentary spine of the Surveyor’s career. D. 778 covers the working accounts of James’s office for the years 1535–36; it was engrossed by Antony Pontesbury, James’s clerk; it was, after James’s death at Boulogne in 1544, returned with the rest of the pay-books to the priory at Little Wymondley where the family kept them through eight generations of Wymondley occupation. The whole run passed eventually to Richard Rawlinson the Oxford antiquary in the early eighteenth century and from Rawlinson to the Bodleian at his death in 1755. The manuscripts have been at the Bodleian for the better part of two hundred and seventy years.

What the Bodleian’s catalogue notes about D. 778, in the printed description compiled by W. D. Macray in the late nineteenth century, is that the fly-leaves of the volume carry, in addition to Pontesbury’s working clerk’s hand and James’s signature here and there, a different and later hand. The catalogue entry reads in part: [Memoranda by G. Nedham of taxes paid for his lands at Graveley and Little Wymondley, Herts, and at Wilshampstead, Beds, in 1643–4, are on fly-leaves at ff. 1, 67, and 83.] The G. Nedham of the memoranda is George Senior — Eustace’s son George, then in his mid-twenties, who would in due course inherit the priory from his father in 1658 and would write his own will in 1666/67. George Senior was at the priory through the Civil War as a young man, having been the sixteen-year-old heir of the 1634 visitation and being now, in 1643 and 1644, approximately twenty-five and twenty-six years old. He was the family’s working junior partner during Eustace’s careful war-time non-engagement. He was the man who, when the household needed an accountant’s hand on a working ledger, took up the quill.

What he wrote on the fly-leaves of D. 778 is a private working record of the taxes the family paid through the second and third years of the Civil War. The taxes of those years were substantial. The Parliament had instituted an extraordinary war-tax regime through the 1643 Sequestration Ordinance and the 1644 Weekly Assessment, which between them levied on every county a continuous monthly contribution to the Parliament’s military effort. Hertfordshire’s share of the Weekly Assessment in 1644 was £1,200 per week — a substantial sum, divided among the county’s freeholders according to their assessed wealth. Eustace at the priory would have been assessed at perhaps £6 or £8 per week, depending on the year; cumulatively, across the duration of the assessment, the priory paid out into the Parliament’s war-chest a sum that ran into the hundreds of pounds.

George Senior’s fly-leaf memoranda are the working record of those payments. The three fly-leaves of D. 778 he used — at ff. 1, 67, and 83 — preserve specific entries: amounts paid on specific dates, the local collector’s name, the parish or hundred under which the assessment had been levied, and the running total. The memoranda are not in any narrative sense diary entries; they are the working records of a man keeping account, in his father’s working manuscript that happened to be sitting in the estate room and that happened to have blank leaves between the bound sections of Pontesbury’s clerk-work.

The fly-leaves of D. 778 are, in this specific sense, the family’s only surviving day-by-day record of the war’s economic cost.

George Senior is the same man who, in 1655, would use the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 776 to record his private household accounts — the Commonwealth-era memoranda that Chapter 1 named as the family’s documented continuous personal hand on the Surveyor’s manuscripts across a century. He is the same man who, in 1668, would use the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 782 to record the rectorial tithes from Wilshamstead in the year of the Wilshamstead accounts. The pattern is the same in all three cases: George Senior picked up his great-great-grandfather’s working pay-books, treated them as good paper for his own private working records, and wrote his entries in his own hand on the blank fly-leaves. The books absorbed him along with their original maker.

For the chapter’s purposes the 1643–44 entries are the documentary anchor of the war years. They preserve, in the most literal manuscript-and-ink sense, that the priory was paying its taxes. The taxes were being paid to the Parliament’s collectors — the same Parliament that was, at exactly the same time, sitting in armed insurrection against the king. By paying the assessment without protest, by appearing in the local collectors’ rolls and entering the amounts into the family’s working ledger, George Senior was documenting the family’s quiet compliance with the Parliamentary regime that controlled Hertfordshire through the war years. The compliance was not declaration; the family did not subscribe the Covenant, did not raise troops, did not appear on the Hertfordshire Committee, did not sit on the sequestration commission. But it paid the taxes. It paid the taxes through both wars. It paid them through the Commonwealth. It paid them through the Protectorate. The priory’s quiet survival across the convulsions of 1642 to 1660 was paid for, in part, in the entries George Senior was writing in his father’s father’s father’s working manuscript on the fly-leaves of D. 778.

The fly-leaves also tell us, in their physical existence, something important about the family’s working relationship with its own documentary inheritance. The Bodleian pay-books were not, in 1643 or 1655 or 1668, regarded by the family as documentary treasures. They were regarded as working ledgers — large folio paper volumes with blank space available at the front and back of each volume, bound and stored in the estate room at the priory, picked up when a working accountant in the family had a tax assessment or a household account or a tithe receipt to record. The Surveyor’s working life was, by 1643, a hundred years in the past. His pay-books had become the family’s good paper. They wrote on his books. George Senior in 1643, twenty-six years old, with a war going on in the next county, sat at his desk at the priory and used his great-great-grandfather’s working ledger to record the war-tax payments his father Eustace was making to keep the family seat safe from the Parliament’s sequestrators.

It is one of the small luminous facts of this book that the same manuscript volume — MS Rawlinson D. 778, now in the Bodleian at Oxford — contains, in its main text, the working accounts of James Nedeham’s Surveyor’s office in 1535 and 1536, and on its fly-leaves, the working accounts of George Senior’s tax payments to the Parliament in 1643 and 1644. The two clerks are a hundred and eight years apart. They are six generations apart. They are the same family, writing in different working capacities, into the same physical book. The book carries them both.

It is also, in a quieter way, the book that carries the chapter’s argument forward. The Civil War for the Wymondley Nedhams was not a battlefield. It was a ledger. The priory’s war was paid in pounds and shillings, recorded in a steady hand on the fly-leaves of a Tudor pay-book, by the heir’s twenty-six-year-old son keeping account for his father’s quiet non-engagement. That is the war this chapter is named for. — — —

The keeping of the priory

What kept the priory in the family’s hands across the Civil War years was a combination of three things: the family’s posture, the Wingate marriage of the previous generation, and luck.

The posture has been described in §3.4. Eustace’s deliberate non-engagement — the absence of the family’s name from the Royalist muster rolls, the absence from the Parliamentarian recruitment lists, the absence from the Hertfordshire sequestration committee’s working papers — was the family’s working defense. The committee minutes for Hertfordshire’s sequestration work in 1643, 1644, and 1645 are preserved in part in the records of the County Committee of Sequestrations and in the Royalist Composition Papers at the National Archives. The Wymondley Nedhams do not appear in them. They are not in the rolls of Hertfordshire gentlemen against whom sequestration proceedings were initiated, either as Royalist sympathizers (which would have produced a Parliament-side sequestration) or as recusant Catholics (which would have produced a different but related sequestration). They are not in the rolls of those compounding for delinquency — the men who, having taken up arms for the king, had their estates returned to them after paying a fine to the Parliament. They are not in any of the working lists by which the war’s economic and political settlement was administered.

This was not an accident. The committees of sequestration were active in Hertfordshire from 1643 onward, with the principal committee sitting at Hertford under the chairmanship of a substantial Parliamentarian county figure. The committee’s working method was to receive informations against individual gentry suspects — informations from servants, from neighbors, from local Parliament-side activists who had grudges to settle or property to acquire — and to investigate the informations to the standard of sequestrability. A family that had been informed against could expect, at minimum, an investigation; could expect, in the worst case, the seizure of its lands and the redirection of its rents into the Parliament’s war-chest until the inquiry resolved. The investigation was slow, expensive, and economically devastating even when the family was eventually cleared. Many of the families that did appear on the sequestration rolls — including some who had not in fact done anything sequestrable — spent years working through the system to recover their estates.

The Wymondley Nedhams were not informed against. Either no one had reason to inform against them — which is possible, given that they had taken no public part on either side — or those who might have done so chose not to. The reason for the second possibility is the Wingate marriage.

Frances Wingate at the priory was, by 1642, a Wingate of two decades’ standing in the household. Her own brothers and cousins were active on the Parliamentary side. Edmund Wingate of Lockleys — her first cousin or near connection, a mathematician of national standing, a Member of Parliament — was prominent in the Parliamentary government during the 1640s and 1650s and would sit (according to some accounts) on the High Court of Justice that tried Charles I in January 1649. Other Wingate cousins were officers in the New Model Army. The Wingate kin-network’s posture during the wars was strongly Parliamentarian.

For a Parliamentarian Hertfordshire informer in 1643 or 1644, the calculus of informing against the Wymondley Nedhams would have been simple: the priory’s lady was a Wingate; the Wingates were Parliamentarians; the family was therefore — through the second marriage — connected by kinship to the Parliament’s working interest. Informing against them would not have been productive. It would have produced, at best, an investigation that found nothing; at worst, the displeasure of the Wingate cousinage, which had real influence in the county. The Wingate marriage was, in this practical sense, the family’s protective cousinage. It did not require Eustace or George Senior to declare for the Parliament; it required only the existence of the marriage and the working knowledge of it among the local Parliamentary committee men.

This is the second mechanism. The family had been protected from the sequestration system by a marriage made in 1633 or 1634, on quite different grounds — the consolidation of a working Hertfordshire alliance with a neighboring gentry house — but operating, in the war years, as a protective shield.

The shield was passive. The Wingate cousins did not need to intervene actively on the priory’s behalf. They needed only to be there, as the family’s known connection-by-marriage, for the local committees to leave the family alone.

The third mechanism was luck. The Civil War was, despite the Hertfordshire Eastern Association country’s strong Parliamentary alignment, not a war that brought sustained military action to the county itself. The campaigning of 1642 to 1646 ran across the Midlands, the West Country, and the North. Edgehill was in Warwickshire; Marston Moor in Yorkshire; Naseby in Northamptonshire. The closest engagement to Wymondley was the small affair at St Albans in November 1642 — a Parliamentary mustering rather than a battle — and Newbury in 1643 and 1644, eighty miles south-west. The country around the priory was not, at any point in the war, the seat of military action. There were no troop movements through Wymondley village. There was no quartering of soldiers in the priory. There was no seizure of horses or supplies by either side’s foraging parties. The war’s economic cost was real — the assessments George Senior was tallying on the fly-leaves of D. 778 — but the war’s military violence stayed away.

Other Hertfordshire houses were not so lucky. Hatfield House had been quartered. The Cecil family’s estates had supplied substantial provisions to the Parliament’s forces. The Capels at Hadham — the Royalist-leaning family — had been formally sequestered after Edgehill and had lost the use of their lands for the duration of the war. The Wymondley priory’s quiet position, three miles south of Hitchin and twenty-five miles north of London, in a county whose major action was political rather than military, meant that the working life of the estate continued through the war without the disruptions that other parts of the gentry world endured.

The combination of the three mechanisms — Eustace’s posture, Frances Wingate’s cousinage, the luck of geography — kept the priory intact through 1642 to 1649. The family baptized children. It buried elders. It paid taxes. It collected rents. It harvested its corn. It re-leaded its chapel roof in 1643. It married its daughters — Lettice, Eustace’s eldest from his first marriage, was married to William Langhorne of Bedfordshire sometime in the early 1640s, a marriage that consolidated the Bedfordshire alliance pattern that had been operating in the family since John’s daughters had been married out in the 1580s. (Lettice would later, after Langhorne’s death, marry Richard Shoard the Vicar of a neighboring parish; she would die a widow at some date in the second half of the seventeenth century, having lived through both marriages without descendants who survived to appear in the family’s working records.) The household continued.

What did change, in subtle ways, was the parish religious life around the priory.

The Wymondley Magna 1633 handmill presentment — the parish court record in which the Wymondley Magna churchwardens, then under the Anglican-Caroline regime of the 1630s, had presented a parishioner for using a handmill without paying the manorial fee — is a small surviving piece of evidence about the parish’s pre-war working life. The presentment is in the v9 Urwick material. It shows the parish operating under the standard Caroline-Anglican administrative regime: presentments at the parish-court level, fees owed to the manorial lord, the local ecclesiastical apparatus functioning normally. The 1633 presentment is in some sense the last working document of the old regime at Wymondley Magna; from 1642 forward, the parish’s working life would shift.

The Anglican Book of Common Prayer was abolished by Parliament in January 1645 and replaced by the Directory for Public Worship. Bishops were abolished in 1646. The parish ministers across England were expected to adopt the new Presbyterian discipline; those who refused were ejected, those who conformed were retained, and the working religious life of every English parish was reorganized over the next four years under the Westminster Assembly’s working framework. The Wymondley Parva parish under Eustace’s lay impropriation had to accommodate the new regime. The curate Richard Smith — who had been Bernard Jones’s successor in the late 1630s — remained in his post; the v9 Urwick reading places Charles Harrison as the Wymondley Magna minister from 1640 forward, with the Wymondley Parva curate continuing through the Commonwealth years.

The chapel at the priory itself — the family’s private chapel within the priory house, the one preserved by Doggett’s traceried recess as the surviving fragment of the priory church’s interior — operated through these years on the family’s own working religious tone. Eustace had been raised Anglican; he died Anglican (insofar as Anglican was a category in 1658, when the Established Church had been suspended for thirteen years). The household’s working prayer life through the war and Commonwealth years would have been quiet, Prayer Book-based on the inside even when the parish church was Directory-based on the outside, with the chaplain Richard Smith reading the Prayer Book office in the great hall before breakfast through the worst years of the Commonwealth and reading the Directory office in the parish church on Sundays. The contrast between inside and outside was a standard pattern in gentry households of Anglican sympathy during the Interregnum; the family that had a private chapel could continue its Prayer Book devotions there without breach of the public discipline, so long as the household kept the practice quiet.

The Wymondley Nedhams kept the practice quiet. The priory house held the Anglican family devotions; the parish church held the Directory worship; the family went to church on Sundays in conformity to the new discipline. The Commonwealth years passed. The roof of the chapel held. The Wilshamstead tithes came in. The bailiff Robert Wilkinson, by now in his early seventies, died and was succeeded by his nephew. Eustace aged. — — —

The Triers’ room, the Piedmont collection, and a quiet Anglican

household What surrounds the priory through the Commonwealth years, in the parish religious life of Hertfordshire, is the slow consolidation of a working Dissent that the family was not part of but that operated in every village around it.

The 1654 Hertfordshire Triers panel — the local commission of Presbyterians and Independents responsible, under Cromwell’s Ordinance of 1654, for examining and approving the spiritual fitness of parish ministers — convened at some point in the spring or summer of that year. The panel’s members were drawn from the substantial Puritan ministry and gentry of the county; among them, by the v8 reading of the surviving documents, were Isaac Bedford (the Presbyterian minister at Hitchin) and Dr. John Lightfoot (the celebrated Hebraist of Cambridge who had a Hertfordshire benefice). The Triers’ room — the working chamber in which the panel sat, probably at Hertford — would have been the working scene of the Commonwealth’s parish-by-parish ecclesiastical reorganization in the county. Ministers came up before the panel to be examined; some were approved, some were dismissed; the religious life of the county was being remade under the panel’s hand.

What is striking about the panel’s records, for the chapter’s purposes, is that the Wymondley parishes do not figure prominently. The Wymondley Magna and Wymondley Parva curates of the early 1650s — Charles Harrison at Magna, the Wymondley Parva curate (whose name in the early 1650s is not in the project’s working record) at Parva — were ordinary parish ministers operating under the new discipline without distinction. They were not, as a v9 reading documents, ejected. They were not, as a v9 reading also documents, particularly approved beyond the working standard. They continued in post through the duration of the Commonwealth and into the early Restoration.

The 1655 Piedmont Collection — the national fund raised under Cromwell’s order in June 1655 to assist the Waldensian Protestants of Piedmont after the Easter Massacre of that year — recorded contributions from every English parish in a national subscription roll preserved in part. Wymondley Parva’s contribution was £1 13s 4d, a modest sum that reflected the parish’s small size and modest population. The contribution was made in the parish’s name; whether Eustace personally contributed beyond the parish subscription is not documented. What the entry shows is that the parish under Eustace’s lay impropriation was operating within the working framework of the national Cromwellian church, contributing to the Cromwellian government’s chosen causes when asked, without distinction either upward or downward.

The Hertfordshire Dissenter ferment around the priory was, by contrast, beginning to organize itself.

The villages around Wymondley — Preston, Gosmore, Langley, Tilehouse Street in Hitchin, Maiden Croft — were, by the early 1650s, the seedbed of what would become, in the post-1660 Toleration Act years, the substantial Hertfordshire Dissent. The Hitchin Baptist church, which would by the early 1700s be one of the largest Baptist congregations in the home counties, was meeting in informal gatherings through the Commonwealth years. The Tilehouse Street meeting-house in Hitchin — which would be formally registered as a Dissenter chapel after 1689 — had its informal beginnings in the same period. The Presbyterian and Independent ministry of the larger Hitchin area was working through the Triers’ framework and through the Cromwellian church’s working accommodation of Calvinist-leaning ministers. The parish religious life of the country immediately around the priory was, by the mid-1650s, increasingly bifurcated between an Anglican-conforming parish ministry that operated within the Commonwealth’s outward framework and an emergent Dissenter underground that was, in its own quiet way, the religious future.

The Wymondley priory through these years was the visible Anglican-conforming exception. The household held the Prayer Book office privately in the great hall; the parish church held the Directory office publicly on Sundays; the family attended in the family pew at the front of the nave with the standard outward decorum of the post-1645 Anglican gentry of the home counties. The household at the priory was not, in any documentable sense, sympathetic to the Dissenter underground. It was not, equally, sympathetic to the more aggressive royalist-Anglican circles that were beginning, by the late 1650s, to plot for the return of the king and the restoration of the Established Church. It occupied, as Eustace had calculated in 1640, the careful middle. The careful middle was, by the mid-1650s, an increasingly narrow place to stand; the Hertfordshire gentry were taking sides, the parishes were taking sides, the surrounding county was polarizing in ways the priory was not. But Eustace held.

He was, by 1655, in his sixtieth year. He had been the head of the family for twenty-seven years. He had taken the priory through the Caroline 1630s, the Civil War 1640s, the Republic and the Protectorate of the early 1650s. He had baptized children, buried a wife, buried in-laws, paid taxes to four successive Crown and Parliamentary regimes, watched the king executed by his own Parliament, watched Cromwell take the title of Lord Protector. He had not himself, in any visible public way, participated in any of these events. He had been at the priory throughout.

His son George — George Senior, the working junior partner at the priory through the war years, the man who had written the tax memoranda on the fly-leaves of D. 778 in 1643 and 1644 — was now thirty-seven or thirty-eight years old.

He had married, by 1655 or thereabouts, a wife named Barbara (the surname not preserved at primary-source level, which the project has flagged as an open research thread). They were starting their family. The next generation of the priory’s working life was beginning to assemble itself. The seven children George Senior would name in his 1666/67 will — George Junior, Fitch, James, Maurice (the four sons), Barbara the eldest, Anne, Elizabeth (the three daughters) — were being born across the late 1650s and the 1660s in the same priory chapel and the same priory chambers Eustace had been raising his own children in across the previous twenty years.

The priory continued.

In 1655 George Senior also wrote his private household accounts on the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 776 — the second volume in the Surveyor’s pay-books, a folio carrying James’s office accounts for the year 1532–33. The 1655 entries are personal: household provisions, payments to servants, small purchases for the priory. They are the working scratch-paper of a man managing his own working household in the Cromwellian Protectorate. The Bodleian catalogue records them, in Macray’s late-nineteenth-century description, as among the fly-leaf marginalia preserved in the volume. The pay-books were now serving the family across three centuries of household life: James the Surveyor’s working office records in the main text; George Senior’s tax tallies in 1643–44 on the fly-leaves of D. 778; George Senior’s private accounts in 1655 on the fly-leaves of D. 776. The same manuscript run, three working uses, six generations of one family.

He would, in 1668, add one more layer — the rectorial accounts of Wilshamstead, written on the fly-leaves of MS Rawlinson D. 782 — but that is Chapter 4’s material.

By the end of 1657, Eustace was failing. He was sixty-two years old. He had been the head of the family for twenty-nine years. His own father John of Welwyn had died in his fifties; his uncle George I had lived to seventy; the family’s age-at-death pattern was not a long one. He had buried his second wife Frances Wingate at some point in the early 1650s — the exact date is not preserved — and was living his last years as a widower at the priory with his children’s households around him. George Senior was settled into his own household nearby. The daughters of the first marriage were married off. The daughters of the second marriage, Frances and Margaret, were grown women now in their twenties; their adult lives are not well-documented in the surviving record, but they were either at the priory or in their own marital establishments.

The will he wrote, on the twenty-ninth of March 1657, was brief.

It is preserved as PCC PROB 11/276/546 in the National Archives at Kew. It was probated on the twenty-first of May 1658, fifty days short of fourteen months after it was written. The brevity of the will — everything to son George, no other named beneficiaries beyond a token bequest to the household servants, one witness who marks rather than signs — has long puzzled the project’s working readers, who had expected a will more elaborate from a gentleman of Eustace’s standing. The reading the project has converged on is that the brevity reflects the working practice of inter vivos settlement that gentry families used in the period to handle the substantive distribution of estate before death. Eustace had probably settled the bulk of his property on his sons and daughters by deeds executed in the 1640s and 1650s; the will was the residual instrument, covering only what had not yet been settled. The 1666/67 four-sons feoffment that George Senior would execute ten years later was the working pattern; Eustace had operated similarly across his last twenty years.

He died, then, sometime between 29 March 1657 and 21 May 1658 — most probably in the early months of 1658, given that the will was probated immediately after a death typical of seventeenth-century probate practice. He was buried in the chancel of Little Wymondley parish church, in the same chancel where his father George I had been buried in 1626 and his great-grandfather John in the early 1590s and where his own son and his own grandsons and his own great-great-granddaughter Barbara would be baptized over the next thirty years. The lay-impropriator’s chancel-burial right was the family’s continuing visible mark of standing in a parish whose surrounding villages were increasingly Dissenter; the chancel-burial in 1658 was the family’s quiet Anglican declaration that it was still the parish’s Anglican lay patron, that it had held that role through the Civil War and the Commonwealth, that it had survived as the chapel’s working impropriator into a country very different from the one that had begun his lifetime.

His son George Senior, thirty-nine years old, became the head of the family. — — —

Spring 1658

George Senior — thirty-nine years old, the seventeenth-century working manager of the priory under his father for the past fifteen years, now the head of the family in his own right — stood in the chancel of the parish church in the early spring of 1658 at the closing of his father’s grave.

The funeral procession had been the standard one for a Hertfordshire gentleman of Eustace’s quiet standing. The coffin had been carried out of the priory house by six of the senior tenants of the estate, men who had paid their rents into Eustace’s ledger across thirty years. The pall over the coffin carried the bend-and-bucks of the 1586 grant of arms, embroidered at the four corners by the household women in the weeks before. Frances Wingate’s stepdaughters had attended. George Senior’s young children — by then probably three or four small children, with more to come — were in the household behind. The Wingate cousins had come over from Lockleys. The Bedfordshire connections — the Langhornes through Eustace’s daughter Lettice; the Denton cousins from Barton; the Pigotts from Gravenhurst — had come up from their respective seats for the funeral.

The curate of Wymondley Parva — the Commonwealth-era minister whose name the project has not yet fully recovered — read the Directory office at the church door, then led the procession inside for the Cromwellian burial service. The Book of Common Prayer was still suspended; the Restoration was two years away. The chancel was opened. Eustace was lowered into the prepared grave under the flagstones near his father and his grandfather. The mourners returned to the priory for the funeral meal.

The country at the moment of Eustace’s burial was at the precise hinge between the Cromwell years and the Restoration. The Protectorate, which had begun with Oliver Cromwell’s installation as Lord Protector in December 1653, was approaching its end without anyone yet knowing it. Cromwell himself would die at Whitehall on the third of September 1658 — about six months after Eustace was buried, depending on exactly when in early 1658 the funeral fell. Cromwell’s son Richard would succeed him as Protector and would fail at the office within nine months. The army would break with Richard in the spring of 1659. The Long Parliament — the same body that had been sitting in some form since November 1640 — would be re-assembled in May 1659. General George Monck would march south from Scotland in January 1660 with his army of occupation. The Convention Parliament would meet in April 1660. Charles II would be invited to return. He would land at Dover on the twenty-fifth of May 1660. The Restoration would be a fact.

George Senior in 1658 could not have foreseen any of this with confidence, though he could feel that the Cromwellian regime was running out of governing capacity. The country was tired of the army, tired of the Major-Generals, tired of the religious commissions, tired of the unsettled constitutional question. The Hertfordshire gentry — the ones who had taken sides in the 1640s and the ones, like the Nedhams of Wymondley, who had not — were tired with the rest of the country. The priory at Little Wymondley in the spring of 1658 was sitting, with the rest of England, in the long pause before whatever came next.

What came next, for the family, was a settled inheritance.

George Senior took up the headship without difficulty. He had been preparing for it for fifteen years. The estate was substantially in his hands already, through the inter vivos settlements his father had executed across the 1640s and 1650s. The tenants knew him. The bailiff — Robert Wilkinson’s nephew, the second-generation Hitchin man who had been at the work since his uncle’s death in the early 1650s — knew him. The Wymondley Parva curate knew him. The Wingate cousins knew him. The Bedfordshire alliances knew him. He stepped into the role with the working ease of a man who had been doing the work in his father’s name for a decade and a half.

He had married, by 1658, a woman named Barbara — the Barbara of his 1666/67 will, surname not preserved at primary-source level despite multiple search threads. She was the lady of the priory through the next twenty-one years. She bore him seven children across the late 1650s and the 1660s, named in his will: George Junior the heir, Fitch, James, Maurice (the four sons whose feoffment of 1667 would settle their portions), Barbara the eldest daughter (named after her mother), Anne, Elizabeth. Brother Luke — Eustace’s second son from the first marriage, George Senior’s younger brother — was also named in the will, confirming Luke as a working presence in the family through these years.

The chapter is, formally, over at Eustace’s grave in 1658. The Restoration is Chapter 4’s opening. What remains for the chapter is to mark the moment.

George Senior stood in the chancel that spring morning, with the flagstone re-set over his father and with the small mason’s mark of the year 1658 freshly chiselled into the stone (a customary mark, set by the mason at the time of burial rather than by a later monument), and looked out at the family gathered around him. His mother-in-law-of-record — the Frances Wingate dowager — was probably dead by 1658, or was living quietly somewhere. His half-sisters Frances and Margaret were grown women. His own wife Barbara stood at his right hand. His own first or second small child — depending on which year of the late 1650s the funeral fell in — was held in the arms of one of the women of the household. His brother Luke was at the chancel rail. The senior tenants stood in the back of the nave with their hats in their hands.

The chapel he stood in was the same chapel that James the Surveyor had taken over in 1538 and adapted for family use, the chapel where John had been baptized in 1524 and George I had been baptized in the 1550s and Eustace in 1595 and George Senior himself in 1618 and where George Senior’s eldest son George Junior had been baptized in the mid-1650s. The same chapel where the bell that John Dyer had cast in 1595 hung in its tower. The same chapel where the 1605 brass to James and John, commissioned by George I, was set on the north wall of the chancel. The same chapel where, eighty-two years later, in the autumn of 1740 — when George Senior himself would be sixty-one years gone and the priory would have been out of the family’s hands for seven years and a strange family named Vanderplank would be living in the converted nave — the brass would still be standing on the wall under the lay impropriator’s right that had once belonged to his family.

He turned, with the rest of the household, and walked out of the chancel. The procession moved back across the priory yard toward the great hall and the funeral meal that waited.

The family had now held the priory for a hundred and twenty years.

The republic was about to end.

The country was about to come back to itself, in a form none of the men standing at Eustace’s grave that spring morning could have entirely predicted. The Charles whose father had been executed at Whitehall on the thirtieth of January 1649 was a thirty-year-old prince-in-exile in the Spanish Netherlands; he had spent ten years on the continent moving between French and Spanish and Dutch courts; he was waiting for the moment to be called home. The moment was two years away. The Established Church that had been suspended since 1645 was about to be restored. The Book of Common Prayer was about to come back. The bishops were about to be reappointed. The lay impropriators of the home counties — including the Wymondley Nedhams — were about to find themselves operating again in the working framework they had been raised in.

The priory house was intact. The chapel was intact. The Wilshamstead tithes were intact. The bend-and-bucks of 1586 were still on the door above the lintel and on the seal of the new head of family. The estate was solvent. The line was intact.

Eustace had calculated correctly. He had kept the priory.

Closing

The closing of this chapter is small.

In the spring of 1658, in the chancel of Little Wymondley parish church, with his father just buried under the flagstones near his ancestors, George Senior — thirty-nine years old, the seventh-generation male head of the family if you count from John of Needham Grange in Derbyshire, the third-generation Wymondley lord by descent if you count from James the Surveyor — stood on the same priory grounds he had been standing on all his life. The same chapel. The same cloister yard. The same tithe barn at the eastern edge of the grounds. The same conduit on the west side, the same dovecote north-west of the moated enclosure, the same orchard with the Spanish chestnut at its centre.

He had been at the priory through the Civil War as a young man. He had paid the taxes on the fly-leaves of his great-great-grandfather’s pay-book. He had written his private household accounts in the same pay-books a decade later. He had watched his father navigate the family through the Commonwealth without taking sides. He had married Barbara — whose surname is somewhere in a parish register the project has not yet found — and had begun his family. He had a son George who was about three years old in 1658, who would be ten years old when Charles II was at his coronation, who would be eighteen when the Test Acts came in, who would be twenty when George Senior wrote his 1666/67 will, who would in due course be Maurice’s elder brother and Barbara Tempest Needham’s uncle. The next generation was already underway.

What he could not yet know, standing in the chancel that spring morning, was that the family had now entered the period that would, in due course, be its last fifty years at the priory.

From Eustace’s death in 1658 forward, the line was on its way out of Wymondley. Not yet visibly. Not yet in any way that anyone in 1658 could have read. The visible measure of the family’s standing was still the priory: the manor, the rectory, the tithes, the arms above the door, the bell in the chapel, the brass on the chancel wall. By the working measure of a Hertfordshire gentry house in 1658 the Wymondley Nedhams were what they had been in 1538 — the lords of the manor of Little Wymondley, second-tier county gentry, comfortable, propertied, settled. They were still the family that had received the priory from a king’s hand.

But the seventeenth-century English gentry was a moving target. The families that survived its convulsions did not always survive its long-term economic and demographic pressures. The family at Wymondley was, by 1658, beginning to contract in ways that would not become visible until the next generation. George Senior would have seven children but the male line would funnel down: the heir George Junior would marry and produce a son George (b. 1672) who would produce only three daughters; the priory would pass through Martha (the youngest daughter) to her husband Thomas Browne (the future Garter King of Arms) in 1733 and out of the family altogether. Maurice — the fourth son of the 1666/67 will, the recipient of the £500 portion — would have a daughter Barbara Tempest Needham in 1685 who would, in October 1713, walk out of the priory yard for the last time and ride east toward London and the Atlantic.

These are Chapter 4’s events.

The chapter ends, then, on its quiet pivot. The war is over. The Commonwealth is approaching its end. The Restoration is two years away. Eustace is buried. The household has returned to the priory for the funeral meal. The new head of family is taking up the work. The Wilshamstead tithes will come in next month, brought by the same carter who has been bringing them since the early 1640s. The Michaelmas rents will be received in September. The harvest will be brought in in August. The bell at Wymondley Parva will ring for morning prayer. The bell at Graveley three-quarters of a mile away will ring for evensong. The conduit-water will run through its stone channel on the kitchen side of the house. The wind in the box-tree hedge will pick up in the late afternoons.

The priory continues.
