In the summer of 1572, in a Hertfordshire field three miles south of Hitchin and a hundred and twenty paces east of the chapel that had once been the priory church, a man named John Nedham sat down at a trestle table that a herald’s clerk had set up that morning under an awning, and signed his own name at the bottom of a folio of vellum.

He was about forty-eight years old. He had been lord of the manor of Little Wymondley for twenty-seven years. He had married twice, had buried one wife and was living with the second, and had twelve children — five sons and seven daughters — across the two marriages, of whom the eldest was a boy of sixteen who would, nine years later, ride into Kent to marry a Beckenham heiress, and the youngest were not yet out of leading-strings. He held a former monastic house and a working estate that produced rents in coin and tithes in kind from arable land in Hertfordshire and parsonage tithes from a parish in Bedfordshire. He held the rectory of Wymondley Parva by lay impropriation. He was, by every visible measure, what the herald in front of him had ridden up from London to verify. He was a gentleman. He was the right kind of gentleman to be one.

The herald in front of him was Robert Cooke, Clarenceux King of Arms.

Cooke had taken up the Clarenceux office five years before, in 1567, and would hold it until his death in 1593 — a quarter-century of riding from county to county auditing the gentry of half the realm. Clarenceux had jurisdiction over the southern province of England, every county south of the River Trent; the northern counties were the province of Norroy, his colleague. The two of them, between them, were the Crown’s working census-takers of the English gentry class. They came armed with letters patent under the Great Seal of Elizabeth I and with the authority to summon every man in the realm who called himself a gentleman, and to demand that he prove, in writing and to the herald’s satisfaction, his right to do so.

The proof was a pedigree. Drawn out on paper or vellum, prepared in advance by the gentleman himself or by a kinsman or by a clerk he had retained for the purpose, the pedigree was a written genealogy showing the gentleman’s descent through the male line back as far as the evidence permitted, with marriages noted, daughters’ alliances recorded, and children’s ages given. It listed the coat of arms the family had borne and any earlier coat it had borne before. It was sworn to by the head of family in front of the herald and signed by him as a statement of truth. The herald either confirmed it under his own hand and seal — meaning he accepted it as good — or rejected it as false or unfounded, in which case the gentleman’s children would be disclaimed at the next visitation as not gentry at all.

The legal stakes were substantial. A family disclaimed by the heralds lost the right to call its head an Esquire, to bear arms over a door or on a seal or on a tomb, to take precedence in church, and — at the more punitive edge — to be admitted to certain professions and to certain levels of office in the county. The visitation was the gentry’s own audit, conducted on the gentry’s behalf, by their fellow gentlemen working under royal warrant.

John Nedham had prepared his pedigree carefully.

The clerk had written it out on the page in front of him in the formal hand the heralds used for fair copy. At the top, anonymous and unornamented, the dynastic anchor: Nedam of Darbishire. The name with which the family had begun and to which it still looked. Below it, the first named man: James Nedam of Chislest in Kent, married to Alice, daughter of Merry of Hatfield. James was John’s father — the Surveyor of the King’s Works under Henry VIII, the man who had received Wymondley from the Crown in 1538, the man who had built the tithe barn three miles down the road in 1541 and had died at Boulogne in 1544. The clerk had recorded him with his Kentish residence as Chiselhurst, where James had held a house before Wymondley was granted; the Wymondley seat itself was not noted in the 1572 pedigree, presumably because the herald already knew where he was sitting that afternoon. The pedigree was a record of antecedents. The present was what the herald could see for himself.

Below James and Alice came John, the man at the table.

He was recorded with both his wives. Ann, daughter of William Coppin of Canterbury, 1 wife.

Jane, daughter of Weldishe of Cranbroke in Kent, 2 wife. The first wife was named with her father’s full name and the Canterbury seat; the second with her father’s surname and the Kent town. The first wife was dead by 1572 — Anne Coppin had given John five or six of his children and had died sometime in the 1560s; the chapter cannot precisely date the death because the Little Wymondley parish register before 1583 has not survived. Jane Weldish was alive that summer, somewhere in the priory house, possibly with the younger children. She would outlive John by several years and would die a widow at Wymondley in the early seventeenth century, after raising not only her own children but her stepchildren as well.

A note in the right-hand margin of the pedigree recorded the existence of John’s younger brother William Nedam of London, draper — a man who had taken his portion in trade rather than in land, joined the Drapers’ Company in the City, and lived out his life in the cloth trade in the capital. William was not at Wymondley that summer. He was at his counting-house in London and would not be summoned by the Clarenceux herald, because the City of London was its own jurisdiction and its gentry were registered under separate visitation arrangements. He was named in the pedigree as a courtesy to the documentary record. The two brothers had remained in contact across the divide between county and capital — gentry and trade — that ran through every Tudor family with a younger son.

Below John and his two wives came the twelve children, listed by the clerk in two clusters separated by their mothers, with the boys’ ages noted because boys’ ages mattered for inheritance. By the first wife Anne Coppin: George the eldest, sixteen years old, son and heir; then in tangled order Coppin Nedam aged thirteen, Thomas aged eleven, John the younger aged seven; and the daughters Anne, Gilian, Bridgett, Mary, Elizabeth, the second Anne, Margaret, Jane. Daughter Anne — the eldest — had already been married, to a Thomas Pole, younger son of one of the Poles of London; her marriage had taken place before the visitation and had brought the family its first London-merchant alliance. The other seven daughters were not yet married. Some were children. Some were on the cusp of marriageable age. All of them would, in the next twenty years, be matched to gentry families of the home counties — Bedfordshire, Northamptonshire, Hertfordshire, Kent — in the pattern that minor county gentry used to extend their kin-network outward without dispersing the family seat.

The arms came next. The clerk had drawn them in pen-and-ink trick — the heraldic shorthand by which an artist of small skill but precise hand could indicate the tinctures of a coat without needing to apply color. Azure, on a chevron between three escallops Argent as many acorns Vert, on a chief crenellée Or three martlets Gules. In plain English: a blue shield, charged with a silver chevron on which sat three green acorns; on either side of the chevron, silver escallop shells; and across the top of the shield, an embattled gold border on which stood three red martlets, the small heraldic birds with no feet, traditional emblems of the younger sons of an armigerous house. The crest, drawn in a wreath above the shield: a dolphin naiant Or, a gold dolphin swimming horizontally. The arms were the family’s identity in visible form. They would appear over doorways, on seal-rings, on plate, on the cover of books, on funeral hatchments, on tomb stones for the next three and a half centuries.

A small note, in Cooke’s own hand or in his clerk’s, was appended at the head of the entry: Vide Cla. Cook’s guift for another coat. See Clarenceux Cook’s gift for another coat. The herald had brought with him, in his saddlebags, a separate document — a draft grant of arms in his own name, ready for the family’s eventual taking-up. The acorns-and-martlets coat was the family’s older blazon, the one James had used or that James’s son Christopher had used before him. Cooke was offering a second coat, fresh and distinct, granted on the family’s contemporary standing rather than confirmed on its inherited claim. The second coat would not be formally registered for another fourteen years; in the summer of 1572, Cooke recorded only the intention. The family declined to take it up immediately. The reason for the fourteen-year delay is not preserved in any surviving record. It may have been money: a grant of arms cost a fee, sometimes a substantial one, and a man with twelve children and a recently-rebuilt parish church was a man whose ready cash was committed.

It may have been deliberation: the older coat was the one Cooke was confirming on the day of the visitation, and the family may have wanted to think before changing its visible identity.

Whatever the reason, in the summer of 1572 the family carried the acorns and the martlets. The dolphin swam above the shield. The shield went up at the gable end of the priory house and was carved in stone above the principal door. The same arms were impressed in the wax of John’s seal whenever he sent a letter or witnessed a deed.

At the bottom of the page the clerk stopped writing.

He turned the vellum across the trestle so the writing faced the man on the other side. He set a quill across the inkstone, and he set the inkstone — a small flat stone of black slate, refilled that morning from a clay bottle — within John’s right hand’s easy reach. He stood back a half-step. The herald, sitting just behind the clerk on a stool brought out from the house, said the words the heralds said at this point in the procedure — that the pedigree as written was the petitioner’s own statement, given under his own hand and seal, and that the petitioner now subscribed it as the truth. The petitioner picked up the quill. He dipped it. He set the tip to the vellum.

By me JOHN NEDHAM.

He signed it in the careful late-Tudor hand his father had taught him forty years earlier in a London schoolroom and that he had been practicing on accounts and indentures and parish-vestry minutes ever since. The capital letters had a small finishing flourish; the m of me and the e of NEDHAM descended slightly below the line. The signature occupied an inch and a quarter of the bottom edge of the page. The ink was the standard iron-gall mixture every clerk used in 1572: it would still be legible on the same page four hundred and fifty years later, when his nine-times-great-grandson’s nine-times-great-grandson sat in a house in Alabama and read it on a screen. There was no way to know that. He sealed the entry by pressing his ring into a small puddle of red wax — the dolphin-and-chevron seal, the family’s old blazon — and waited while the clerk dusted the ink with pounce and folded the vellum back to its register-place.

The herald accepted the entry. The visitation court moved on to the next family.

John Nedham stood up. He thanked Cooke for his attention, with the courtesies that the gentleman-class used to its own working professionals. He walked back across the priory yard toward the house. His eldest son George, sixteen, met him at the chapel door and asked, in the manner of a son who had been waiting for an outcome, whether everything had gone as expected. It went, John said. We are recorded.

It went, indeed. The signature he had set on the page that afternoon was the family’s documented existence beginning at primary-source level. Everything in this book that comes before that signature is reconstructed from older traditions, from his father’s surviving pay-books, from the architectural fabric of the priory, from the wills that survive of his more distant cousins. Everything that comes after the signature, every birth and marriage and death and inheritance and quarrel and emigration that this book is going to follow across the next hundred and forty-two years, sits inside a documentary frame that begins, in concrete public record, on a Hertfordshire summer afternoon in 1572 with a man pressing a quill to vellum at a trestle table in his own priory yard.

This is his story, and what came of it.

James to Christopher to John: the inheritance generation

John Nedham was born in or about 1524 in London.

The date is inferred. No baptismal record survives — the parish register of any London church that might have recorded a London-born Nedham child of the early 1520s has either been lost or has not been searched, and the project’s working hypothesis rests on the documented chronology of his father’s career and his own subsequent life. James Nedeham, John’s father, was in London continuously from about 1517, when he completed his Carpenters’ Company apprenticeship, through 1532, when he was appointed Surveyor of the King’s Works. James married Alice Merry of Hatfield in Hertfordshire sometime in the 1510s or early 1520s — the 1572 pedigree names her father’s surname as Merry, the 1634 pedigree as Goodere, a discrepancy that may reflect a remarriage on her mother’s side or a simple sixty-year-later misremembering and that the project flags as an open documentary question. John was the eldest surviving son. He was old enough by April 1544 to be named as a joint grantee with his father in a Hertfordshire land purchase at Bygrave (TNA LR 14/468), which placed him at about twenty in that year. Working backward from twenty, he was born about 1524.

He spent his boyhood in London. James’s working life through the 1520s and the early 1530s was a London life — a London carpenter’s-yard life through his twenties, a London royal-buildings life from 1531 onward. The Nedeham household was somewhere in the City. The exact parish is not preserved in the surviving record. John would have been baptized at a London church on the eighth day after his birth, in the standard Tudor practice, and he would have learned to read at a London grammar school in the late 1530s. The grammar in question is not documented, but two plausible candidates exist: St Paul’s, the elite Mercers’ school refounded by Dean Colet in 1509, where the sons of City freemen and rising royal servants alternated on the benches; or the Carpenters’ Company school, which the Company operated for the apprentices and the sons of its members. James was a Carpenters’ Company member, a warden in 1515 and 1533 and a Master in 1534, 1535, and 1542. If the Company school took the sons of senior wardens, John may have been there.

What is documented is that by 1538, when his father was granted Wymondley Priory and the family began to make the priory into a country seat, John was about fourteen — old enough to ride out to Hertfordshire with his father and to begin to think of the priory as the family’s eventual home rather than as one of his father’s many working projects. He may have spent the years 1538 to 1544 dividing his time between London and Wymondley: at the City house with his mother and the younger children when his father was in town; up at the priory in the long Hertfordshire summers when the works at Westminster slackened and the household decamped to the country. He may, at fourteen or fifteen, have ridden over from Wymondley to Hitchin on market days to look at the cattle and the cloth-stalls and the gathered farmers of the Broadwater Hundred. He may have stood in the south doorway of the priory chapel — the chapel that was, in James’s hands, becoming the family’s burial church — and watched his father’s masons cut Tudor windows into medieval lancets. The project record does not preserve these things specifically; the boy’s life inside the man’s working life is one of the silences in the documentary record. What can be said is that John spent his late boyhood and early adolescence in the transition between his father’s London identity and the family’s Hertfordshire one. He came of age, in the most literal sense, at the moment the family was changing what kind of family it was.

His father died on 22 September 1544.

James was at Boulogne with Henry VIII’s army, in the camp outside the recently-captured French city, eight days after the city had fallen. The cause of death has not been recovered from the surviving record. Tudor military camps were lethal places even in victory; dysentery, plague, gangrene from minor wounds, and the typhus that travelled in army cloth all killed more soldiers in the 1540s than the fighting did. James was buried in the church of Our Lady at Boulogne, in a foreign country, on a campaign his eldest son had not been able to follow him on because the eldest son had been left in England as the family representative for the interim. He was fifty-four. He had been Surveyor for twelve years all but eight days. The news of his death travelled back from Boulogne by Crown courier to London and from London by household messenger to Wymondley.

John, twenty years old, heard it sometime in the first week of October 1544 in the great hall of the priory.

The succession was not automatic. James had foreseen this and had taken precautions. Before he sailed for France he had obtained a royal licence to entail the manor of Little Wymondley on John — meaning that the estate was settled by deed to descend to John and his heirs, rather than passing under the ordinary rules of inheritance through the Court of Wards. The Court of Wards was the Crown agency that supervised the inheritances of men who held land of the king by knight-service, which James’s Wymondley grant had been; without the licence to entail, the Court would have taken John as a Crown ward (he was an under-age heir at his father’s death, by the operative definition of under-age in the Court’s procedure, which counted men under twenty-one) and would have administered the estate during the minority. The licence sidestepped that. It allowed John to enter the freehold once the Court of Wards had verified the entail’s terms — a process that took the better part of a year, between September 1544 and the summer of 1545.

In 1545 John entered.

He was twenty-one, possibly twenty-two, depending on which month the entail-execution ran through. He had a stepmother in the household — Alice Merry, James’s widow, who was probably still alive in 1545; her death is not documented and may have come at any point between 1545 and the 1570s. He had at least four younger siblings: his sister Mary, whose marriage to John Parker, Counsellor at Law of Radwell, would in due course be a marriage of legal and gentry significance; his brother William, the future London draper; and at least two other siblings, whose names and life-courses are obscured by the gaps in the documentary record. The household at Wymondley in the autumn of 1545 was a household in transition. The Surveyor was dead. The young heir was the head. The country was at war with France and with Scotland simultaneously. The king on the throne would be dead in two years. The young heir would have to make every decision about what kind of country house Wymondley would now be — what kind of household, what kind of estate, what kind of family standing — without the man whose office and reputation had made the family’s standing possible in the first place.

He was, suddenly, the inheritance generation.

He had not asked for it. He had inherited a working priory, a working tithe barn (then four years old), a working set of agricultural lands, the rectory and tithes of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire, a wood in South Mimms, a recent property in Bygrave, and the legal style of Esquire of Wymondley. He had inherited his father’s reputation — both the favorable side of it (the man who had built the king’s palaces and had been a household name in the King’s Works office) and the more complicated side (the man who had been close to Cromwell in the consequential summer of 1532, the man who had personally overseen the stripping of the lead from the dissolved monasteries, the man whose Wymondley priory had until eight years earlier been a working Augustinian house with its own canons singing the offices). The inheritance was unambiguous as a matter of property and ambiguous as a matter of public character. John would have to decide, over the next four and a half decades, what to keep and what to set aside.

What he kept, mostly, was the property. What he set aside, mostly, was the dust of his father’s career as a Crown servant. He did not himself enter Crown service. He did not, so far as the record shows, seek office at Court. He did not ride to musters or sit on commissions of the peace at the highest county level; he served, when he was called, on more local instruments — parish vestry, hundred boards, the occasional commission. He set the family’s center of gravity firmly in Hertfordshire, in the priory, in the parish, in the surrounding gentry. He was, in the modern phrase, the man who took the founder’s gains and put them on a steady track.

He also got married. Twice. — — —

The two wives

The first wife was Anne Coppin of Canterbury.

She was the daughter of William Coppin, gentleman, of the parish of St Margaret in Canterbury — a member of the small Kentish gentry circle that the Coppins inhabited in the middle decades of the sixteenth century, neither at the level of the great Kentish houses (the Sidneys at Penshurst, the Wyatts at Allington) nor down among the yeoman freeholders, but in the comfortable in-between in which a gentleman held a town house, a small country property, and a body of leases in the cathedral city’s prebendal estates. The Coppins of Canterbury were a Kentish merchant-gentry family of the kind that had risen during the dissolution of the cathedral monastery in 1540 to take up some of the secular tenancies that the monks had previously held. They were not a great catch in the high-Tudor marriage market. They were, however, a perfectly respectable one for the heir of Wymondley.

The marriage took place sometime in the late 1540s. The exact date has not been recovered; no record of it survives in the Canterbury cathedral or parish archives that have been searched at the present writing. The marriage was very probably arranged through the Kent connection that James Nedeham had maintained from his pre-Wymondley years — James had held a house at Chiselhurst in Kent in the early 1540s, and the 1572 pedigree records his Kent residence rather than his Hertfordshire one, suggesting that the Kent connection had remained operative until the end of his life. James, before he died, may have proposed the Coppin match for his eldest son. Or the match may have come about through John’s own visits to Kent in his early twenties, perhaps at his father’s surviving connections. The mechanism is recoverable only by inference; what is documented is that the bride was Anne Coppin and that John acquired through her a small dowry, a Kentish connection, and a wife.

Anne brought him five or six children. The 1572 pedigree, drawn up after her death, divides John’s twelve children into two clusters by their two mothers and gives the first cluster as the elder. George Nedam, son and heir, then sixteen; born therefore about 1556. Coppin Nedam — named for his mother’s family, in the Tudor practice of carrying the maternal surname forward as a child’s Christian name — then thirteen, born about 1559. Thomas Nedam then eleven, born about 1561. The other named child of the first marriage is Anne, the eldest daughter, who had already been married off to Thomas Pole of London by the time of the 1572 visitation. There may have been additional children of Anne Coppin who died in infancy and who are not in the pedigree’s roster because the pedigree recorded only the survivors. The infant mortality of mid-Tudor England was substantial. It would have been remarkable for a marriage of ten or twelve years’ duration to produce only the four children the pedigree records as Anne Coppin’s; the working assumption of family historians of the period is that one in three children born did not reach their seventh birthday. The Coppin–Nedham line probably ran to seven or eight births of which five or six survived to be named in 1572.

Anne died sometime between 1561 (the birth of Thomas) and 1565 (when the second-wife cluster begins). The probable midpoint is about 1563. The cause of her death is not preserved. Childbirth was the most common single cause of death for women in their thirties in Tudor England — the puerperal fevers, the obstructed labors, the slow blood-loss of a poorly resolved pregnancy all killed a substantial fraction of married Englishwomen between the ages of twenty-five and forty. The likelihood is that Anne died in or shortly after childbirth, possibly carrying a child who did not survive her. She was buried at Little Wymondley parish church, in the chapel that had been the priory church, in the cloister or under the chancel; the exact spot is no longer marked, the priory church’s interior having been substantially refigured in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and again in the nineteenth, with any surviving Tudor monuments either re-set, plastered over, or removed. The 1605 monument that John’s son George I would erect in the chancel commemorates James and John but not Anne; the family’s pre-1605 dead remained unmemorialized, in the post-Reformation Protestant convention of avoiding monumental display for the soul’s benefit and reserving it for posterity’s instruction.

The second wife came two or three years later. She was Jane Weldish of Cranbrook in Kent.

Cranbrook is a small Wealden town in southwest Kent, about thirty miles south of Canterbury and forty south of the priory at Chiselhurst. The Weldish family — sometimes spelled Welbish or Welbisshe in earlier records — were minor Cranbrook gentry of the cloth-making class. The town of Cranbrook in the sixteenth century was at the heart of the Kent broadcloth trade, the high-end of the English cloth industry that produced the best lengths of dyed-in-the-wool fabric for the European market. The Weldish family had risen through cloth, like several Wealden families before them, and held property in and near the town. They were not Canterbury-gentry; they were Wealden cloth-gentry; the distinction was small but real. Jane was probably a generation younger than Anne Coppin had been; she may have been a young woman of twenty or twenty-two when John married her in the mid-1560s.

The two Kent marriages, taken together, made the Nedhams a Kent-Hertfordshire family in a particular and specific way. Their first three marriages — James to Alice of Hatfield, John to Anne of Canterbury, John to Jane of Cranbrook — had all been into the home counties broadly, with a marked preference for Kent.

James’s hand was probably in the first two; the third was John’s own choice, perhaps using the Kent contacts his father and his first wife had bequeathed him. The Kent connection persisted into the next generation. It would not, however, become the family’s defining feature; the next-generation match (George the heir’s marriage at Beckenham in 1581) would be a Kent marriage too, but after that the family would turn inward toward Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire, marrying its neighbors rather than its more distant correspondents. The Kent phase of the Wymondley Nedhams was a function of James’s pre-Wymondley life, carried over into John’s first two marriages by the inertia of established contact, and quietly dropped after George.

Jane Weldish gave John his second cluster of children. By the 1572 pedigree’s roster: a son named John the younger, then seven years old — born therefore about 1565, very close to the start of the second marriage — and a daughter named Mary, plus the additional daughters Elizabeth, the second Anne, Margaret, Jane. Gilian and Bridgett may also have been Jane’s; the pedigree’s ordering of the daughters does not perfectly disambiguate them by mother. The 1634 visitation, drawn up sixty-two years later when the herald’s clerk no longer had John or Jane to consult, would record the children of John’s marriages in a different order and would name a son James who is not in the 1572 list, along with the now-famous Sir John of Lichborough — both apparently of the second marriage. The 1634 list has the structural defect of being drawn from family memory rather than from John’s own deposition; the 1572 list, signed by John himself, is the primary anchor.

What needs to be noted, even at the risk of getting ahead of the narrative, is one specific child of the second marriage. The 1634 pedigree, identifying the brothers of the 1581 Beckenham groom (the heir George), names Sir John Needham of Lichborough, co. Northampton, Kt., Pensioner to Q. Elizabeth, as one of John of Wymondley’s children. The 1572 pedigree lists a John Nedam, is 7 yeres old — born about 1565 — among the children of the second marriage; but the 1572 pedigree does not name a son James, who appears in the 1634 list as another of John’s children. The two pedigrees disagree on the exact roster. The most economical reconciliation is that the 1572 list is incomplete (very young children sometimes were not listed if they had just been born, or if they were felt to be unlikely to survive) and that James and the second John of the 1634 list are the same generation but were either born very late in the 1572 visitation year, after the herald had recorded the roster, or in the late 1570s. Sir John of Lichborough must have been one of these. He grew up to be the family’s only courtly figure; he was knighted by Elizabeth I; he became one of Her Majesty’s Gentleman Pensioners; he married a sister of Sir Lewis Watson of Rockingham, the future Baron Rockingham. He is the topic of §2.6.

For the present, the central fact about Jane Weldish is simply that she was the second wife and that she carried, through her remaining children, half of the next generation of the family. She would outlive John, who died in 1591 or 1592 — the exact date is unrecovered; he was buried at Little Wymondley at some point in the early 1590s — and she would die at Wymondley sometime in the next decade. She would have lived to see George marry at Beckenham in 1581. She would have lived to see Sir John knighted at Court. She would have lived to see the Spanish Armada and to hear of its defeat. She would have lived to see at least three of her stepdaughters marry into Bedfordshire houses (the Mordaunts of Northill, the Pigotts of Gravenhurst, the Langhornes of Bedfordshire). She would have died sometime around 1600 — old enough to be a grandmother several times over, by then with grandchildren growing up in the same priory house in which she had raised her children.

There was also the third connection. The marriage of the eldest daughter, Anne.

Anne Nedham, John’s eldest daughter by Anne Coppin, had already been married off to Thomas Pole, 2 son of Thomas Pole of London, by the time of the 1572 visitation. The marriage had taken place perhaps in 1569 or 1570 — Anne would have been about fifteen or sixteen, the standard upper age for a first gentry marriage of a daughter without an estate to inherit. Thomas Pole was a younger son of the Pole family of London, a minor branch of the much larger Pole connection that had stood at the highest levels of mid-Tudor politics until the executions of the elder Plantagenet Poles in 1538 and 1539. The London Poles of the 1570s were a respectable merchant-gentry line, second cousins or further removed from the great Poles, with the political stain of the older generation receding into the past. Anne Pole was the first Nedham daughter to be married off in the new generation. Her marriage settlement is not preserved. The Pole connection is recorded in both the 1572 and the 1634 visitations and seems to have been a settled, working alliance; the line continued forward into the early seventeenth century, after which the project record loses track of it.

Anne’s marriage was the first piece of evidence that John’s daughters would be married well. It set the template for what the daughters’ lives would look like. We shall come back to those lives — to the eight of them — in the section after the next. — — —

Twelve children at the visitation

The list of children that the clerk wrote out at John’s dictation on that summer afternoon in 1572 was, in formal terms, simply a roster — a paragraph of names and ages for the herald’s record. In substantive terms it was a generational portrait of a Tudor gentry family at the moment of its inherited consolidation.

George the eldest, sixteen.

He was already, in 1572, what the family expected him to become: the heir, the son being prepared to step into his father’s place, the young man whose marriage and whose property would carry the line forward. He had been born about 1556, twelve years after his grandfather James had died at Boulogne and eleven years after his father had entered the freehold of the priory. He had grown up entirely at Wymondley — the first generation of the family to be a Wymondley native rather than a London transplant. The chapel where he had been baptized was the same chapel his great-grandfather Christopher would have used had Christopher ever come up from Derbyshire to see his son’s new estate, which Christopher had probably not done. The cloister yard he had played in as a small boy was the same yard the canons had walked in for three hundred and thirty years before the dissolution. He had been to the parish school at Little Wymondley, perhaps with private tutors at the priory for the higher Latin. He had ridden to Hitchin many times — three miles down the road, the nearest town, the place where Hertfordshire countrymen went for market day on Tuesdays and Saturdays. He had been to London once or twice with his father, on the road his grandfather had used a half-century earlier. He was sixteen. He could read Latin slowly. He could ride. He could not yet, perhaps, manage an estate; but he was being prepared.

He was also, in the way of all eldest sons in the period, the family’s living guarantee that the property would not divide. Under Tudor inheritance law a substantial gentry estate held in tail male passed entire to the eldest surviving son, with the younger sons receiving portions in cash or in smaller subsidiary holdings. The system was harsh on the younger sons but it preserved the integrity of the estate generation by generation. George at sixteen embodied that system. He would, in the fullness of time, inherit Wymondley entire. His brothers Coppin and Thomas and John the younger would receive portions, would take wives, would make their own way through Crown service, the Inns of Court, the City companies, the army, or as cadet branches on smaller estates a few miles distant. The eldest son would carry the seat. The younger sons would carry the surname outward.

The eldest son’s central role in 1572 was the marriage market. He was sixteen; he was not yet of marriage age in the strict legal sense (twenty-one for full majority); but a heir’s matrimonial future was an asset to be deployed within the next five years, and the family had already begun thinking about it. Marriageable daughters of suitable gentry houses were inquired after through the kin-network. The Kent connection was still operative — through his stepmother Jane Weldish of Cranbrook and through his uncle William the London draper, whose City contacts reached down into the Kent broadcloth trade. The Hertfordshire neighbors had daughters. The Bedfordshire houses had daughters. The London merchant families had daughters whose dowries were good and whose fathers wanted gentry connections. In due course, nine years after the visitation, one of these searches would resolve into the Beckenham marriage of September 1581. We will get to it in §2.5.

The other sons.

Coppin Nedam, thirteen. He carried his mother’s maiden name as his Christian name, which was a Tudor convention for honoring a maternal house when the boy was not the heir. He would, in the fullness of time, take his portion, perhaps £200 or £300 in cash, and would make his own life. The project record loses sight of Coppin after the visitation. He may have died young; he may have gone into trade in London like his uncle William; he may have moved to Kent and worked in the Coppin connections there. He may simply have stayed at the priory as the unmarried second son, helping George manage the estate. The 1634 pedigree does not name a Coppin Nedam descent, which suggests that whatever path he took, it produced no male heirs known to the herald of 1634.

Thomas Nedam, eleven. The same fate, in documentary terms — the 1634 pedigree does not carry a Thomas Nedam descent forward. He may have died young; he may have lived without issue; he may have been a clergyman or a soldier; he may have gone to one of the Inns of Court and lived out his life in London. The project record loses track of him as well.

John Nedam the younger, seven. This is the second John of the family — Christian-name reuse was standard practice in Tudor families and produced the documentary confusion that family historians spend their careers unraveling. The second John was almost certainly Sir John of Lichborough — the eventual Queen’s Pensioner, the only one of the four sons who would rise above the level of country gentleman. The chronology fits: a boy of seven in 1572 would be in his early twenties by the late 1580s, the standard age for a junior Court appointment as a Pensioner-on-probation. By 1592, when he is recorded as one of the family of John of Wymondley in the year of his father’s death, he would be twenty-seven — a Pensioner in good standing. The full story of Sir John is §2.6.

The eight daughters.

The 1572 pedigree’s roster of the daughters preserves their names but not their ages and not their order of birth. Eight is a substantial number for any single household, even by the standards of a fertile late-Tudor gentry family. The Nedham household at Wymondley in 1572 — with twelve children in residence, two living wives’ worth of mothers’ kin to maintain contact with, a working priory estate to manage, and the comings and goings of seven young women between roughly the ages of eight and twenty — was a large, busy, sometimes loud establishment. The young women each had to be educated. The young women each had to be matched to a husband. The young women each represented, for the family, an outward extension of its kin-network and, for themselves, a future that was being decided largely by their father at a series of negotiations they were not in the room for.

What the daughters’ lives would be — this is worth pausing for, because the daughters’ lives are the dimension of the family that the chapter’s documentary apparatus is poorest at recovering. The eight daughters who were listed in the pedigree as Anne, Gilian, Bridgett, Mary, Elizabeth, the second Anne, Margaret, and Jane — the same eight whose fates are traced in part by the 1634 pedigree, with new alliances added that the 1572 court could not have known — would all, with the exception of the ones who died young, be married off into other gentry families of the home counties over the next twenty years. The pattern was the standard one for the period. A daughter was a marriageable asset for whose disposition the father was responsible. The dowry was the negotiating instrument. The bride and the groom met formally a small number of times before the engagement was settled; the engagement was settled by the fathers, with the mothers present or not depending on the household; the marriage took place at the bride’s parish church or at the groom’s, on a date that worked for both families’ calendars; and the bride moved into her husband’s house, taking with her a small chest of household goods that would have been called her paraphernalia in the legal Latin and her gear in the parlor.

The 1634 pedigree’s later naming of these daughters’ marriages preserves five of the eight. Anne — the eldest, the Pole bride — was already in 1572 in her new household. Jane Nedham was married to William Warren, alias Bygrave — a sub-tenant of one of the Wymondley properties James had acquired in 1544; the marriage may have been a local-tenant alliance that consolidated the family’s smaller holdings. Margaret Nedham was married to Robert Langhorne, Esq., of Bedfordshire — a Bedfordshire gentleman of the Langhorne family who held estates near Bedford. Bridgett Nedham was married to Benjamin Pigott, Esq., of Gravenhurst in Bedfordshire — a Pigott of the long-established Bedfordshire gentry house. Elizabeth Nedham was married to Sir John Fern, Knight — possibly the same Sir John Fern who served in the Privy Council under Elizabeth I and James I and whose seat was in Lincolnshire, though the identification is not certain. Jane the second was married to Lewes Mordaunt of Northill in Bedfordshire — a Mordaunt of the Bedfordshire branch of the Mordaunts of Drayton, a connection that would deepen in the next generation. Gilian and the second Anne and Mary are not named in the 1634 pedigree as having married, which probably means they had died young or had not married at the time the 1634 herald spoke to the family.

Five of the eight daughters, then, married into Bedfordshire houses. The Bedfordshire connection — the Mordaunts, the Pigotts, the Langhornes — was the dominant marital direction of the next twenty years. It is also the same direction the family’s tithe income flowed from: James’s 1538 grant had included the rectory of Wilshamstead in Bedfordshire, and the family was drawing tithes from Bedfordshire continuously from then through the 1660s. The 1572 daughters’ marriages would deepen the family’s Bedfordshire footprint at the kin-network level, complementing the working economic ties that were already in place. Five of John’s daughters would, in the next twenty-five years, be in Bedfordshire houses; their husbands would be at the Wymondley table on family occasions; their children would grow up calling the Nedham cousins their cousins, and would themselves marry into the next generation of Bedfordshire and Hertfordshire gentry alliance.

The girls had, before their marriages, the priory yard. They had the cloister, which by 1572 had been an enclosed family courtyard for thirty-five years and which functioned as the daughters’ working outdoor space — for needlework in fair weather, for the airing of linen, for the kitchen-garden vegetables, for the small kitchen-spice plot, for whatever small games girls played within their fathers’

enclosed walls. They had the chapel, where they were baptized and would, if they died young, be buried. They had the kitchens, where they would learn from cook and housekeeper the management of a gentry household. They had the embroidery frame and the linen-chest and the wool-card, all the standard girl’s apparatus of a Tudor gentlewoman in training. They had the riding, which they did with their brothers when they were young and with their grooms in their teens, on roads to Hitchin and to Stevenage and on occasion to the Bedfordshire houses where the cousins lived. They had — most of them — their mother Anne and then their stepmother Jane; they had each other.

What they did not have was any expectation of agency over the central decision of their adult lives. Their husbands would be chosen for them. Their dowries would be negotiated for them. Their married households would be established in their husbands’ counties. Their education was the education of a daughter who would marry; their literacy was real but functional, their music ornamental, their needlework genuinely a working skill. They were prepared to be the mistresses of households. They were not prepared to be anything else.

This is not, in 1572, news. It is the standard arrangement of the gentry life-cycle in the period. It is also worth saying — at the front of this book, before we get any further into the generations — because the women of the Wymondley line are going to recur as a substantial presence across the next hundred and forty years, and the convention by which they recur needs to be named honestly. Most of them will be off the page after their marriages. Most of them will be remembered only by their husbands’ surnames in the heralds’ records, by their birth names in the parish registers, and by their burial dates in the chancel registers — not by any record of their inner lives, their accomplishments outside the domestic, their friendships, their fears, their political opinions, or their personal preferences. The documentary record was not designed to preserve those things, and it does not preserve them. What it preserves is what it was designed to preserve: husbands and dowries and children and deaths.

Eight generations on, in the autumn of 1713, a daughter of this house named Barbara Tempest Needham will do something that none of John’s eight daughters had any way of imagining. She will, at twenty-eight years old, against the entire convention of her sex and class, sail out of Gravesend on a packet bound for South Carolina, alone but for her trunk, on the strength of a three-word letter she has written and posted and that has gone across the Atlantic ahead of her. She will not have been chosen by anyone. She will have chosen.

That is in Chapter 5. It is also, in a longer sense, in this paragraph. The convention from which Barbara breaks is named here. The eight daughters of John Nedham, in the priory yard in 1572, are part of the inheritance she will eventually have to set down. — — —

Robert Cooke comes back

Robert Cooke was, in the summer of 1572, fifty-seven years old. He had been Clarenceux King of Arms for five years and would hold the office for twenty-one more, until his death in 1593. By the standards of his profession he was both careful and generous. He confirmed old coats on slim documentary footing when a family’s local standing seemed plain; he granted new coats to rising houses whose claims he found credible; and he disclaimed, when he had to disclaim, with a brevity that suggested he disliked the work but would not shrink from it. He had risen into the heralds through York Pursuivant in 1562 and Chester Herald in 1566, the conventional ladder, and was respected within the College of Arms as a competent administrator with a particular interest in the gentry of the home counties.

He was also, in a way that mattered for the Wymondley Nedhams that afternoon, a working man with his own pre-occupations. He had to ride from Hertfordshire to the next county the day after he finished at the priory. He had a clerk on a stipend whose hand was beginning to ache from a week of close work. He had ridden up from London on a borrowed horse and would have to ride back south within the month with his vellum register in a leather wallet across his saddlebow. He had not eaten since dawn. He was, like most men in their fifties, conscious that his back hurt when he sat on a trestle bench for too long. The historiography of the heralds tends to render them as embodied institutions; they were also tired men with sore backs.

What is recoverable about Cooke’s reading of the Wymondley case is that he was, by 1572, already disposed to think well of the family. The reasons are conjectural but reasonably solid. James Nedeham was a name Cooke would have known professionally — a Surveyor of the King’s Works whose pay-books were still in the Crown’s hand a generation after his death, a man who had built the king’s palaces and whose grant of Wymondley was on the books of the Court of Augmentations and was in the published printed calendars of Henry VIII’s letters patent. The family was not making it up. The Derbyshire-origin claim at the top of the pedigree — Nedam of Darbishire — could be checked against the visitations of Derbyshire if necessary, and probably had been, at least cursorily. The priory itself was visible to the herald as he rode up the Hitchin road: a working country house with a chapel and a tithe barn and a cloister, on land granted by the Crown thirty-four years before. The local standing was plain. Cooke confirmed the pedigree without difficulty.

He also offered the gift coat. The note on the page — Vide Cla. Cook’s guift for another coat — recorded the offer rather than the acceptance. Cooke had brought with him, evidently, a draft grant of arms that he was prepared to make to the family on his own authority as Clarenceux. The new coat would be visibly different from the old. It would not carry the chevron and the escallops and the acorns and the martlets; it would carry a different scheme entirely. A grant of arms by Clarenceux was a recognized act of new heraldic distinction — the family being given a coat by the office of the king’s herald, rather than confirmed in a coat it had borne before — and it cost a fee that ran, in the 1570s, into several pounds and sometimes substantially more. The fee was the herald’s pension, in effect; visitations were partly self-financing through the grant-fees they generated, and a generous herald who offered grants to families he thought capable of paying them was a herald who was supplementing his own income while extending the formal nobility of the realm.

John declined the grant in 1572 and accepted it fourteen years later. This is the central institutional fact of §2.4. The fourteen-year delay is documented; its causes are not. There are three plausible explanations.

The first is money. A grant of arms cost a substantial fee — the standard 1570s rate, by the records of the College of Arms preserved in later inventories, ran from three or four pounds for a modest grant up to twenty pounds and beyond for one with a knight’s coronet and elaborate quartering. John in 1572 had twelve children to provide for. He had a recently-rebuilt parish church to maintain (the priory’s chancel needed regular re-leading and the small repairs that any large building requires). He had estate works that drew on the surplus. The grant fee, even at the low end, would have been five years’ worth of small expenses. A man with his cash committed to current needs would naturally defer a discretionary expense.

The second is deliberation. The acorns-and-martlets coat was the family’s older blazon. It had been the family’s visible identity for at least a generation, possibly longer; James Nedeham had used some form of it on his seal in the 1530s, and Christopher before him in Derbyshire had used something close, the recovery of which from Derbyshire visitation records remains an open project task. To change the family’s coat — to take up a new one on Cooke’s grant — was to set aside the visible thread back to Derbyshire and to acknowledge a new identity as Hertfordshire gentry of the herald’s own confirming. The older coat said: we are the Derbyshire Nedhams, transplanted by Crown service, with our older identity intact. A Cooke gift coat would say: we are the Wymondley Nedhams, established on our own terms, with a coat of arms freshly granted to us by the office of the king’s herald, anchored to our present standing rather than to our antecedents. John may have wanted to think about which of these the family preferred to be.

The third is the eldest son’s marriage. The 1581 Beckenham marriage of George, when it happened, brought into the family a Kentish heraldic alliance — the Stiles of Langley, themselves an armigerous house — and it may have made sense to defer the new grant until the heir’s marriage was settled and the family’s external profile could be reviewed in its married rather than its bachelor form. Cooke’s grant when it finally came, in 1586, came five years after the Beckenham marriage. The timing may be coincidental; it may be that George at thirty, married for five years, with a wife and first children in residence and a settled position as heir-in-waiting, was the moment the family decided to take up the gift coat.

Whatever the combination of reasons, on 18 February 1586 — the date preserved in Burke’s General Armory in the late-Victorian heraldic compendium that catalogues every recognized English coat — Cooke executed the grant. The new arms were registered in the College of Arms’ own records under his hand and seal. They are recorded in the 1634 visitation as the family’s primary blazon, with the formal note in the herald’s text: This descent and arms testified under the hand and Seale of Robert Cooke, Clarenceux, 1586. The fourteen-year-old offer became, in the spring of 1586, the family’s living heraldry.

The new coat was deliberate.

Argent, on a bend engrailed Azure between two bucks’ heads cabossed Sable, an escallop Or.

In plain English: a silver shield, crossed diagonally from upper-left to lower-right by a blue band with a scalloped edge; the band charged at its center with a single gold escallop shell; flanking the band, in the upper and lower triangles of the shield, two black bucks’ heads facing forward, severed at the neck and shown without the body — cabossed, in the technical heraldic term. Above the shield, the crest: out of a gold pallisado coronet — a small heraldic coronet with upright stakes, a relatively modern Tudor invention that signified neither baronial nor knightly rank but a working-gentry distinction — a black buck’s head, attired (i.e., antlered) in gold.

The escallop survived from the older coat: it had been present, in 1572, as a triplet of silver escallops on the chevron. The new coat kept one of the three escallops and made it the focal charge — promoted, in effect, from three of a kind to the single anchoring symbol. The acorns and martlets were dropped. The chevron was replaced by an engrailed bend. The whole tonal register shifted: the older coat had been a busy, narrative, ornament-rich blazon, telling the family’s story in heraldic shorthand (acorns for an oak-wood origin, martlets for younger sons, escallops for pilgrimage or maritime venture, the embattled chief for a Crown-granted house). The new coat was austere. Two bucks’ heads. A bend. An escallop. The buck’s head as crest. The pallisado coronet. It was the coat of a Hertfordshire gentry house that had decided what it wanted to look like and was no longer carrying inherited iconography.

The new coat went up over the principal door of the priory house. It was carved in stone, painted in tinctures, and set above the lintel. It went onto John’s new seal-ring, which would press the same device into wax for every deed and indenture and letter he sent for the rest of his life. It went onto the family’s new plate, where John had it engraved into the silver of the great gilt cup and the basin and the salt-cellar. It went, eventually, onto the 1605 monument that George the heir, by then George I lord of Wymondley, would erect to his father and grandfather in the chancel of Little Wymondley parish church. The dolphin crest of the old coat fell out of use within a generation; the buck’s head crest of the new one carried the family’s visible identity for the remaining hundred and forty-seven years of the Nedhams’ tenure at Wymondley.

Cooke himself rode out of the Hertfordshire summer of 1586 and went on to the next county. He had eight more years to live. He would do another half-dozen home-counties visitations before his death; he would grant several hundred more coats; he would confirm several thousand pedigrees. The Wymondley grant was, in the scale of his working life, a small piece of business. For the Wymondley Nedhams it was the visual identity of the family fixed on its own terms, by the office of the king’s herald, on a February afternoon in 1586. It would outlast the priory itself. — — —

The marriage at Beckenham, 25 September 1581

In the third week of September 1581, George Nedam — then twenty-five years old, the heir of Wymondley, son and surviving eldest of John and the late Anne Coppin — rode out of the priory yard on the road south.

The road south was the great London road, the Roman way that ran from York to the capital and that passed two miles east of Wymondley through Stevenage and on down through Hatfield to the City. He had a manservant with him, perhaps a groom, perhaps two; he had a packhorse for the wedding clothes and the marriage settlement papers; he had, in a small steel-strapped chest tied to the saddle of the second horse, a quantity of plate that was the Nedham contribution to the bride’s marriage gift — silver cups, a salt-cellar, possibly a basin. The chest had been packed by his stepmother Jane and the household servants under his father’s direction in the days before. The wedding clothes had been made up in London in the early autumn; he had ridden down a fortnight earlier to a tailor in Cheapside for the fitting. The journey from Wymondley to London took two long days on horseback, with a night at one of the standing inns on the road — possibly the George at Hatfield, possibly the Coach and Horses at South Mimms, three miles from the wood his grandfather had bought in 1537. From London he turned east. Beckenham was nine miles south-east of the City, beyond Lewisham, on the Kentish chalk above the Ravensbourne stream. He arrived on the twenty-third or twenty-fourth of September. The wedding was on the twenty-fifth.

The bride was Margaret Stile, daughter of Edmund Stile of Langley in Beckenham. The Stile family had held the manor of Langley — a small estate of about four hundred acres in the parish of Beckenham, on the chalk uplands above the Cray valley — for the better part of a century. They were a Kentish gentry house of the lower-middle tier: armigerous, comfortable, well-connected within the county, with cousins in the City of London and in the cathedral chapter at Canterbury. They were not at the level of the great Kent houses; they were in the working gentry layer below it, which was the same layer the Wymondley Nedhams inhabited in Hertfordshire. The match was practical and balanced. Margaret was not a great heiress and the Nedhams were not a great house. The settlement was the standard one for a gentry first marriage of an inheriting son to a younger daughter of a comparable family — a dowry from the Stiles of perhaps four or five hundred pounds, a jointure secured against the priory’s Wymondley lands in case George predeceased her, a small annuity for her widowhood, the standard provisions for any sons and daughters of the marriage.

The contract had been negotiated by the fathers over the previous winter. Edmund Stile had ridden up to Wymondley in February or March 1581 — the visit is undocumented but is the obvious framing — and had spent perhaps a week in the great hall going through the proposal item by item with John. The dowry value had been argued. The jointure terms had been set. The location of the wedding had been agreed: Beckenham, the bride’s parish church, by the convention that the bride married out of her own house. The date had been chosen for late September: after the harvest was in, before the rains of October made the roads impassable, with the moon waxing toward the full so that the country roads would have light for the night rides. The arrangements had been mailed back and forth across the next six months by carter and London post.

George had been to Beckenham once before the wedding, perhaps twice. The first visit, in the late spring of 1581, had been the formal presentation — Edmund Stile in his hall with his wife and Margaret and the older Stile cousins, George being received as the prospective groom, the conversation careful and exploratory and concerned with whether the bride and the groom were content with each other. The conversation had gone well enough. Margaret was, by the visible measure of her father’s hall, what an heir of Wymondley ought to be marrying: in her early twenties, healthy, well-mannered, dressed in the quiet fashion of the Kent gentry rather than the loud fashion of the Court, capable of reading her psalter and her Book of Common Prayer and of writing a careful letter, accustomed to the management of a household. George was, by the visible measure of the Stile hall, what a Stile daughter ought to be marrying: also in his mid-twenties, also healthy, well-spoken, of an armigerous house with a documented Crown grant in its history, the heir to a working estate.

They had been left, after the formal interview, in the garden of the manor for an hour. The Tudor convention of supervised privacy permitted that much. What they said to each other in that hour is not preserved. Whatever it was, both parties returned to their fathers and indicated assent. The marriage was settled.

The wedding day, when it came, was a working ceremony rather than a state occasion. The church of St George’s at Beckenham had stood on its site since the Norman period; it was a modest parish church with a square tower and a nave under a Tudor roof, with the standard apparatus of pew-rows, font, chancel-screen, and pulpit. It would be rebuilt in the nineteenth century — the building that stands today is largely a 1880s Gothic Revival reconstruction, with only fragments of the older fabric incorporated — but in 1581 it was the small flint-and-stone Wealden church it had been for four centuries. The Stile family had its own pew at the front of the nave. The Stile family had its own monuments in the chancel. The Stile family had paid for the chancel re-leading in the 1570s.

George rode up to the church gate at about ten in the morning. The Stile household met him there. He was received by Margaret’s brother — Margaret’s father Edmund was apparently no longer alive by 25 September 1581, by the silence of the surviving record at the wedding itself; he is named in the visitation pedigrees but not in the marriage register, and the Stile household at the wedding seems to have been led by Margaret’s brother, possibly the John Stile who succeeded to Langley in the early 1580s. The bridal party processed up the path from the gate to the church door. Margaret came out of the church porch with her mother and her sisters; the wedding party combined; the curate of St George’s, who had heard the banns called three Sundays running through August and September, performed the ceremony at the church door in the older convention, then led the parties inside for the blessing at the high altar.

The vows were the standard Tudor vows. The ring was the gold band George had bought in Cheapside the month before. Margaret’s father’s substitute — her brother or her uncle, again the record is silent — gave her to George. They were pronounced man and wife. The register entry was made: 25 Sep 1581, George Nedham, Esq., to Margaret Stile. The transcript was indexed three centuries later by the Findmypast archives and recovered for the project’s documentary base in May 2026 by the author of this book.

After the ceremony, the wedding feast.

It was at Langley Hall, the Stile family seat half a mile away. The dining was substantial — there are no household accounts surviving for the day, but the conventions of a 1580s gentry wedding involved roasted meats from the home park (beef, mutton, possibly venison from a deer-park on the estate), boiled fowl, a great pie or two, autumn vegetables from the kitchen garden, bread baked that morning, and quantities of beer and Kentish cider and, for the head table, claret from the cellar. The wedding party would have been thirty or forty people: the Stile family in full, the Stile cousins from the City and from Canterbury, several of the local Beckenham gentry as neighbors, the curate of St George’s, and on the Wymondley side a small contingent — George’s manservant and his groom, perhaps his younger brother Coppin if Coppin had ridden down with him, perhaps one or two of his Bedfordshire brothers-in-law-to-be if they had come up from their respective seats for the wedding. The musicians played throughout the meal. The speeches were the standard ones. The bride’s brother proposed the standard toast to the groom; the groom proposed the standard toast to the bride; the curate said a brief grace; the company drank.

In the afternoon George and Margaret rode out on the manor’s grounds, together, in the conventional small ceremony of the bridal walk. Late in the day the wedding party broke up; the immediate Stile family went into the house for evening prayers and supper; the Wymondley contingent rode the few miles back to the standing inn at Bromley where George had arranged accommodation; George and Margaret took the principal bedroom of Langley Hall, which had been freshly aired and made up by the housekeeper that morning. They spent the wedding night at her father’s house. The next morning they began the long ride north toward Wymondley.

The journey home took the better part of four days. They paused at London for a day to allow Margaret to be presented to Margaret’s London Stile cousins. They paused again at Stevenage on the second day out of the City, at the same inn the project’s John of Wymondley used when he came down to London on business. On the fourth day, late in the afternoon, they rode up to the priory gates. John Nedham, sixty-three or sixty-four years old in 1581 and not in robust health, met them at the door of the great hall. He embraced his new daughter-in-law with the formal courtesy the period required. He said the words a Tudor patriarch said when his eldest son’s bride first crossed the threshold — words preserved nowhere in the documentary record but standard in the period’s wedding-handbooks: Welcome to the house. Welcome to this family. May you bring it joy and may you bring it sons.

Margaret would do both.

She would bear George Nedham of Wymondley at least three sons over the next two decades — the second John Nedham of Welwyn who would inherit Welwyn as a cadet estate and would in due course be the grandfather of Eustace Nedham of Wymondley Priory, the central figure of Chapter 3; and at least two other named children whose adult lives are recoverable from the 1634 visitation. The marriage was, on the evidence of the next thirty years, a settled and productive one.

Margaret survived George by an unknown number of years (his date of death is not preserved at primary-source level; he was alive in 1605 to commission the monument to his father and grandfather, and was dead by 1626 when his son George I died); she was the lady of the priory through the long Stuart twilight that followed Elizabeth’s death in 1603. The Wymondley arms began to appear, in the 1580s and 1590s, with the Stile arms quartered alongside them, in the heraldic shorthand by which a Tudor gentry house marked its marital alliances. The Stile-Nedham match was the first of those quartered marks. It would not be the last.

The wedding at Beckenham is the small private hinge between the chapter’s first half — the inheritance generation, the two wives, the children at the visitation, the herald’s confirmation — and its second half, the family’s Elizabethan working life. From 1581 forward John Nedham was a father-in-law as well as a father; the next-generation marriages of his other children followed, one by one, through the 1580s and 1590s; the family’s kin-network deepened into the home counties; the priory’s rhythms of harvest and hearing and christening and burial repeated themselves through Elizabeth’s late reign. The Wymondley Nedhams settled into a pattern that would hold without major disturbance for sixty years.

There was, however, one Wymondley Nedham of John’s children who did not settle into that pattern. He went to Court instead. — — —

Sir John of Lichborough at Court

Of John Nedham’s twelve children, only one rose to a position of public visibility above the level of country gentleman. He was Sir John Needham of Lichborough, Northamptonshire — knight, Pensioner to Queen Elizabeth, married to a sister of Sir Lewis Watson of Rockingham. He is the family’s one Elizabethan courtier. He is also a marker, in the documentary record, of how far the family’s standing had risen in two generations.

The 1572 pedigree lists him as John Nedam, is 7 yeres old in the second-wife cluster — born therefore about 1565, three years after his father’s second marriage to Jane Weldish. He grew up at the priory through the 1570s, the youngest son of the second marriage and the youngest of the four boys. He had the standard gentry boyhood: tutors at home, the parish church on Sundays, the Hertfordshire small-game shooting and riding, the schooling that prepared a younger son for whatever life his father could arrange for him. He was, in the family’s calculation, neither the heir (that was George) nor the second-line backup (that was Coppin or Thomas). He was the second son of the second marriage. He could expect a portion of a few hundred pounds, an education, and his father’s help in finding a place. He would have to make his own life elsewhere.

He went, sometime in the early 1580s, into the Inns of Court — probably Gray’s Inn, the standard choice for Hertfordshire gentlemen’s younger sons in the period, and the same Inn his father may have attended in the late 1540s. The Inns at the time functioned not so much as law schools in the modern sense — though they did teach law — but as finishing academies for the sons of the gentry, places where a young man learned to dress, to speak, to write a careful hand, to fence, to ride better, to deal with the standing of his social superiors and his inferiors, and to make the contacts that would carry him into Crown service or country life. A young man at Gray’s Inn in 1583 or 1584, well-connected through his father’s Kent and Bedfordshire alliances, with a reasonable allowance and a settled good name, was a young man being prepared to enter the Court if the Court would have him.

The Court would have him. Sometime in the late 1580s — the exact date is not preserved at primary-source level, though the 1634 visitation’s notice of him as a Pensioner places him in the office by the early 1590s at the latest — he was sworn in as a Gentleman Pensioner to Elizabeth I.

The Gentleman Pensioners were the elite ceremonial bodyguard of the monarch’s person. The institution had been founded by Henry VIII in 1509, in the early enthusiasm of his accession, as a band of fifty gentlemen of good birth and physical presence who would attend the king at state occasions, ride in his train when he progressed, stand near him at the great court ceremonies, and form the visible ring of armed Crown servants closest to the sovereign body. The strength was nominally fifty, occasionally as high as sixty. Members were appointed by royal warrant on the recommendation of the Captain of the Band — in the 1590s, Sir William Knollys — and held the office for life or at pleasure, with the right to retire if their health failed or their estates needed them.

What a Pensioner did, in practical day-to-day terms, was to take his turn on the rota of attendance at Court. Sir John would have spent some weeks of each year — perhaps a third of the year, perhaps half, depending on the rota and on whether he chose to be present at particular state occasions — in the Queen’s immediate household, lodged in the royal palaces (Whitehall, Greenwich, Hampton Court, Richmond, Nonsuch, Windsor, Oatlands, depending on where the Court was) and dining at the Pensioners’ table at Court expense. He wore the Pensioners’ uniform — a livery of crimson and gold, with the gilt half-armour of the working ceremonial guardsman, a halberd or pole-axe in his hand on duty days. He stood, on state occasions, in the front rank of the gentlemen-at-arms behind the Queen’s chair. He rode, in her progresses through the home counties, just behind her own immediate household. He was, in the most literal sense, one of the men whose body stood between the body of the Queen and any disorder that might approach her.

The standing was not military in the modern sense. The Pensioners were not the army; they were the ceremonial inner guard, the visible mark of the monarch’s personal honor. Their role at the great Court occasions of the late Elizabethan reign — the receptions for foreign ambassadors, the Garter ceremonies at Windsor, the openings of Parliament, the Queen’s birthday celebrations, the Christmas revels — was to provide the splendid armed background against which the Queen appeared in majesty. They were the visual establishment of state. They earned, for the work, a stipend of about £100 a year — a substantial gentleman’s income in the 1590s — plus their food and lodging at Court on duty days, and they were expected to maintain at their own expense the horse, the arms, the uniform, and a servant who would attend them.

Sir John in this position is the Wymondley family’s single closest approach to the inner circle of late Elizabethan power. He stood, on Pensioners’ duty days, within thirty paces of the Queen’s person. He saw the great Court figures of the 1590s at close range: Lord Burghley still ruling at Whitehall until his death in 1598; his son Sir Robert Cecil rising into the chief ministership; the Earl of Essex in his rise and his fall; Sir Walter Ralegh in his court ascendancy; Sir Francis Walsingham until 1590, then Walsingham’s successor in intelligence work; Sir Christopher Hatton as Lord Chancellor; the Archbishop of Canterbury; the foreign ambassadors. He would have been present at the Court’s reception of the news of the Spanish Armada’s defeat in August 1588. He would have stood at the Queen’s funeral procession through London on 28 April 1603, in his Pensioner’s livery, as part of the band of fifty escorting her body to its first resting place at Westminster.

He married — at a date not precisely recovered, but presumably in the 1590s — a sister of Sir Lewis Watson of Rockingham, Northamptonshire. The Watson family of Rockingham was a Northamptonshire gentry house on its way to becoming a peerage; Sir Lewis Watson, knighted in 1607, would in due course be created Baron Rockingham in 1645, and his descendants would carry the Rockingham earldom forward into the eighteenth century, into the Whig administrations of the 1760s, into the Marquess of Rockingham who served twice as Prime Minister. In the 1590s, when Sir John Nedham married Watson’s sister, the Watsons were a rising county family with the standard kinship-trajectory of late-Tudor advancing gentry: a baronetcy probable within the generation, a peerage within two. The marriage was a substantial one. It moved Sir John, in the standard gentry calculus, several social rungs above what his father and brother in Hertfordshire occupied. It also gave him a Northamptonshire seat to settle on: Lichborough, sometimes Litchborough, a small village a few miles from Towcester, where the Watson family or its kin held property that became the Nedham country residence after the marriage.

He was knighted by Elizabeth at some point in her reign — probably at one of the state occasions when batches of Pensioners and household officers were dubbed at the Queen’s discretion. The dubbing was the standard short ceremony: the gentleman knelt before the sovereign, the sovereign laid the flat of a sword on his right shoulder, the sovereign said Arise, Sir John, and the gentleman rose with the prefix to his name for the rest of his life. The date is not preserved at primary-source level; the 1634 visitation simply records him as Sir John Needham of Lichborough, co. Northampton, Kt., Pensioner to Q. Elizabeth. Burke’s General Armory, two and a half centuries later, would catalogue the Lichborough Nedhams as a separate armigerous house carrying the same arms as the Wymondley senior line — Same Arms — with a slightly altered crest: Out of a pallisado coronet or, a buck’s head sa. attired or of the first. The cadet branch was visibly the cadet branch, sharing the family identity but distinguishable from the Wymondley senior line.

He had no surviving male heirs at primary-source level. The Lichborough Nedham line is not continued in the 1634 pedigree, and Burke’s nineteenth-century catalogue carries the family forward in name but not in active descent. The Watsons of Rockingham, by contrast, carried their line forward into the peerage. The Court connection that Sir John had brought into the family thus did not, in the next generation, deepen into a settled Northamptonshire Nedham branch. He was the family’s Court figure in his own person and ceased with him.

For the Wymondley Nedhams of John’s house in Hertfordshire, Sir John in his lifetime was a glittering distant brother. He came up to the priory perhaps once a year — at Christmas, at family weddings, at funerals — in his Pensioner’s livery (which he would have packed in a chest and not worn on the road) with stories of the Court and gifts from London and his wife the future baroness-by-marriage on a side-saddle behind him. He paid for the small ornaments at the priory church that were beyond what John’s working budget could supply — perhaps the new oak panelling in the chancel, perhaps the silver chalice for the communion plate, perhaps the lead repair on the chapel roof. He represented, for his nieces and nephews growing up in the priory yard in the 1590s, the visible proof that a younger son of the Wymondley house could rise into Court attendance and could marry a sister of a Knight of the Body. He was the family’s living evidence that the Reformation-gentry rise had not stopped at the country.

He outlived his elder brother George. He is named in the 1634 visitation, sixteen years before that visitation but recovered through the family memory of the 1620s and 1630s, as having died at Lichborough in the early seventeenth century — the exact date is unrecovered in the project record. He was buried at the parish church of Lichborough, Northamptonshire, beneath an inscribed tomb-stone whose text the project has not yet retrieved. His widow may have survived him by some years and may have been received at Rockingham by her brother. Their children, if any survived infancy, are not in the 1634 visitation’s roster.

What he stood for, even after his death, was the family’s one moment of Court visibility. The Wymondley Nedhams would have, from Sir John forward, a small specifically royal-Court memory inside the family record. A Pensioner’s halberd. A crimson-and-gold livery. The face of the Queen at thirty paces. The Queen’s funeral. The accession of James I. We were there. We saw the Court. The memory would be carried forward by George the heir and George’s son George I and George I’s son Eustace through three generations after Sir John’s death, until it faded into the standard family stories told at Christmas and at weddings. By the time of Eustace’s son George Senior in the 1660s, the Pensioner uncle was a remote great-great-uncle whose name was known but whose face was not. By the time of Maurice’s daughter Barbara in the 1690s, he was a portrait on the wall — if a portrait of him had ever been painted — and a sentence in the family tradition: one of our great-uncles served the Queen at Court. The Court memory was the family’s smallest piece of nobility. It was carried, lightly, across the long Stuart twilight that followed. — — —

Arms over the door

What the 1586 grant actually did, in the daily life of the family, was to put a new coat over the principal door of the priory house.

The carving would have been commissioned in the summer or autumn of 1586, from a Hertfordshire stonemason — possibly the same workshop that did the parish church repairs, possibly a Hitchin yard, possibly a Stevenage one. The mason would have been given a drawing of the new arms, executed in pen-and-ink trick on a sheet of paper by John’s clerk working from the herald’s text, with the tinctures noted in the margins so that the mason and the painter could later finish the carving in color. The carving itself was a piece of work that took several weeks: a block of Totternhoe clunch limestone, the local soft chalk-stone that masons preferred for fine architectural carving, was quarried from the chalk pits south of the village; the block was squared up and brought to the mason’s yard; the device was laid out in pencil on the dressed face; the carving was done with chisel and mallet over perhaps a fortnight of patient work, the bend engrailed with its scalloped edge, the two bucks’ heads cabossed, the escallop, the buck’s-head crest above with its pallisado coronet. The painter then came in with his tinctures — the silver of the shield, the blue of the bend, the gold of the escallop and the coronet, the black of the bucks — and finished the work over a few days more. The whole carving was hoisted on a scaffold above the principal door of the priory house and set into the masonry, where it would remain for the next hundred and eighty-eight years until the 1973-74 restoration cleared away most of the older external fabric.

The same device went onto John’s new seal-ring. The old dolphin-and-chevron ring of the 1572 visitation period was passed on — possibly to one of the younger sons, more likely melted down and recast as a new ring with the bend and the bucks. A seal-ring in the 1580s was a working tool, not just a piece of jewelry; it was pressed into the wax of every deed, indenture, lease, and letter the head of family sent out. The new seal would appear on every document the priory issued from 1586 forward. The earliest surviving documentary impression of it that the project has yet located is on the Elizabethan Chancery papers in TNA C 2/Eliz/N5/46 — the suit against William Clarke that occupies the next section — where John’s seal in red wax appears on the verso of the bill of complaint. The same seal would be used by his son George I after John’s death and would, in modified form, be used by Eustace and George Senior across the seventeenth century.

The plate, too. The Wymondley silver of the late 1580s and 1590s was engraved with the new arms. The great gilt cup, the basin and ewer, the salt-cellar at the head of the high table — all carried the bend-and-bucks. The plate would be passed down in the family through the seventeenth century until, generation by generation, pieces of it were sold off to meet the family’s slowly contracting fortunes. The cup may have gone in the 1666/67 settlement, when George Senior wrote his will. The basin may have gone with one of the younger sons in the 1680s.

The whole of the Wymondley plate had probably been dispersed by the early 1700s, well before the priory itself passed out of the family in 1733. The arms on the plate, where the plate has survived in any English collection, would now be unidentified — anonymous late-Tudor silver, no provenance clear, the family’s identity legible only to someone who knew what the bend engrailed and the bucks’ heads were and where to look them up.

The arms also went, eventually, onto stone. The 1605 monument in the chancel of Little Wymondley parish church — the brass that George I would commission to commemorate his grandfather James and his father John — carried the new arms above the inscription. The monument, transcribed by Sir Henry Chauncy when he wrote his county history in the 1690s and confirmed in place by the Victoria County History architectural survey of 1909, carries the new coat as the family’s visible attestation of itself. The 1605 monument is the chapter’s documentary terminus. It is the moment at which the second generation of the Wymondley line — George I, John’s eldest son — set in stone, in the same chapel where the family was being baptized and buried, the arms that the herald had granted his father nineteen years before.

The arms outlasted the family at Wymondley. They survive today, in the College of Arms records and in the printed reference volumes of English heraldry, as the registered arms of Needham of Wymondsley, co. Hertford, confirmed 18 February 1586. Burke’s General Armory of 1884 catalogues them as still extant in nineteenth-century heraldic use through the senior line’s collateral branches. Whether any physical example of the original 1586 carving over the priory door survives in any private collection is unrecovered; the 1973-74 restoration would have either disposed of the stone or transferred it elsewhere. The Lichborough cadet branch’s monuments at the Northamptonshire parish church — if any have survived the centuries — would carry the same arms. The Wymondley silver, if any of it has come down into a private or museum collection, would carry the same arms. The visual identity that Cooke granted on 18 February 1586 is, in a real institutional sense, the surviving signature of the Wymondley Nedhams. It is what the family was known by, in heraldic terms, for a hundred and forty-seven years.

It is also, in a quieter way, what we shall see one more time in this book. In the autumn of 1713, a young woman named Barbara Tempest Needham will be carrying, in a small leather case at the bottom of her trunk, a personal seal pressed with the family arms — the bend, the bucks, the escallop. It will travel with her across the Atlantic on a packet out of Gravesend. The arms will be on the wax of the letter she sends ahead of her — I am coming — and that will reach Christopher’s hand at New Bern on the seventeenth of November of that year. The arms are in Chapter 5. They are also, in a longer way, here. — — —

Nedeham v Clarke

Sometime in the 1580s — the exact year is not preserved on the surviving file’s covering documents, though the suit was active during the reign of Elizabeth I and most probably falls in the middle decade of that reign — John Nedham filed a bill of complaint in the Court of Chancery against a man named William Clarke.

The file is preserved at the National Archives at Kew under the call number C 2/Eliz/N5/46. The catalogue entry gives the short title as Nedeham v Clarke. The plaintiff is John Nedeham, Esq., of Little Wymondley in Hertfordshire. The defendant is William Clarke, of whom the surviving record gives little detail; he may have been a Hertfordshire tenant of one of the priory’s outlying parcels, or a neighbor with an adjacent property whose boundary touched John’s, or a tradesman to whom John had advanced money on credit, or some combination of these. The bill of complaint as it survives in the Chancery papers preserves John’s own framing of the case. He recites, in the conventional form of an Elizabethan equity pleading, that the property in dispute — Wymondley, Hertfordshire, formerly belonging to the priory of Little Wymondley — had passed since the dissolution of the said priory by Crown grant by King Henry 8 to James Nedeham esq, plaintiff’s father. He recites the descent from James to himself. He asserts his title. He complains of some specific wrong that Clarke is doing him.

The substantive content of the wrong is the one piece of the file that the project has not yet retrieved at primary-source level. The surviving catalogue description gives the parties, the subject (litigation; landed estates), the period (Elizabeth I), and the bare opening recitations of the bill, but does not summarize what Clarke was actually doing. A direct reading of the manuscript at TNA Kew, which the project’s research apparatus has flagged as a next-pass action, would clarify the case. The most likely substantive content, on the conventions of the period’s Chancery suits between landed gentlemen and lesser men, is one of three things: a boundary dispute over a specific parcel of priory land; a contract dispute over an unpaid lease or a defaulted loan; or a claim by Clarke to some right of common, pasture, or way over priory ground that John believed Clarke had no title to. Any of these would have been the standard Elizabethan complaint of a small country squire against an inconvenient neighbor.

What matters about the suit for the chapter’s purposes is not its outcome — the outcome, if it was decreed, may be in the Chancery decree-rolls and remains for a future research pass — but the framing of the bill itself. John, in setting out his case, asserts the Crown grant to his father as the foundation of his title. Wymondley, Hertfordshire, formerly belonging to the priory of Little Wymondley, and since the dissolution of the said priory granted by King Henry 8 to James Nedeham esq, plaintiff’s father. This is the family’s documented self-attestation, before the Lord Chancellor of England, of how it came to be at Wymondley. It is a sworn statement of legal title that goes on the official record of the Court of Chancery.

It is the same descent the 1572 pedigree gave to Robert Cooke; it is the same descent the 1634 pedigree would give to Sir Richard St George; it is the same descent the family had been telling around its own dinner table for two generations.

The suit is, in this specific institutional sense, the family’s foundation story being told in a courtroom rather than at a herald’s table. The 1572 visitation had recorded the descent on the herald’s vellum; the Chancery bill of the 1580s recorded the same descent on the Court’s parchment. Both records survive. Both records say the same thing. The redundancy is the historian’s good fortune; the family’s foundation is now documented twice over, in two completely separate Crown institutions, in two completely separate handwritten records that have come down through four hundred and fifty years on different file-paths and that the project has now reconciled to the same documentary base.

The suit also tells us, in a quieter way, something about John as a man. He was, by his fifties, a litigant. He went to law to defend his property. He did not let an inconvenient neighbor encroach on the priory’s lands without taking the encroachment to the Crown’s equity court. He paid the legal fees — the writ costs, the counsel fees, the clerk’s copying fees, the dozen small expenditures of a country gentleman pursuing a suit at London — and he sustained the case across what was, for an Elizabethan Chancery suit, probably several years of pleadings and answers and rejoinders and counter-claims. He kept the bill. He kept the family’s title.

This is consistent with what we have seen elsewhere of John. He was the inheritance generation. He did not gamble the estate. He did not let it drift. He litigated when litigation was the necessary course and he kept on hand at the priory the documents — the letters patent of 1538, the licence to entail of 1544, the Court of Wards’ record of his own succession in 1545, the marriage settlements, the deed of the South Mimms wood, the deed of the Bygrave land, the rectory of Wilshamstead, the tithe-book — that proved his title to every piece of the estate his father had assembled. When Clarke or whoever else encroached, John could produce the documents. The Chancery bill of the 1580s is one of the working evidences of the working management of the working priory under John’s hand for the better part of fifty years.

He also, sometime in this period — the date is again imprecise — endowed the parish school. The Stevenage Free School, which the v9 Urwick material identifies as having been founded in 1561 or 1562 with funds from a Hertfordshire gentleman, was probably a Nedham endowment in significant part, or at least was supported by John’s contribution among others. The school’s founding is in a slightly earlier window than the Chancery suit; the v9 reading places John at about thirty-seven or thirty-eight when the school was endowed. The endowment is consistent with what late-Tudor gentry of John’s standing did: they put money into the parish institutions of their county, partly out of religious obligation, partly out of social standing, partly out of the practical wish to educate the local boys whose labor would in due course be on their estates. The school operated through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It is the small parochial monument to John’s discretion with his money.

By the early 1590s John was in his late sixties. He had been at the priory for nearly fifty years. The estate was settled. The Chancery suit had run its course, whatever its outcome. The plate carried the new arms. The eldest son was married with children. Sir John of Lichborough was at Court. The daughters were married off to the Bedfordshire houses. The household at the priory was, by the standards of any late-Elizabethan gentry establishment, a model of quiet consolidation.

He died in 1591 or 1592. The exact date is not preserved at primary-source level; the Little Wymondley parish register that would have recorded his burial does not survive for the early 1590s. The 1634 visitation, in its retrospect, places him as deceased before George I took up the headship of the family. He would have been about sixty-seven or sixty-eight years old. He was buried in the chapel that had been the priory church, in the chancel, perhaps under a flagstone with a small brass plate. The brass is not described in any later record; if it ever existed it had been lost or paved over by the time Chauncy worked through Hertfordshire’s monumental inscriptions in the 1690s. What Chauncy preserves at Little Wymondley is the 1605 monument that John’s son George I would erect to James and to John together — the only family memorial to John that survived to be transcribed. The inscription, on the brass on the north wall of the chancel, reads in Chauncy’s transcription: James Needham, of the ancient Family of the Needhams in Derbyshire, came into this County of Hertford, in the year of our Lord, 1536. He was advanced by King Henry the eight, for his Service in England and France, and lyes buried in our Ladies Church in Boloigne; he had issue John, who had George, who dedicated this Monument to their Memory, Anno 1605. The line that names John — he had issue John, who had George — is the family’s own statement of the chapter’s three generations. James, John, George. Surveyor, inheritance generation, the next.

John’s signature on the vellum of 1572 was the first piece of his documented personhood. The 1605 brass is, in a quieter way, the last. — — —

The handing-on

The funeral procession was the standard one for a Hertfordshire gentleman of John’s standing.

It began at the priory house on a winter or early-spring morning — the season is conjectural, the date is unrecovered — with the coffin carried out of the great hall by six of John’s senior tenants, men who had farmed the priory’s land under his hand for two or three decades and who had been chosen by the household to perform the final office of carrying their lord to his rest. The coffin was of oak, plain, with iron fittings, made by the local carpenter at Hitchin in the days before. It was draped with a black pall on which the new arms of 1586 were embroidered in white silk at the four corners — the bend and the bucks, the family’s visible signature. The procession moved out of the priory yard, through the gatehouse, and onto the short road that led to the parish church a few hundred yards away — the church that had been the priory church, the church that the family had been baptizing its children in and burying its dead in for fifty-four years by the early 1590s.

The household followed. Jane Weldish, the widow, walked in the place of chief mourner with her stepson George the heir at her right hand. The other children walked behind. The married daughters had come up from Bedfordshire and Kent for the funeral, with their husbands and the eldest of their own children; the funeral was the kind of family occasion that brought the dispersed alliances back to the seat for a few days. Sir John of Lichborough had ridden over from Northamptonshire with his wife the future baroness-by-marriage. Coppin and Thomas, if they were still living, were there; the second John before he became Sir John was there, in his Court livery if he chose to wear it or in standard mourning if he did not. Anne the eldest daughter had come up from her Pole household in London. The Bedfordshire cousins — the Mordaunts, the Pigotts, the Langhornes, the Warrens — had come up from their respective seats. The procession was substantial, perhaps fifty or sixty mourners in the immediate train, with the parish villagers behind and the tenants and laborers of the estate filling out the body of the cortege.

The curate of the parish — the Wymondley Parva minister of the period was probably John Marshall, who would serve until 1616, or his predecessor — met the procession at the church door. The standard Book of Common Prayer burial service was read. The coffin was carried inside and set on a trestle bier in front of the chancel screen. The lesson was read; the prayers were said; the curate gave a brief funeral sermon, in the standard form of the period — a meditation on the lessons of the deceased’s life, framed by the appropriate scriptural texts, lasting perhaps fifteen minutes. The communion service followed, with the family at the altar rail. The hymn was sung — probably the metrical psalm that was the standard funeral closing in late-Elizabethan parish practice.

Then the coffin was carried into the chancel and lowered into the prepared grave under the flagstones. The chancel was the family’s burial place by lay-impropriator’s right; the same right would carry George I to his burial here in 1626 and George II to his in 1669. The flagstones were lifted, the grave was sealed, the chancel was restored to its working state. The mourners returned to the priory for the funeral meal.

The meal would have been substantial but not lavish. Tudor funeral feasting was at its peak earlier in the sixteenth century; by the 1590s the Reformation-influenced English gentry had begun to scale back the ostentation of funeral hospitality in favor of a more sober commemoration. A roasted joint, boiled fowl, the autumn or spring vegetables of the kitchen garden, bread and cheese, beer and a moderate amount of wine. Conversation was quiet. The married daughters caught up on the news of each other’s households. Sir John from Court told one or two careful stories of the Queen’s recent progresses. George the heir, now formally lord of the manor, presided at the head of the table where his father had sat the day before.

That was the moment.

George Nedham, then thirty-four or thirty-five years old, married for ten years to Margaret Stile, father of two or three small children with more to come, was the new lord of Wymondley. He had been being prepared for the position since he was sixteen and Robert Cooke had recorded him in the visitation as son and heir. The role he had been preparing for was now his. The estate was intact. The arms were on the door. The Chancery suit had run its course. The Court connection through Sir John was operative. The daughters were married. The brothers were settled or dead. The widow Jane Weldish would live on at the priory for as long as her health permitted, supported by George under his father’s wishes, and would be buried in the chancel beside John when her time came. The household was in transition once more.

George I — the historian’s notation, to distinguish him from his eldest grandson George II and his eldest great-grandson George III — would hold the priory for the next thirty-four or thirty-five years. He would erect the 1605 monument to his father and grandfather, and would commission the inscription that named the family’s foundation: He was advanced by King Henry the eight, for his Service in England and France. He would be the lay impropriator of Wymondley Parva rectory, paying Bernard Jones, M.A., as the stipendiary curate. He would baptize his children in the chapel. He would bury his wife in the chancel when she predeceased him. He would die on 27 June 1626 and be buried in the same chancel three days later, under the bell of John Dyer that had been recast in 1595 and that would, by Eustace’s generation, be ringing for the third generation of the family it had begun ringing for in the 1590s.

George I’s son Eustace, who would inherit the priory in 1626 and would carry it through the long Stuart-into-Cromwell convulsion of the seventeenth century, is the protagonist of the next chapter.

In the summer of 1572, a man named John Nedham had sat down at a trestle table in his own priory yard and signed his name at the bottom of a herald’s pedigree. He had spent the next twenty years and more being what the herald’s pedigree said he was: the lord of Wymondley, the head of a documented Hertfordshire gentry family, the second generation of the Reformation rise that his father had begun. He had married twice, raised twelve children, married off the daughters into Bedfordshire houses, paid for the parish school and the Chancery suit and the new arms and the silver and the chancel repairs. He had sustained the priory’s working life through the high noon of Elizabeth’s reign. He had handed it on to his eldest son, intact.

He had not, in the working register of his life, been a remarkable man. He had not been a Surveyor of the King’s Works, like his father. He had not been a Pensioner of the Queen’s household, like his second son. He had been a county gentleman, second-tier, working the estate his father had assembled and ensuring it would be there for the third generation. The visible measure of his life is the priory itself — still standing in the field three miles south of Hitchin in the spring of 2026, still standing in the field three miles south of Hitchin in the spring of 1626 when his son died on its grounds, still standing in the field three miles south of Hitchin every year of those four hundred and fifty-four. He had kept it. He had passed it on.

His signature on the herald’s vellum is the family’s documented beginning. His funeral procession to the chancel of the parish church, sometime in 1591 or 1592, is the chapter’s quiet end. The two events frame his working life and the chapter’s analytical arc. Between them lie the marriages, the children, the Court visit, the Chancery suit, the new arms, the long working years of a Hertfordshire country gentleman doing what a Hertfordshire country gentleman was for.

Three generations down the line from him, the priory would be in the hands of his great-grandson Eustace, sitting in the same small estate room off the great hall in the Michaelmas of 1640, reading a letter from his Welwyn cousin about a Parliament the king had been forced to call against his will, and trying to decide what the family’s posture would be in the war that everyone in Hertfordshire could feel was coming.

That decision is the next chapter.
