The Believer Who Could Not Prove It

Tania Miagkova and the trap of sincerity. A companion essay to Yuri Slezkine's The House of Government.

The Party expelled her, exiled her, locked her in a political isolator, and sent her to Kolyma. Through all of it she wrote home that the Party was right. The obvious question is whether she meant it. The harder question, and the one that killed her, is that no one could ever know. Not her jailers, not her family, and, as Yuri Slezkine admits, not even the historian holding her letters. Tania Miagkova's story shows what happens to belief inside a movement that demands total sincerity and has no way to verify it.

The puzzle

Anyone who reads much about the Soviet 1930s runs into a pattern that is hard to believe the first time. People were arrested by their own Party, sent to prison or the camps, and kept professing their faith in it. Evgenia Ginzburg, in Journey into the Whirlwind, describes cellmates who sang Party songs and insisted their own arrest was a mistake while everyone else's was deserved.

The usual explanations are fear, opportunism, and a supposed Russian fatalism. People said what they had to say to survive.

Tania Miagkova's letters are the best test of those explanations in Slezkine's book, because there are so many of them. She wrote to her family from exile, prison, and the camps, and her daughter preserved and typed them up (p. xv). But there's a catch, and Slezkine states it plainly: her letters were not confidential (p. 575). Every one of them passed through censors. So the letters can't simply be read as a window into her soul. They are a record of what a believer said when she knew the Party was listening, and that turns out to be the more interesting thing.

Who she was

Tania was born in 1897 (p. 990) and raised inside the movement. Her mother, Feoktista Miagkova, was a Tambov revolutionary and the closest friend and mentor of the Bolshevik editor Aleksandr Voronsky (p. 309). Tania joined the Party, studied at the Kharkov Institute of Economics and Sverdlov Communist University in Moscow, and married Mikhail Poloz, head of the Ukrainian planning agency and earlier Ukraine's finance commissar. Their daughter, Rada, was born in 1924 (pp. 309, 990).

Then Tania joined the Trotskyist opposition, and in 1927 she was expelled from the Party (p. 309). In 1928, at thirty-one, she was exiled to Chelkar, a railroad town in Kazakhstan, where she rented a room with two other young exiles, Sonia Smirnova and Mirra Varshavskaia (p. 310). By 1930 she had probably left Chelkar, having reconciled with her husband, her mother, and the Party line (p. 365). Slezkine does not say she was formally readmitted to the Party. In 1930 the family moved into one of the first finished apartments in the House of Government, later Apartment 199. Poloz came to Moscow as deputy chairman of the Central Executive Committee's Budget Commission (p. 387).

On the surface, the heretic had come home.

The isolator and the letters

It didn't last. In January 1933, two months after her thirty-fifth birthday, Tania was arrested again. She was tried as part of the "counterrevolutionary Trotskyite group" of I. N. Smirnov, Ter-Vaganian, and Preobrazhensky, and sentenced to three years in the Verkhneuralsk Political Isolator, a prison for oppositionists on the Ural River near Magnitogorsk (pp. 562–63). (On what the isolators were and where they fit among the Soviet punishments, see The Outer Rings.)

By now she was no longer an oppositionist, and she acted like it. In Verkhneuralsk she found Mirra Varshavskaia, her old roommate from Chelkar. Mirra had stayed in opposition while Tania had embraced the Party line, and the two women were not on speaking terms (p. 788). That detail matters more than any profession of faith in a letter. Refusing to speak to an old friend in a prison cell, where no reward was on offer, is behavior, not performance.

In the isolator Tania studied industrial economics and read Das Kapital, and she wrote to her mother. In one letter she urged, "Don't let life pass you by" (p. 312).

Then on January 12, 1934, Poloz was arrested in Moscow as a Ukrainian nationalist and former member of the Borotbist party, the Ukrainian Left SRs (p. 571). When Tania heard, she put down Marx and read Tolstoy, Resurrection and Anna Karenina (p. 573). Within a month she was back at her studies (p. 573). In late July she learned that her husband had been sentenced to ten years in a labor camp (p. 576). He was sent to the Solovetsky Islands.

The trap of sincerity

Slezkine's argument is that Bolshevism was a millenarian movement: it expected the imminent, violent destruction of the old world, followed by an eternity of harmony. Every such movement eventually faces the same problem: the world doesn't end on schedule. The psychologist Leon Festinger, in When Prophecy Fails (1956), found that committed believers often respond to failure by believing harder, because admitting the prophecy was false would mean their sacrifices were for nothing.

Tania fits that pattern. But her letters show a second problem that Festinger's doomsday group never faced, because Tania's sect held state power and police files.

The Party demanded more than obedience. It demanded sincere, inner conviction, and it had no instrument for measuring it. Slezkine says it was, and remains, impossible to be sure when Tania was practicing mental reservation, saying one thing while holding another (p. 576). Her jailers faced the same problem without a historian's patience. That created a trap Tania saw clearly: claims of sincerity could be read as proof of duplicity (p. 578). The more fervently a former oppositionist declared her loyalty, the more it looked like exactly what a hidden enemy would say. Silence was suspicious too. There was no correct move. All she could do, Slezkine writes, was wait (p. 578).

That trap is the hinge between Slezkine's theory and the Terror. A millenarian sect that has "won" but still has no paradise must explain why. The explanation is hidden enemies. And if enemies are hidden, then outward conduct proves nothing, and anyone can be one, including the most faithful.

The end

In 1936, having served her three years, Tania was sentenced to three more years of exile in Kazakhstan. She was told to go to Uralsk, where she found work as an economist in a spare-parts warehouse and rented a room (p. 784). On June 14, 1936, she was arrested again and sentenced to five years in a labor camp. She was sent by train across the country to Kolyma, and her first letter home is dated July 18, 1936 (p. 787). "To be honest with you," she wrote, "I still haven't recovered from the shock, and the atmosphere around here is not conducive to concentrated reflection . . . but you know I am indestructible, and quite soon I'll be in good shape again" (p. 788).

In Kolyma she found Mirra Varshavskaia again. The differences that had kept them apart in Verkhneuralsk had lost their significance (p. 788). Faith or heresy, they were now just two women in a camp.

Her mother and Rada wrote to her regularly. There was nothing from Mikhail (p. 857).

In September 1937 a new wave of accusations swept the camps. Tania was moved back to Magadan. Her old Chelkar roommate, Sonia Smirnova, ended up in the same barracks, and she remembered prisoners being brought in from distant camps to hear their new guilt and their new sentences (p. 860).

Tania was interrogated on September 26. The case against her rested on two witnesses. The first was a guard at Yagodnoe, Artemy Kadochnikov. He said that on September 14, when a party of prisoners being moved to another camp stopped there, Tania ignored his order to step away from one of them, a man named Poliakov, and tried to hand him something. When he threatened to shoot, she screamed at the guards, "Fascists! Fascist lackeys!", and told them their lawlessness would soon be over. Poliakov shouted back, "That's right, Tania!" Kadochnikov added that she often tried to leave the zone at unauthorized times and called the guards fascists when he stopped her.

At the interrogation Tania denied it. She said she had learned the prisoners were coming two minutes before they arrived, heard no order from the guards, and talked to her friend for exactly two minutes. The guard and the prisoner don't even agree on the friend's first name: Kadochnikov calls him Mikhail, and Tania calls him Veniamin. The second witness was a woman Tania had shared a room with at Yagodnoe. She testified that Tania was an "unreformed Trotskyite," bitterly hostile to the regime.

Slezkine doesn't decide whether Kadochnikov told the truth. Even if he did, Slezkine writes, there is no way to know what the outburst meant. It might have been Job's rebellion against God, a believer finally turning on the power she had served. Or it might have been a version of the theory held by two senior Bolsheviks, Robert Eikhe and Filipp Goloshchekin, as they themselves fell: that the NKVD had been taken over by fascist saboteurs. On that reading, shouting "Fascists!" at a camp guard was an act of loyalty to the Party (p. 860). This is the trap of sincerity at its last turn. The one recorded moment of open defiance in Tania's story can be read as faith lost or as faith kept, and the evidence can't tell us which.

On November 3, 1937, the NKVD troika of the Far Eastern Territory sentenced her to death on three charges: "maintaining regular contact with convicted Trotskyites," holding a six-month-long hunger strike, and expressing counterrevolutionary, defeatist ideas (p. 860). She was shot on November 17, 1937.

On the same day she was sentenced, November 3, 1937, Mikhail Poloz was shot at Sandarmokh in Karelia, one of more than a thousand Solovki prisoners executed there that autumn. The coincidence of dates comes from outside Slezkine's book, from the Encyclopedia of Ukraine. Neither of them could have known.

Rada was raised by her grandmother, Feoktista, alongside her cousin Volia. Feoktista lived on newspapers and the radio and raised both children as fervent Soviet patriots (p. 566). Years later, Rada typed up her mother's letters (p. xv).

Where the frame explains her, and where it falls short

Slezkine's millenarian frame explains Tania better than fear or fatalism can. A frightened woman would have kept quiet. A fatalist would have stopped studying. Tania kept reading Marx in a prison cell and refused to speak to an unrepentant friend. Those are the acts of a believer.

But the frame has limits, and Tania exposes them.

The first is that we can't fully know what she believed, and neither could anyone else. Slezkine is honest about that. A theory of belief built on censored letters has to live with the possibility that some of what it is reading is protective performance.

The second is the household. The same building that held Tania's kind of faith also held dachas, nannies, special stores, and summer sanatoriums. Some residents grew comfortable, and their private lives quietly stopped matching their creed. The faith was perhaps strongest in people like Tania, who had been thrown out of the comfort, and weakest in those who were safely inside it.

The third is the Terror itself. A failed-prophecy cycle explains why a believer would accept her punishment. It does not by itself explain the quotas, the troikas, and the decisions of one man in the Kremlin. Tania's faith explains Tania. It doesn't explain Stalin.

What she leaves us with is a harder question than "were they believers or hypocrites?" It's what happens to true believers in a movement that requires sincerity and can't verify it, and that has decided its enemies are hidden.
