Volume III

Epilogue

786 words · about 3 min read

Mountain Brook, Alabama — 2026

I went looking for a grave in Alcorn County, Mississippi, and I did not find a stone. What I found was a certificate — one page, filled out by a country registrar in the middle of October 1920 — and it is the strongest single thing I have on her, and it is also, read closely, the record of the people who did not wait for the state.

Mary Jane Searcy died on the eleventh of October 1920, at half past four in the morning, in her son's house in the Third Beat of Alcorn County, eighty-seven years and very nearly eleven months after the family Bible could not decide what to call her and wrote her down as Milbry or Mity Jane. The certificate gives her name whole at the top of the page — Mary Jane Searcy — the first time in the whole documentary record that anyone set it down complete and unhurried, and by then everyone who might have said why the Bible hesitated was gone. The cause of death is entered as paralysis, but it is entered by a hand that was not a doctor's, because the medical section carries no physician's signature — No Doctor, the page says, and against the line asking what test confirmed the diagnosis, No. There was no doctor at the end. There was no undertaker either: where the form asks for one it says, in the plain economy of that country, no undertaker — homemade coffin. Her sons made the box. She was buried the next day at Pine Grove, and her son Robert Edwin, fifty years old, told the registrar what he knew, and the registrar filed it on the sixteenth, and that is the whole official account of the end of her.

What the certificate does not say, and what I have spent this book trying to say for it, is what she did with the eighty-seven years between the two entries. She held the line. I mean that as plainly and as literally as the phrase will bear. She held a specific line — an actual boundary around an actual eighty acres of specific dirt, the old Rorie ground her mother had taken up and kept without the help of any court, and that she took up in her turn and kept the same way, through a war that came within thirteen miles of her door, through a widowhood, through the long moving of a world that never once asked her leave to move. The dirt stayed and the world went, and she stood on the one and watched the other go, and she did not let go of the line, and when she died the line was still there and the dirt was still theirs.

I have come to think she is the whole point of the three books, and not because she was the greatest of the people in them. Because she was the plainest, and the plainest is where the thing shows clearest. There is a baron in the first volume and a Revolutionary grant in the second, and they are in here because they lead down to a girl at a loom on a ridge who never held an office or owned a famous name and did, all her life, the one thing the family had always actually done, which is stay — price what the century sends, and stay, and keep the record forward a line at a time. Her grandmother did it in a corner bed. Her mother did it at a churn and then at a farm the court never touched. She did it for sixty years after the men were gone, and then she handed it, without a word said about it, to a daughter-in-law and a set of grandchildren who would carry it into a century she never saw.

She was watched doing it, the way she had watched her own mother, the way her mother had watched Sarah. That is how this kind of thing goes into a family — not by instruction but by attention, one woman keeping the count while the next one learns it from across the room without knowing she is learning. The census called Mary Jane mother on the line where it kept such women, and I have kept her the same way, and the truest thing I know how to say about her is the thing this last volume is named for and the thing I will not spend a second time, having spent it where it belongs, at the close of her own chapter and here at the close of her book:

She held the line.
