The Outer Rings

Upon "release": how free were the freed and the exiled? On the many fences beyond the wire — and why the Gulag had no outside.

Put the word in quotation marks, the way the prisoners did, and you are already close to the truth. A zek who finished his sentence was not, as a rule, released — he was moved. The Gulag was never a single place with a gate you walked out of; it was a gradient of unfreedom, ring within ring, and "release" usually meant stepping from the innermost ring, the camp, into a wider one where the fences were invisible but no less real. To ask how free the freed and the exiled truly were is to discover that in the Soviet system, freedom in any ordinary sense was simply never on offer — not for them, and arguably not for anyone.

Exile in perpetuity

Take the man you are reading. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn walked out of the Ekibastuz camp in February 1953, the very month Stalin died — and straight into exile "in perpetuity." Not a second sentence with an end date: forever. He was consigned to Kok-Terek, a dusty settlement in southern Kazakhstan, where he taught school, wrote in secret, and was nearly killed by the cancer that would become Cancer Ward. His "release" had merely swapped one keeper for another — the barbed wire for a signature in the commandant's ledger. And this was by design: a 1948 decree had made perpetual exile the built-in fate of the political "repeaters," who served their ten years and were then banished without term. The sentence did not end. It changed shape.

A prison without walls

Exile and the "special settlements" had no fence — and needed none. The exile reported to the commandant (komendatura) on a fixed schedule, could not travel a mile without a pass, and a single unauthorized absence was itself a fresh crime, punishable by a return to the camps for years. Whole deported nations lived their lives this way, in villages that were prisons without walls. Solzhenitsyn's own image for it was exact: exile was simply the outer ring of the same Archipelago, the wire gone underground.

You did not leave the Gulag. You were promoted outward through it — from the cell to the barracks to the settlement to the marked life beyond — and at no ring was the door ever quite unlocked behind you.

The hundred-and-first kilometer

Even those genuinely freed rather than exiled hit a wall. Under the "minus" system a released prisoner was barred from the major cities — "minus twelve," they said, meaning banned from the twelve largest — and shoved out past the "hundred-and-first kilometer," the belt beyond which undesirables were required to live. You could not go home to Moscow or Leningrad; home was now, by law, someone else's city. The internal passport, reintroduced in 1932, and its residence stamp — the propiska — had turned the whole country into a lattice of permissions, and the ex-zek's papers carried exactly the marks that kept him out of anywhere worth living. (Remember, too, that the collective farmer had no passport at all until 1974 — an entire peasantry pinned to the soil, the "second serfdom" the Gulag reference notes.)

The mark that never washed off

Behind the legal fences lay a quieter one: social death. Every job application and housing form carried the question — were you convicted? — and a truthful yes closed the door. Employers would not risk the marked; neighbors feared the taint of association; the "family of a traitor" bore its own stain, passed to spouses and children. And the returnee frequently came back to nothing at all: the apartment reassigned, the wife remarried, the children raised to deny the name. Orlando Figes's The Whisperers and Eugenia Ginzburg's Within the Whirlwind are crowded with these half-lives — people legally free and yet, socially and materially, erased.

Freedom on a leash

And what freedom there was could be revoked without warning. In 1948–49 a wave of re-arrests swept up former Article 58 prisoners for no new offense whatever — the "repeaters," jailed a second time for the same imaginary crime, precisely because they had once been jailed for it. To be released was to live provisionally, on a leash the state could shorten at any hour, and the dread of the second knock at the door never left. Even Khrushchev's rehabilitations after the 1956 Secret Speech restored the paper record but not the lost years, the ruined health, or the trust of a society that mostly wished the returnees would keep quiet.

Released was never a state of being. It was a permission — conditional, revocable, and stamped, like everything else, in a passport the state could always ask to see.

Solzhenitsyn, to the last border

Follow his life to the end and you trace every ring in turn. The cell and the interrogation; the camps; exile in perpetuity in the Kazakh desert; then rehabilitation and a few astonishing years of daylight — One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich published in 1962 with Khrushchev's own blessing; then the slow closing again, expelled from the Writers' Union, hounded, unpublishable; and at last, in February 1974, seized, stripped of his citizenship, and put on a plane out of the country altogether. The state's final word on its most famous prisoner was the oldest Russian punishment there is — banishment — only this time past the border itself. He had charted the whole Archipelago ring by ring for one unanswerable reason: he had been carried through every one of them.

So how free?

Freer than the camp, and it would be a lie to pretend that did not matter — a man in exile could teach a class, marry, write in a drawer, feel the sun on his face with no tower watching. But free in the full sense the word carries in a country that means it? No. The released and the exiled had been moved to the outer rings of a system that had no outside — where the fences were made of paperwork and fear instead of wire, and were, for that, no easier to climb. The barbed wire was only the first of them. The cruelest thing "release" taught a person was how many more fences there were, and how silent.

Further reading: Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, The Gulag Archipelago, Vol. III (Parts VI–VII, on exile and release); Eugenia Ginzburg, Within the Whirlwind; Orlando Figes, The Whisperers; Stephen F. Cohen, The Victims Return; Miriam Dobson, Khrushchev's Cold Summer: Gulag Returnees, Crime, and the Fate of Reform after Stalin; Anne Applebaum, Gulag: A History.

A note on certainty: the mechanisms here — exile in perpetuity, the special settlements and the commandant's regime, the "minus" and hundred-and-first-kilometer restrictions, the passport and propiska system, the 1948–49 re-arrests, and Solzhenitsyn's 1974 expulsion — are all firmly documented. The interpretive claim is the framing: that these were not separate policies but concentric rings of a single unfreedom. That is an argument, offered as such.
