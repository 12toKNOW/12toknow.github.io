The Fourteen Rungs

Peter the Great's Table of Ranks promised rank by service, not birth. What it built was chin — a country where a man's number was his name.

On 24 January 1722 Peter the Great signed a document that was, physically, a table: fourteen numbered rows, three columns headed military, civil, and court, and in each cell a title. Field marshal and chancellor at the top; ensign and collegiate registrar at the bottom. Nineteen numbered rules followed, and the eleventh of them said the thing that made the Table famous: any man who reached the eighth grade in civil service, or held any officer's commission at all, was to be counted among the hereditary nobility "even if of low birth." Every servitor of the Russian state, from that morning until a decree of November 1917, had a number. The textbook reading is that Peter replaced rank by birth with rank by merit, and opened Russia to talent. It is what the document says. It is about half of what the document did.

What Peter wrote

He did not invent it. Sweden had ranked its officials in a table since the 1680s, Denmark since 1699, Prussia since 1705, and Peter had the foreign models translated and worked over by his German-born secretary Ostermann before editing the draft in his own hand. But the Russian version went further than any of the originals, because Peter attached it to something the Swedes had never needed: compulsory, lifelong service by every nobleman, on pain of losing his estates. Since 1714 the sons of the nobility had been forbidden to marry until they could pass arithmetic and geometry; since the same year they had been required to serve, all of them, for life. The Table told them where they stood while they did it. And then, in rule eleven, it opened the door from the other side: a peasant's son who made ensign was a noble, and his sons after him.

The intent is not in doubt, and neither is the malice behind it. Peter loathed the old boyar hierarchy, in which a man's precedence was fixed by his ancestors' precedence — the system of mestnichestvo, abolished in 1682, under which generals had refused to serve beneath men whose grandfathers had sat lower at the Tsar's table. The Table was its exact inversion. Rule eight said flatly that the sons of the greatest families would receive no rank until they had served for it, and Peter meant to be believed: princes of the oldest blood in Russia began as privates in his Guards regiments, and foreigners and nobodies commanded them.

The door opens

And the nobodies came through. Alexander Menshikov — the story that he sold pies in the Moscow streets as a boy may be foreign malice, but he was certainly nothing — ended as a prince of the Holy Roman Empire, a field marshal, and, for a year after Peter's death, the actual ruler of Russia. Peter Shafirov, vice-chancellor and baron, was the son of a converted Jew from the Smolensk trade. Pavel Yaguzhinsky, the first procurator-general — the "Tsar's eye" over the Senate, created the same month as the Table — was the son of a Lithuanian church organist. Anton Devier, the first police chief of St. Petersburg, had been a cabin boy on a Dutch ship. No Muscovite court had ever looked like that, and no European one either.

Quantify first, though, before the story runs away. Brenda Meehan-Waters went through the whole generalitet of 1730 — every man holding the top four grades, five years after Peter's death — and asked where they came from. The answer was that the great majority came from families that had already been serving the Muscovite tsars for generations. The spectacular new men were real, and they were a minority. What the Table had done was not to displace the old service families but to reorganize them: they went through Peter's door first, because they were nearest to it, and they came out the other side as generals and privy councillors with a number instead of a pedigree. The opening was genuine. The people who walked through it were, mostly, the people who had always been going to walk through it.

The Table did not abolish birth. It converted birth into a head start — and then, within a generation, the nobility learned to convert the head start into years.

Gaming the clock

Here is the flaw, and it is the flaw in every performance system ever built: the Table could not measure merit, so it measured service, and service was measured in years. Promotion by seniority — vysluga, time served — became the ordinary path, with talent and patronage as accelerants. And a system that rewards years can be gamed by anyone who controls when the clock starts. By the middle of the century the great families had discovered the method. A boy was enrolled in one of the Guards regiments as an infant — a sergeant at five, a corporal in his cradle — so that when he actually reported for duty at sixteen he arrived with a decade of "service" on the books and an officer's commission waiting. Pushkin gave the practice its immortal line in The Captain's Daughter: Grinyov is enrolled as a sergeant in the Semyonovsky Guards while his mother is still pregnant with him. Catherine the Great's Guards lists carried thousands of these phantom officers; when her son Paul came to the throne in 1796 he struck them off in a fury, and the Guards, who murdered him five years later, did not forget it.

The clock was gamed from the other end too. In 1762 Peter III's Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility abolished compulsory service altogether; the nobility was free to stay home. Overwhelmingly, it kept serving anyway, because by then the rank was the point: a landowner with no chin was nobody at his own provincial assembly. The state, meanwhile, discovered that the door in rule eleven had let in more people than it wanted. In 1845 Nicholas I raised the bar for hereditary nobility from civil grade 8 to grade 5, and in 1856 it went up again to grade 4, with a colonel's rank required on the military side. The merit ladder had a growing crowd on it, and the response was to saw off the lower rungs.

The top rung owns people

There is a darker thing in the Table that the "rank by merit" story leaves out entirely. Hereditary nobility in Russia was not primarily an honor. It was a property right: the right to own populated estates, which is to say the right to own serfs. Every commoner the Table promoted to grade 8 became, on the day of his promotion, eligible to buy human beings — and by mid-century the law had confirmed that only nobles could. Peter had built a ladder open to talent whose top rung was ownership of other Russians, and the effect was to bind the interest of every ambitious man in the empire, from the ensign's son to the merchant's clerk, to the survival of serfdom. The Table did not merely coexist with bondage. It recruited for it.

A man's number is his name

What the Table produced in the end was not a meritocracy but chin — the Russian word for rank, and the root of chinovnik, the bureaucrat, literally "rank-holder." A Russian's grade determined how he was addressed: "Your High Excellency" for grades 1 and 2, "Your Excellency" for 3 and 4, "Your High Nobility" from 6 to 8, "Your Nobility" from 9 down. It determined his uniform, his wife's precedence at table, his place in the church procession, the number of horses he could harness. Foreign visitors were baffled that nobody in Russia asked what a man did; they asked what grade he held. And the literature, which is always the check on the ledger, is full of it. Gogol's Akaky Akakievich in The Overcoat is an "eternal titular councillor" — grade 9, the rung just below the one that would have ennobled him, where a commoner's climb stalled by design. Kovalyov in The Nose is a collegiate assessor, grade 8, who calls himself "Major" because it sounds better. Chekhov's "Fat and Thin" is a whole story about a man physically shrinking, along with his wife and his son, when he learns that his old schoolfriend has reached grade 3. The Table had done something no rank system in Europe quite managed: it had gotten inside people.

Grade 9 was the last rung before nobility. Gogol put his clerk there and left him — and every reader in Russia knew exactly what the number meant.

What it built

The serious argument about the Table is not whether it was meritocratic — it was, for a while, at the edges — but what kind of country it made. Marc Raeff's answer is the one that has lasted: the Table created a service ethos that built the modern Russian state, a nobility that understood itself through duty to the sovereign rather than through land or lineage, and that trained its sons in the sciences and languages the service demanded. And then, when the state stopped having enough to offer the educated men it had made, those men turned their trained minds on the state itself. The intelligentsia, on this reading, is the Table's unintended child. Richard Pipes reads the same facts more bleakly: a state that ranks everyone has bought everyone, and a society whose every ambition runs through the sovereign's ladder has no place to stand outside it. Both are right about the same thing. The Table did not create a middle class; it made the state the only employer worth having.

It outlived the tsars by less than two weeks. The Bolsheviks abolished ranks and estates by decree on 10 November 1917, and then, within a few years, built the nomenklatura — a list of positions, each with its access to housing, cars, food, and precedence, filled by seniority and patronage, gamed by anyone who knew how. Post-Soviet Russia restored formal class ranks for its civil servants within a few years of the Union's collapse. A country that has learned to give every man a number does not easily forget how.

Rank, merit, and the clock

So: worthy of admiration, yes, and the admiration should be precise. Peter looked at a country where a man's place was fixed by his great-grandfather's, and replaced it with one where a man's place was fixed by what he had done for the state — and he made the second rule stick for two hundred years, which is more than most reformers manage. The system genuinely opened, and genuinely promoted, and the men it promoted were among the most able Russia had. But a system can only reward what it can count, and the Table could count years. Within a generation the people it was meant to displace had learned to manufacture years in the cradle; the state that built it spent the next century raising the thresholds to keep the door from swinging too wide; the reward at the top was the ownership of serfs; and the number a man held became, for the whole of the empire's life, the thing he was. The Table of Ranks opened Russia by bureaucratizing it, and it did the second thing more thoroughly than the first. Any executive who has ever watched a clean incentive plan turn into a game of clock management will recognize every move.

Further reading: Marc Raeff, Origins of the Russian Intelligentsia: The Eighteenth-Century Nobility; Richard Pipes, Russia under the Old Regime, especially the chapter on the dvorianstvo; Brenda Meehan-Waters, Autocracy and Aristocracy: The Russian Service Elite of 1730; Walter M. Pintner & Don Karl Rowney (eds.), Russian Officialdom: The Bureaucratization of Russian Society from the Seventeenth to the Twentieth Century; Lindsey Hughes, Russia in the Age of Peter the Great; and, for the human ledger, Gogol's The Overcoat and The Nose, Chekhov's "Fat and Thin" and "The Death of a Government Clerk," and Pushkin's The Captain's Daughter.

A note on certainty: the date and text of the Table, its foreign models, the rules on low birth and on the sons of great families, the 1762 Manifesto, and the 1845 and 1856 threshold changes are documented. Meehan-Waters's finding about the 1730 elite is a single scholarly study, though a careful and widely cited one. The infant-enrollment practice is well attested for the Catherine era; how early it began is less clear. The Menshikov pie-seller story is contemporary gossip, not fact. The claim that the Table "recruited for serfdom" is this essay's framing of a documented legal link, and the parallel to the Soviet nomenklatura is an argument, not an established lineage.
